The Real Future of AI and Work

A weekend Long Reads episode built around Every's new Thesis Statements project — 100 builders and thinkers writing short essays on the future of work after automation. NLW reads through the first batch and argues the tired 'will AI take our jobs' debate is finally giving way to the questions that actually matter: how work, teams, companies, and human advantage get rebuilt.

The One Idea

"Will AI take all our jobs" is the boring question. The real future of work is finally getting specific.

AI doesn't just take jobs — it changes the entire landscape of how we work: what individuals and teams can aspire to, how companies organize, which skills matter. Every's Thesis Statements project pulls the people living that future into the discourse, and a pattern emerges across the essays: more expert human work after automation, companies rebuilt as human-agent systems rather than software factories, and human advantage shifting toward wisdom, weirdness, unpredictability, and the heart.

The Other Side

Zitron's objection is not to any particular essay in the Thesis Statements collection — it is to the genre. He opens this piece by arguing that the whole discussion of AI's relationship to jobs, benefits and productivity is theoretical, that Anthropic's Economic Index and OpenAI's Economic Research Exchange are "marketing operations" propagating a belief for which little evidence exists, and that Anthropic's own head of economics has conceded "no material increase in the unemployment rate to date". His charge is that this conversation is "masturbatory and a conscious attempt to avoid" the messier one: roughly $110bn of trailing twelve-month revenue across the entire industry against hundreds of billions in commitments. Where NLW hears a hundred practitioners finally getting specific about work after automation, Zitron hears a hundred people describing a future underwritten by money that does not yet exist.

Source: Ed Zitron, “Premium: AI Is Getting Way Too Expensive”, 31 July 2026 (from 31 July’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether the reorganisation of work now being described is being driven by capability or by capital. Both accept that agents do useful work today, that headline job losses have not yet appeared in the aggregate statistics, and that the labs are spending far ahead of revenue. The empirical question that will resolve is whether firms restructured around human-agent systems show measured productivity or margin gains that survive audit — the observable is sectoral productivity and headcount-per-unit-output data, plus whether AI-native firms keep those structures once compute is priced at cost rather than subsidised. The residue is worldview: Whittemore treats the practitioners living it as the best available evidence; Zitron treats first-person accounts from people selling the future as inadmissible. Whittemore is vindicated if the new organisational forms persist and spread beyond the essayists' own companies; Zitron if they turn out to have been a function of cheap tokens and end when the subsidy does. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is where they agree despite opposite priors: neither claims the labour-market data currently shows anything much. Watch what happens to those firms when inference is repriced.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

"More expert work, not less" is exactly what the scribe has done to a surgery day

Dan Shipper's claim is that automation takes the stable, well-framed layer and leaves more expert work behind, because someone must point the tool at the right thing, judge its output and turn it into a decision. That is a fair description of what an ambient scribe has done to a consultation. The typing has gone; the drafting is largely gone; what remains is the part that was always the job — deciding what the story actually is, what to safety-net, what to leave out of the record because it belongs to the patient rather than the notes. The important consequence is that the saved time is not neutral time. If it is quietly absorbed into more appointments, the practice has bought efficiency and nothing else. If it is spent on the patients where the diagnosis is genuinely uncertain, it has bought the thing Shipper is describing. That is a partnership decision, not a software decision, and no vendor will make it for you.

The "best clock" problem is the discharge summary problem with a new name

Tom Critchlow's point is that agents operate in seconds while teams meet weekly and finance plans quarterly, so the organisation with the best shared record of goals, decisions and constraints wins over the one with the best model. General practice has had this problem for forty years without agents: the hospital acts on Tuesday, the letter arrives on Friday, the patient rings on Wednesday and nobody in the surgery yet knows what was decided. Adding AI to either end does not fix a coordination failure; it makes the mismatch faster on one side only. Before any practice buys a second AI tool, the useful question is whether it writes into the record everyone already reads, or creates a fourth place where the truth about a patient lives. As Medical Examiner work makes plain, most of what goes wrong between services is not a clinical error but a handover in which two parties were working from different presents.

If Zitron's right / if NLW's right — the answer for a Harrow practice is the same on Monday

If NLW is right, the essays describe a real reorganisation and the sensible posture is to reshape the working week around what the tools genuinely take off you, expecting them to get cheaper and better. If Zitron is right, the whole future-of-work conversation is theatre financed by money that has not arrived, the productivity gains do not show up in any auditable figure, and the tools reprice sharply or disappear when the subsidy ends. Either way the instruction for a small NHS practice is identical: bank the change in working practice, not in the supplier. Keep the notes in a form the practice owns, keep at least one clinician competent to work without the tool, and be able to answer what happens on the Monday morning the vendor switches the lights off. Zitron's strongest and least contested line is worth holding onto in any procurement conversation — that on the labour-market data to date, nothing much has yet happened. It is a reasonable reason to buy on demonstrated workload relief in this building, and not on anybody's thesis about 2030.

NLW’s Takes

"Will AI take all our jobs" is a boring conversation

The AI industry has wanted to have the job-apocalypse debate for far too long. The productive conversations are about how AI changes the entire landscape of work — what individuals and teams can aspire to, how companies should organize, and which skills to prioritize.

AI throws the lights on a much bigger room

NLW's case for the more-work camp goes beyond continued demand for human expertise: we've been playing in one tiny lit quarter of what turns out to be a monumental room. Humanity will race to discover, build, and create more — a radical expansion of what we can all do is the deeper reason there will be more human work than ever.

Enterprises get what startups miss: technology needs institutions around it

NLW's read on Brier's essay: the enterprise world of AI users understands better than the startup world that technology without human and institutional systems around it is just another set of buzzwords. How and to what ends we build those systems is the real question.

Efficiency AI is fine. Opportunity AI is the point.

NLW's framework: doing what you already do faster and cheaper is a reasonable start, but assuming this capability-unlocking technology will be constrained to old workflows fundamentally misimagines what's possible. Opportunity AI takes more iteration and experimentation — which is why prematurely ROI-ifying enterprise AI efforts biases teams toward the same old things, slightly cheaper.

Skepticism for the watch-and-copy agent startups

NLW is skeptical in aggregate of startups whose only job is to watch what people do and train agents to do the same thing. Agents will almost inevitably work in agent-native ways suited to their strengths — which then requires another level of organizational integration to sync those new processes back with humans.

The next frontier: from single-player AI to multiplayer AI

NLW flags a theme he'll explore heavily this fall — the shift from individual agents to team agents, where coordination mechanisms and new systems designed around human-agent alignment become the substance of the conversation.

Finally, the future-of-work crowd is getting specific

One of NLW's long-standing beefs with AI-industry future projections has been a historic unwillingness to get into specifics. Agree or not with each essay, this series digs into exactly the question his earlier episode "The New Jobs AI Will Create" tried to address — and that shift from vague pronouncements to nuanced exploration is the real progress.
