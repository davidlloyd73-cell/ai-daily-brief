## The One Idea

### Graphs are the new layer: stop prompting agents, start designing agentic organizations.

Every stage of working with AI has minted its own 'engineering': prompts control the instructions, context controls what the model sees, the harness controls the environment, and loops control the iteration. Graph engineering is the next layer — it controls the agentic organization itself: which agents exist, what each owns, how work moves between them, and what happens on failure. A loop is how one agent does its job; a graph is how an entire agentic organization works. You don't have to build one tomorrow — but thinking in multi-agent systems terms is becoming a new work primitive.

## The Other Side

NLW's lead is that a new discipline is forming above prompts, context, harnesses and loops — graph engineering, the design of agentic organisations — and that thinking in multi-agent terms is becoming a general work primitive. Zitron's most recent piece treats that whole ladder of new disciplines as narrative rather than demand. His argument is that the money says otherwise: bank analysts now put 70% or more of Microsoft's, Google's and Amazon's AI revenue at OpenAI and Anthropic's own compute spend, so the buildout rests on two loss-making labs rather than on anyone actually getting paid work done. He quotes Apollo's Torsten Slok asking whether ROI will show up for end customers fast enough, and answers "probably not". He also argues the productivity claims are socially coerced — that saying AI is not transforming your job carries professional consequences — and that the real benefit is "much, much less than the boosters will promise".

Source: Ed Zitron, "Don't Look Up", 11 August 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/dont-look-up/

## The Truth Beneath

Both men orbit one variable: whether orchestrating agents produces work someone will pay for. They agree on more than the tone suggests — that prompt-level skills are near-exhausted, that most corporate AI training is theatre, and that the labs' spending is extraordinary. The empirical question is whether multi-agent systems deliver output an organisation would buy at cost; the observable that settles it is per-seat AI software revenue net of the labs' own compute bills — Microsoft 365 Copilot's disclosed revenue against its claimed thirty million seats is the cleanest test available. The residue is worldview: whether a new engineering discipline signals real capability or a bubble generating vocabulary. Whittemore is vindicated if graph-shaped systems produce durable, sold output; Zitron if the discipline matures while the revenue never arrives. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is that both think the leverage has moved past prompting. Watch whether any vendor discloses agent-product revenue separately from compute.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### A practice already runs on a graph — it is just made of people

Strip the jargon and graph engineering is the thing general practice has done for decades: reception triages, a nurse takes the bloods, a GP decides, a pharmacist reconciles the meds, a coder codes it, and every step has a rule about what happens when it fails. The genuinely useful idea in this edition is not the technology but the discipline of writing that down — who owns what, what information travels with the handover, and what happens when a node drops the work. If NLW is right and multi-agent systems arrive in primary care, the practices that cope will be the ones that already know their own handovers; if Zitron is right and none of it lands commercially, mapping the handovers is still the cheapest quality improvement available and costs nothing but an afternoon.

### Anthropic's 89% versus 13.6% is a finding about humans, not about AI

The number worth carrying out of this edition is that human reviewers caught 13.6% of harmful code changes because they were waving through 97% of everything put in front of them. That is not a software finding — it is the same rubber-stamp behaviour that turns a medicines reconciliation into a click, or an AI scribe note into a signature. Anthropic's conclusion is that the machine should decide when to interrupt, since a prompt that always fires stops being read. The equivalent question for a practice using a scribe or an inbox triage tool is whether our safety netting is a genuine check or a habit. If we are approving everything, the approval is not doing the work we think it is, and we should either reduce how often it asks or accept that we are not really reviewing.

### The bit with no NHS relevance, and why it still matters

Most of this edition — ByteDance's ten-trillion-parameter training run, Oracle's Malaysian GPUs, open-weight licensing tolls — has no bearing on a Harrow surgery this year, and it would be forcing it to pretend otherwise. The one indirect line is price. If Zitron is right that the compute buildout rests on two loss-making labs, the cheap or free AI tools practices are trialling now are being sold below cost, and repricing is a foreseeable risk when procuring anything on a multi-year basis. Worth a clause in the contract rather than a worry.

## NLW's Takes

### Prompt engineering is where the least leverage is left

Prompt engineering was the original 'blank engineering' of 2023-24 — and unfortunately it's still the substance of a lot of corporate upskilling courses today. The old layers don't go away as new ones arrive, but the prompt is now the layer with the least remaining leverage.

### Designing agentic systems is a new work primitive

The point isn't that everyone should run out and build complex agentic organizations. Just as understanding loop architecture helped even non-builders automate chunks of their work, graph engineering's value is unlocking multi-agent systems thinking — seeing different agents with different jobs and designing their relationships. Some of you will build these systems, and the discipline forming now will be waiting when you do.
