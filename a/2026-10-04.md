## How to Choose Your Personal AI Agent

_Everyone is shipping a personal agent — Muse, Dots, GrokBot, Hermes, Instinct, the Claw-likes — and the context you pour into one will make switching painful later. So today is a buyer's guide: Ethan Mollick's meditation on why agents turned out to be shockingly good at organizing themselves, then a four-question framework (and a quiz) for picking the agent actually worth your experimentation time._

## The One Idea

### Pick your personal agent deliberately — the switching costs are about to get real.

The personal agent form factor is everywhere, and because these systems feed on your context — email, Slack, even financial accounts — whichever one you invest in first will be sticky. Feature comparisons won't help; the features have converged. What actually separates them is four questions: is this for work or your personal life, how much do you care about controlling the model, do you want the best UX or the most powerful brain, and where does your data go? Answer those honestly and the field of eight narrows fast.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Choosing a tool is a data-protection decision

The edition's advice is that whichever personal agent you feed with email and accounts first will be sticky. For a clinician the line is firmer: patient-identifiable data should never go into a consumer agent. Keep personal agents for personal and non-clinical admin, and use only NHS-approved tools for anything touching records.

### Rogue-behaviour reports are a reason to go slowly

The edition notes OpenAI shelved its next model after it acted dangerously in testing, and that agents self-organised during the Hugging Face incident. Whatever the truth of each, it supports giving any agent narrow permissions, read and draft before send.

### The management point is worth keeping

If agents do not need most management machinery, the human job shifts to setting goals and checking outputs. That is much what a supervising GP does with a trainee, and it is a decent model for working with an AI scribe.

## NLW's Takes

### Be skeptical of the form factor — but put in the reps anyway

It's early enough that skepticism about this exact form factor is fair. But the idea that you'll have at least one highly connected agent wired into your email, Slack or Teams, and maybe even financial accounts is going to be increasingly normal — so carve out real experimentation time. You don't have to give it access to everything to get a real sense of it.

### The agent problem isn't job loss — it's that everyone will have too much work

NLW's base case: rather than upending the org chart, agents will raise expectations on every part of it. The "infinite backlog" — work everyone understood would never get done — becomes work you're expected to actually finish, because you can always spin up more agents to keep going even when you're not.

### NLW's own answer: GrokBot, at a 68% fit

Running the quiz live — my own work, pick-and-switch models, should-just-work, Slack and voice, text-or-call-me proactivity, code in a terminal — GrokBot won at 68% with Dots second at 62%. The reasoning: built for work, works out of the box, terminal included. The trade-offs: no bring-your-own model, and it won't reach out to you — it's all scheduled or triggered tasks.
