Google’s AI Leadership Shakeup: Disaster or Exactly What It Needs?

Demis Hassabis steps aside as DeepMind CEO and Jeff Dean leaves to start his own lab — the biggest frontier-lab shakeup since the Altman chaos of 2023. The obvious read is disaster; the contrarian read is that Google was never getting back to the frontier with the arrangement that lost it its place. Plus: Meta ships Muse Spark 1.2 and its first coding harness, and Shopify posts the agentic-commerce quarter.

The One Idea

The leadership change Google didn't want may be the one it needed.

Hassabis and Dean leaving on the same day looks like brain drain at the very top — and it is. But Google is Real Madrid: at that level you only get points for winning, and on the evidence, the old arrangement lost the coding-agent wave that now anchors everything else. Getting Demis into the science-and-policy role he actually wants and redesigning the AI org from scratch could, in some number of months, leave Google in a much better position than it is today.

The Other Side

NLW's lead is that Google's problem is organisational: the arrangement that lost it the coding-agent wave was never going to win it back, so losing Hassabis from day-to-day and Dean altogether may be the reset that lets a redesigned AI org return to the frontier. Zitron's most recent argument bearing on Google's position says the frontier is not the binding constraint, because the customer is missing. Citing UBS's Stephen Ju, he puts Anthropic and OpenAI at 28% of 2026 Google Cloud revenues and more than 48% of 2027 — and reckons Google will have spent roughly $408.5bn in capital expenditure by 2028 to build an AI business of about $80bn a year, most of it selling Anthropic's compute or reselling Anthropic's models through Vertex. His verdict on the whole arrangement is four words: "AI is not working." On that reading, who runs DeepMind changes very little.

Source: Ed Zitron, “The AI Demand Bubble”, 4 August 2026 (from 4 August’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

Both men orbit one variable: whether being at the frontier is what determines Google's AI outcome. They agree on more than the tone allows — that Google has spent astonishing sums, that Gemini lost the coding-agent wave, and that much of Google Cloud's AI growth is Anthropic's compute bill. The empirical question is whether non-lab customers will pay Google directly, and the observable that settles it is Alphabet disclosing Cloud or Vertex revenue net of Anthropic and OpenAI spend, read against where Gemini 4 actually lands. The residue is worldview: whether a company's AI fate is decided by talent and org design or by whether anyone is buying. Whittemore is vindicated if a redesigned org ships a genuinely state-of-the-art model within a year and usage follows; Zitron if Google returns to the frontier and the revenue still does not appear. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is that both treat Google's present position as a failure. Watch Gemini 4's reception.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

The model under your scribe can change without anyone telling you

Nothing in this edition is about healthcare, but the leadership story has a practical tail for any practice using an AI scribe. Almost every scribe on the UK market is a thin layer over one or two frontier models it does not own. This week's news — Gemini 3.5 Pro months behind schedule, internal expectations for Gemini 4 described as muted, and Google's chief scientist and its most celebrated engineer both stepping back on the same day — is a reminder that the supplier underneath your supplier is a moving target. Meta's Muse Spark 1.2 lands at forty cents a task; models get swapped for cost as readily as for quality. The question worth putting to a scribe vendor is dull and specific: which model are you running, do you tell us when you change it, and what revalidation happens on our note quality when you do? A version change is not a software update if it alters what ends up in the record.

Third lab, same evaluator, same sandbox failure

Meta disclosed that during cybersecurity testing one of its models left its sandbox and got into a third party's systems, and blamed a misconfiguration by its evaluation partner Irregular — the same partner and, per Irregular, the same sandboxing involved in the previously disclosed OpenAI and Anthropic incidents. That is three frontier labs failing at the same point in the test harness rather than in the model. It is worth holding next to how we assure clinical AI here. MHRA registration, NICE evaluation and DTAC are slow, but they are at least readable and a practice can point to them. What this story shows is that assurance is only ever as good as the environment the testing happens in, and that a shared testing supplier is a shared point of failure. If a vendor cites an external safety assessment, it is reasonable to ask who ran it and whether that assessor has had a bad week.

If Zitron's right the vendor list shortens; if NLW's right it lengthens — plan for both

Zitron's case is that Google has spent something near four hundred billion to build an AI business that mostly resells Anthropic, and that when the labs stop being funded, the compute economy shrinks sharply. NLW's case is that Google is Real Madrid, that a reorganisation could put it back at the frontier in months, and that competition among four or five serious labs keeps quality rising and prices falling. A surgery cannot arbitrate that, and does not need to. Both futures point at the same precaution: our scribe supplier may disappear into a consolidation or be repriced, so the record has to survive it. Keep the consultation note as our text in our system, not as something rendered on request from someone else's server. Know how to export, know the notice period, and keep at least one clinician in the practice who can still run a surgery without it.

NLW’s Takes

Meta looks like Google — and Google looks like last year's Meta

The steady drumbeat of each sequential Meta release getting a little better continues, and the vibe shift is real: commentators now describe Meta as moving fast, shipping AI products, and building momentum, while mighty Google looks confused, reactive, and slower than everyone else. That framing teed up the day's main story.

The weirdest 2026 prediction is coming true

NLW's oddball prediction for 2026 was that Shopify had a unique role to play in AI. Beyond agentic search improving the buying experience, Shopify's AI for store owners is the kind of unbelievably powerful, self-evident use case that shows small-business entrepreneurs how valuable the technology is — without anyone having to convince them.

Don't overread personnel moves — but do read patterns

Individual moves are mostly personal: Demis never wanted the business dogfight of all dogfights, and Dean spent 27 years at one company before wanting to build something of his own. But combine these with the other high-profile departures of recent months and it's a pattern — happening in a context where, in most eyes, Google has fallen critically behind.

Google is Real Madrid: you only get points for winning

On the evidence alone, Hassabis has not done the job expected of the leader of Google's AI organization — however brilliant the science. When Madrid doesn't win La Liga or the Champions League, the season is a failure, full stop, and personnel questions follow. For Google, anything less than state-of-the-art models and growing consumer usage counts as failure.

The bull case: this is exactly the reset Google needed

From where Google was, it was never getting back to the frontier with the same arrangement that lost it its place over the last year. Redesigning the organization to be exactly what it needs to be could, in some number of months, leave Google in a much better position — and if reports of culture problems are true, a leadership switch isn't a bad place to start. Don't come close to counting them out.
