## AI Won’t Make Our Minds Useless According to 400 Years of History

_On Long Read Sunday, NLW reads Venkatesh Rao's 'Cognitive Mercantilism.' The essay argues that today's AI panic repeats the zero-sum thinking capitalism overturned 400 years ago. It assumes intelligence is scarce, demand for it is fixed, and most minds are about to become useless. The early modern consumer revolution suggests the opposite may happen._

## The One Idea

### AI panic is cognitive mercantilism, and history suggests demand for intelligence has no ceiling.

Mercantilists treated wealth as finite, ordinary people as labor to be commanded, and a farm-free majority as useless mouths. Capitalism proved them wrong: agricultural productivity freed most people from farming, and new desires drove a virtuous cycle of voluntary work and expanding demand. Venkatesh Rao argues that today's AI fears make the same mistake about intelligence. If demand for intelligence is as elastic as demand for material goods turned out to be, then AI doesn't create billions of useless minds. It frees them to become explorers, and the politics shifts from preserving cognitive jobs to securing a fair share of cognitive abundance. NLW adds that a good destination still requires managing the transition, and that managing it is different from trying to stop it.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Latent demand is real in general practice

The idea that cheaper intelligence unlocks demand has a clear NHS parallel: faster, cheaper access has historically increased contacts rather than reduced them. If AI makes advice, summaries and follow-up cheap, expect more questions and more patient-held information arriving in the surgery, not fewer. Planning on the basis that AI simply saves time is optimistic.

### Who shares the surplus?

Rao's point that the politics shifts to fair access to cognitive abundance matters for the NHS. Scribes and triage tools may free clinician minutes, but whether that time goes back to patients, to extra list demand, or to cost-cutting is a commissioning decision, not a technical one. Worth watching how ICB contracts treat any time saved.

### Little direct clinical relevance

This edition is a philosophical essay, and there is no direct guidance for the consulting room. The sceptical counterweight is that demand growth and abundance claims are untested, and transition costs, which NLW concedes, fall on real staff.

## NLW's Takes

### The deepest AI fear isn't hacking or jobs, it's relevance

Beyond security and job loss, people worry about what it means for them when an intelligence we long thought unique to humans becomes abundant. NLW's framing: we've faced this kind of question before, and 400 years of economic history suggests something quite wonderful might happen instead.

### The lump-of-labor fallacy hides a second error: assuming demand is fixed

NLW revisits his May episode 'The New Jobs AI Will Create.' Job-loss thinking assumes we already demand as much as we ever could. He calls that 'completely ahistorical.' Human demand is nearly limitless, constrained mostly by physical limits like hours in a day and calories we can eat.

### Six ways AI could unlock latent demand

Price (I wanted it but it cost too much), access (I couldn't get it), complexity (the system was too confusing), continuity (I'd benefit from help all the time), personalization (I value something made for me), and relational (I want it to be more human and trusted). Each one is pent-up demand that AI could release.

### Agents broke the idea of being 'done'

NLW's own explanation for why AI makes us work more: at work there has always been an infinite backlog, and 'done' was a negotiation shaped by natural limits on our capacity. When you can run tireless parallel copies of yourself, the whole backlog becomes unlockable, and the only constraint is imagining what belongs on it.

### What if AI creates demand, not just supply?

Rao's most radical extension, which NLW says deserves its own essay. The point isn't that AIs become conscious consumers. It's that the systems AIs build, and the systems built around them, create new pathways and needs for consuming intelligence.

### The essay proves its own argument

NLW notes that pieces like this pull readers past answering the work memo and into 'fuzzy, foggy places' of thought. Rao's open question is whether people avoid that exploration for lack of capacity, or for lack of the surplus that gives them time and reason. His answer is the latter, and in that world people are explorers, not replaced.

### Optimism about the destination doesn't erase the cost of getting there

NLW stresses this isn't an argument for or against any particular AI safety concern. You can be excited about cognitive abundance and still worry about the period when AI takes over work our brains used to do. 'Transitions between orders… are never without cost,' but dealing with a transition's challenges is very different from trying to stop it.
