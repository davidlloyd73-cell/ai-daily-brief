## How AI Is Changing What Work Means

_A financial research firm spun up 300 Claude sub-agents, designed 318,000 candidate proteins against scorpion venom, and shipped the results — all while remaining a financial research firm. Plus Meta's Muse hits 10 million downloads, and Anthropic's leaked prospectus gives critics an $80 billion number that isn't really a number._

## The One Idea

### AI doesn't just change how you work — it changes what you work on.

Citrini Research is a thematic equity shop. They thought life sciences were undervalued, couldn't get a straight answer from experts about whether AI drug discovery was real, and so — for under a couple hundred thousand dollars — they designed anti-scorpion-venom proteins themselves with Claude and had a lab validate them. They are not becoming a biotech company. But the fact that this was economically viable at all is the story: a new category of orthogonal work, where firms do things they could never previously justify in order to do their actual job better. That's the opportunity side of the efficiency-versus-opportunity divide.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Neglected conditions and AI-assisted design

Citrini's scorpion venom experiment is a small illustration of an argument clinicians will recognise: diseases that were never worth the cost of developing treatments may become tractable. The caveat from the edition is important. Binding a toxin is not neutralising it, and the four to seven years to release remains. Nothing here changes practice.

### Verification is the bottleneck

Hypothesis generation has sped up enormously, while testing has not. That mirrors clinical AI: a scribe drafts in seconds, but the safety step is still a clinician checking the note. Budget time for that step.

### Retention and personal agents

The point that personal agents sit in a retention no-man's-land is relevant to NHS digital tools. Products people must use, like a booking system, retain through dependency; those they merely might like do not. Meta's Muse also exposed shared links that researchers could read, a reminder to ask any consumer AI product how it handles sharing before staff use it with patient information.

## NLW's Takes

### Car agents might be indispensable — or a setup hassle nobody bothers with

NLW can see this becoming so normal we forget cars ever lacked it. He can equally see people deciding the integration isn't worth it when the phone with the same capability is already sitting right there on the console.

### These leaks only confirm what everyone already believed

The $80 billion figure gives AI critics fresh podcast material, but nobody is actually surprised that Anthropic issued a ton of options and will spend north of half a trillion on compute. Like the SpaceX IPO, this offering won't be priced on the last few quarters — it'll be priced on what people believe about the next decade.

### Labs will compete with their customers — but not all at once

Whether frontier labs start internalizing scientific results instead of selling tools is a live question, fueled by the unverified reports that Anthropic leadership expects to be the only private company left. Citrini's experiment shows how many steps, people, and institutions sit between an AI-designed molecule and a real product. Competition is inevitable; the idea that it arrives across whole industries overnight is not.

### Curing cancer will not fix AI's public opinion problem

The popular theory is that disease cures are the only way to turn sentiment around. But AI-accelerated discovery is more likely to simply become the norm than to arrive as one miracle compound — and as Will Manidis noted, if the industry thinks curing cancer is enough, it should look at polling on big pharma. NLW's conclusion: there's no magic bullet, so labs should work on what they believe has the biggest impact regardless of the headlines it generates.

### The new category: orthogonal work

Citrini didn't pivot to biotech — they ran a wet-lab experiment because it was the cheapest way to answer an investment question, and they remain a financial research firm. Expect far more of this: firms doing things they previously couldn't justify at all, purely because it makes their core work better. That's the opportunity side of the efficiency-versus-opportunity divide.
