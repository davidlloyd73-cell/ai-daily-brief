## Why Everyone Suddenly Hates AI Data Centers

_In twelve months, data center opposition went from niche land-use gripe to bipartisan electoral force — and every explanation on offer (water memes, China psyops, AI job-loss doomerism, big tech baggage) is partly right. NLW goes end to end on the backlash and lands on the real driver: agency and control. Which is exactly why he thinks this might be the most winnable, value-creating political battle of all time._

## The One Idea

### The data center backlash is about agency — which makes it the most winnable fight in politics.

Americans have swung from mild support to overwhelming opposition to data centers in a year, and politicians are sprinting to catch the anger. But talk to people in these communities and the issue isn't really water or noise or even AI — it's control over their own future, negotiated away behind NDAs. That's fixable. Voters prefer clear rules to moratoriums by more than two to one, and the builders have both the money and the motive to make the equation come up positive. The smart politicians will write the checklist and take the credit.

## The Other Side

Zitron's data centre argument is not about whether communities should be angry — it is about whether the thing they are being asked to host will still be running to pay them. He counts roughly 190 gigawatts of capacity in planning, implying about $1.68 trillion of annual compute revenue to justify it, against genuine demand he puts nearer $120 billion a year, 70 to 90 per cent of it from two loss-making labs. Each site is financed inside its own special purpose vehicle that services debt only out of customer revenue, so on his reading "the vast majority of AI data centers and their associated SPVs will collapse" if that demand never arrives. He restated the demand half of it as recently as 11 August. Where NLW sees Loudoun County's tax base and Quincy's swimming pool as the template, Zitron would say those were built on demand that already existed — and that a town signing a twenty-year bargain with an SPV in 2026 is counterparty to a balance sheet, not to Microsoft.

Source: Ed Zitron, "The Subprime Data Center Crisis", 22 July 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-subprime-data-center-crisis/

## The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is durability of the local bargain: not whether a data centre is worth hosting today, but whether the entity promising the taxes, the grid payments and the park is still solvent to deliver them in ten years. Both accept the sites are being built at extraordinary speed, that the money behind them is largely debt, and that host communities currently negotiate in the dark. The empirical question that will resolve is whether newly energised capacity gets contracted — the observable is occupancy and lease renewal at the 2026-27 cohort of sites, and whether any sizeable data centre SPV defaults or is restructured. The residue is worldview: Whittemore reads demand as real and lagging supply, so the fight is distributive and therefore winnable; Zitron reads demand as manufactured by two funded losers, so the fight is about who is left holding an empty shed. Whittemore is vindicated if communities that sign good checklists collect for decades as Loudoun has; Zitron if the first wave of defaults strands towns with derelict plant and stranded grid costs. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is where they agree despite opposite priors: the NDA-shrouded deal structure is indefensible, and communities are bearing risk they cannot see. Watch the occupancy of the first big 2026 sites to switch on.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### The water-stat correction is the whole lesson, and we have lived it twice

The striking finding in this episode is not the 75 per cent opposition — it is that correcting a wrong number changed almost nobody's mind. A famous water-usage figure was out by a factor of a thousand, the correction was published, and only 8 per cent of people found it very convincing. General practice has seen this film. It is care.data in 2014, it is the 2021 GP data extraction that was paused after a million opt-outs, and it is every vaccine conversation where the evidence was never the obstacle. The mechanism is identical: when people believe a decision was taken about them without them, they do not audit the facts, they discount the source. The practical read-across is that anything we introduce which touches patient data — the scribe included — is won or lost on whether people feel they were told and could say no, not on how good the evidence sheet is. Publish first, correct later never recovers.

### Agency, not accuracy, is the design question for AI in the consulting room

NLW's landing point is that the backlash is about control negotiated away behind non-disclosure agreements, and that voters prefer clear rules to bans by 57 to 23. Translate that to a surgery. Patients are not, on the whole, asking whether the ambient scribe transcribes accurately; they are asking who else hears it and whether declining costs them anything. The equivalent of the 'clear rules' answer is a one-paragraph, posted policy: what is recorded, where it goes, how long it is kept, and the fact that saying no changes nothing about the consultation. That is cheap, and it is the only version of this that survives a bad news story. The equivalent of the NDA is the situation we drift into by default — a supplier arrangement nobody outside the practice manager's inbox has ever seen. Worth noting the same logic applies upwards: the ICB and the national programmes are making AI procurement decisions on our patients' behalf with roughly the transparency of a data centre deal.

### If Zitron's right / if NLW's right — the same instruction either way

If NLW is right, compute is genuinely scarce and demand real, prices for clinical AI tools drift down as capacity comes online, and the sensible move is to adopt earlier and negotiate harder. If Zitron is right, a large share of that capacity is financed against demand that does not exist, and the correction arrives as vendors failing, being acquired, or repricing sharply — the scribe market in particular is thin, venture-funded and unlikely to survive intact. The instruction is the same under both: do not let a clinical workflow depend on one supplier's continued existence. In practice that means the exit clause and the export format matter more than the demo, notes must land in the record in a form we own rather than living in the vendor's cloud, and someone should be able to answer, without preparation, what we do on the Monday morning a supplier goes dark. That is not pessimism; it is the same continuity planning we would apply to any single-source clinical system.

## NLW's Takes

### The China psyop theory: partly true, entirely too convenient

From Kevin O'Leary blaming China in court to Founders Fund investors pointing at TikTok, the psyop explanation is everywhere. NLW's take: sowing social media discord is now standard strategy for America's adversaries, so bot farms are almost certainly contributing — but treating the backlash as all psyop is an excuse that will stop us from dealing with the real issues underneath.

### 'Productivity' is a selling point nobody asked for

Ben Thompson notes Silicon Valley relearns every decade that consumers won't pay for software and don't care about productivity. NLW pushes it further: when the industry says AI is coming for your job and CEOs use it as layoff cover, 'productivity' just translates to 'you're going to make me give away more of the labor I don't own.'

### Ford rehiring 'graybeards' does more than any CEO apology

The job-loss messaging has softened lately — too little too late, in NLW's view. What matters more is that the evidence shows the mass-job-loss argument was wrong in the first place: Ford deciding to rehire its graybeard engineers does more than CEOs saying 'oops, I was wrong about job loss' ever could.

### This is the most winnable political compromise NLW has ever seen

The data centers are mission-critical to their builders, and the fix mostly comes down to spending more money in the host community. The template is already emerging: David Crowley's checklist (community veto, banned NDAs, 100% covered grid costs, union labor) and Vivek Ramaswamy's pledge of free home electricity and lower property taxes near new facilities. Expect it to get worse before it gets better — but the politicians who offer a path forward get to be the heroes.
