How to Talk About AI at Thanksgiving

No news day, so a different kind of episode: NLW works through 15 questions most likely to come up about AI around the Thanksgiving table. They cover jobs, cheating, kids and chatbots, water, the bubble, doom and China. Each comes with his rough take, and he argues the goal isn't to win the argument but to invite the skeptics in.

The One Idea

Don't try to win the argument — invite the skeptics in.

Anthropic's IPO, the midterms and the White House's role in the Jensen Huang saga all point to the same phase transition. The future of AI will no longer be decided solely by the labs. A much broader cross-section of society is going to have a say. We don't yet have norms for where democratic process meets private enterprise, and NLW argues they'll get built at dinner tables rather than in Washington or Silicon Valley. So the right disposition this Thanksgiving is to bring people into the conversation, especially the ones who don't like AI one bit. Argue facts where facts help, like water use. Take local and entry-level concerns seriously. And mostly just show them what the tools can do.

The Other Side

Zitron, writing the day before, does not address the dinner-table idea and is in no mood to invite anyone in. Where Whittemore reads the White House's part in the Huang affair as the public gaining a say, Zitron reads it as cronyism: the President rescuing a brittle industry to steady the stock market. He says the episode has made people take sides, and that it "only prolongs the inevitable" because the money is running out. For him, whether AI is a bubble is no longer an open question to be discussed politely.

Source: Ed Zitron, “Defective Accelerationism”, 24 November 2026 (from 24 November’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is who now disciplines the AI industry: the public and its politics, or the money. Both accept as fact that the President intervened at Nvidia, that the market rallied, and that the intervention headed off a repricing of the industry; Whittemore says that is partly why people are upset. The empirical question is whether wider scrutiny produces any constraint. The observables are the congressional hearings and whether AI shares hold their post-rescue gains. The residue is worldview: whether a sceptic is someone to persuade or someone already proved right. For Whittemore to be vindicated, public involvement must produce workable rules and demand must keep pace with the build-out. For Zitron, funding must tighten whatever the conversation. Both are polemicists with incentives, Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype. The signal is their agreement that the rescue postponed a reckoning. Watch whether the hearings lead to anything binding.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Patients over 65 are already using it

Whittemore describes older people coming to these tools when a grandchild scans a confusing insurance form into a chatbot and it suddenly makes sense. Expect the same with hospital letters, benefit forms and test results. Some patients will arrive having already asked. It is more useful to ask what the tool told them, and correct it where needed, than to treat the question as unwelcome.

Fewer errors is not no errors

He says the tools make things up far less than two years ago, and still advises a routine for checking. That is the right rule for an AI scribe: read the note before signing it, every time. Zitron does not dispute what the tools can do; his argument is about money. If the suppliers' finances are as fragile as he claims, prices and products may change at short notice, so a practice should avoid depending on any single one.

The human premium describes general practice

Whittemore expects value to hold where trust, physical presence and knowing who did the work all matter. That is a fair description of a consultation. His worry about entry-level work applies to us as well: if routine tasks go to software first, we need another way for trainees to learn them. Otherwise this is a general-interest edition with little direct clinical content.

NLW’s Takes

AI's future won't be decided solely by the labs anymore

Anthropic going public, the midterm results and the White House's involvement in the Jensen Huang decision add up to a phase transition. A much broader cross-section of society now gets a say in how AI happens. We lack agreed norms for where democratic process meets private enterprise, and we're going to have to build them.

The norms get built at dinner tables, not in DC or Silicon Valley

NLW isn't advocating convincing your family to think like you. The right disposition, he argues, is inviting people into this industry, even and perhaps especially if they don't like it one bit.

Job-apocalypse predictions come from people who've never worked outside tech startups

The labs have spent years predicting that AI would take all the white-collar jobs within months. NLW fundamentally doesn't believe it. History is the smallest part of his case: no technological revolution has left humans with fewer jobs. He concedes 'AI is different' is a fair counter to the historical argument alone.

Changed jobs still come with real loss

These shifts may not mean fewer jobs, but some people chose video production or coding because they loved the craft. For those who didn't want to be managers or editors, the transition will involve some amount of genuine loss.

The real worries: entry-level hiring and the most-exposed jobs

The job market for recent grads is significantly harder, though not clearly because of AI. If AI hits the entry level hardest, that breaks the mentorship path for future skills. Some jobs will inevitably be obviated. Watching those exposed workers is a better target for intervention than the generic job apocalypse.

Are kids using AI to cheat? Yep, of course they are

Young people are maximally efficiency-seeking, and surveys bear it out. But NLW sees an education system that has been moribund for years: skills disconnected from the workforce, broken higher-ed economics, held together by heroic teachers. He hopes AI is the shock that forces a ground-up redesign.

Beyond blue books: AI as the greatest personalized tutor ever

In the short term come stopgaps like handwritten essays in blue books. The longer-term promise is thoughtful pedagogy designed into AI-enabled systems, not the overly simple versions being tried today. The technology is neutral, and outcomes depend on the choices we make.

Don't repeat the social media mistake with chatbots

Parents in the 2000s and 2010s didn't know that letting kids scroll Instagram could be the worst thing for them. Now we do, hence bans and campaigns like 'wait for eighth.' NLW isn't claiming chatbots are dangerous for kids. But the possibility needs considering, and parents have to be the front line.

The water panic is the one to slap down with facts

Viral memes rely on scary numbers without context. US Gulf irrigation uses about 30× the water data centers consume on site. A full year of asking AI 10 questions a day, even at high-end estimates, adds up to about a handful of almonds' worth of water. Data centers are also typically cleaner than the industrial sites that came before them.

Local data-center concerns deserve to be taken seriously

Debunking the generic environmental claim doesn't cover specific, localized concerns about existing or proposed data centers. Those conversations shouldn't be off-limits. They matter more as more people get a stake in AI's future.

More people calling it a bubble makes a catastrophic pop less likely

NLW thinks this is part of why people are upset about Trump's intervention at Nvidia. Jensen's departure was a perfect excuse to reprice the industry before things got out of hand. At the table, aim for shared terms rather than a verdict.

'Who's in charge?' is the easiest place to find common ground

Almost no one from any party is thrilled about how much control CEOs like Sam Altman or Dario Amodei will have, and few trust government to do better. NLW sees a broad demand for democratic involvement and encourages people to be loud with peers and elected representatives.

Hallucinations aren't what they were — but always verify

People still treat hallucinations like the problem of a couple of years ago, which is no longer the case, though skepticism and a process for checking remain wise. On whether AI makes us dumber, the answer mirrors education: it can, or it can make us much smarter. It depends on not letting it do all our thinking.

Parents of philosophy majors might feel a little better now

NLW won't prescribe a path, since obvious-seeming bets may not play out. It's not a dismissal of STEM, but he sees rising relative value in two areas. One is a general arts education that teaches you to construct an argument and a narrative. The other is social skills: working on teams and managing people.

Doom scenarios never explain where AI's intent to harm comes from

Paperclip-style stories offer vanishingly little explanation for murderous intent, which leans on unsupported claims about consciousness. They also assume humans offer no resistance, or that AI hides everything until it's too late. 'Call me crazy, but I think humans like being alive.'

X-risk talk has been a net hindrance to cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is intensely brittle, and systems like air traffic control are wildly outdated. NLW argues the safety crowd's focus on dire doom has pulled limited attention away from here-and-now risks. Society, media and politicians all have to prioritize.

The best Thanksgiving move: just show them what you've built

People's unlock is feeling the power run into them, doing something they never imagined possible. Even something simple, like building a great website for a side business. Those empowering moments make every hard conversation easier.
