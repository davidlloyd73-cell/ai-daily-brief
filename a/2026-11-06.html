Companies Are Spending 5x More on AI Now

Markets just had their worst day since 2020 and the Anthropic IPO is wobbling, but Menlo's latest State of Enterprise AI report tells a different story: business AI spend is accelerating from a bigger base, and agents are the reason. Budgets built for seats are colliding with a per-token reality.

Source: https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-06

▶ Listen — today’s brief (3 min)

The One Idea

Agents turned enterprise AI from a seat purchase into a token bill, and spending growth is accelerating.

The mainstream story about business AI is companies capping employee spend. Menlo's survey of roughly 400 enterprise decision-makers says the opposite: estimated enterprise AI spend jumped to almost $177 billion in 2026, and the growth rate sped up instead of slowing as the market matured. Breadth barely moved, but spend per enterprise rose 4.7x, about 2.5 times what companies budgeted. Companies planned for a per-seat world and got a per-token one. Frontier models, Anthropic and the lab coding agents captured most of the gains. Most of the money is net new budget rather than cannibalized software spend.

The Other Side

Zitron, writing three days before this edition and before the Menlo report, argues the surge in business AI spending was an anomaly of what he calls token-maxxing, when companies let staff spend freely and then pulled back once costs soared; he cites Uber burning its annual token budget in a quarter. He says about 80 per cent of the two leading labs' enterprise revenue comes from venture-backed start-ups, so growth depends on venture capital continuing to flow. He notes Anthropic's third-quarter compute costs rose faster than its revenue, and that Ramp expects fourth-quarter growth of only 15 to 25 per cent.

Source: Ed Zitron, “Running Dry”, 3 November 2026 (from 3 November’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether per-token spending is durable demand or a one-off overshoot. Both accept as fact that spending ran far ahead of budgets, that billing moved from seats to tokens, and that companies are now capping usage. The empirical question is whether spending keeps growing once the caps bite; the observable is Anthropic's fourth-quarter revenue growth against Ramp's estimate of 15 to 25 per cent, and then next year's Menlo figure. The residue is worldview: whether a blown budget shows value being discovered or waste going unchecked. For Whittemore to be vindicated, budgets must reset upwards and the long tail of lighter users must follow the heavy spenders. For Zitron, growth must slow sharply as caps tighten and venture-funded customers run short of money. Both are polemicists with incentives: Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype. The signal is that both say spending is concentrated in a few heavy users who overshot their plans. Watch Anthropic's fourth-quarter numbers.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Per seat or per token: read the scribe contract

Companies budgeted per seat and were billed per token, spending about two and a half times their plan. Most GP scribes are still sold per clinician, which is predictable. If a supplier moves to usage pricing, or adds agent features that run unattended, ask for a cap and monthly reporting before agreeing.

Heavy users are not the whole market

A third of large companies still report no AI use, and spending is concentrated in a few heavy users. NLW reads that as a sign of where everyone is heading; Zitron reads it as an overshoot now being reined in. For the NHS the sensible position is to pilot, measure time saved and safety, and not assume either story.

Read-only access is a useful pattern

Claude's new banking link is read-only and stops short of investment advice, deferring to regulated professionals. That is a reasonable model for clinical tools: let the system read and summarise the record, and keep the decision and the entry with a registered clinician.

NLW’s Takes

One evaluator focused on RSI is an incomplete audit

Bringing in METR was meant partly to address public concern about cybersecurity breaches at the labs. The report doesn't touch sandboxing or other safety measures, so it is largely non-responsive to those concerns. METR can reasonably focus on recursive self-improvement, but if it's the only outside evaluator, the independent auditor suite is incomplete.

The explanation is agents: spend stopped being about seats

From Opus 4.5 last November onward, AI spend shifted from seats to tokens and everything that intelligence could do. Agents work independently, run in parallel and keep going after people step away. Spending now scales with work, not headcount.

We planned for per-seat AI and got per-token AI

This isn't necessarily runaway spend. It's people learning what all this raw intelligence can actually do. The whiplash between token-maxing and token restrictions is a symptom of the paradigm shift. AI budgeting looks somewhat like cloud compute, but even that analogy breaks down. It's a new category that needs to be planned on its own terms.

Capability beats cost for now, because we're early

Since Opus 4.5, businesses have been racing to catch up with genuinely differentiated agentic capabilities, and Opus 5.5 feels like another such jump. As adoption and the stack mature, cheaper and more efficient models will come in to form a more complex stack. The competition for those models still matters.

Open source: 4% of spend, 19% of tokens

Open source's spend share fell from 6% to 4%, but its token share jumped from 11% to 19%. Near-frontier models like Grok 4.7, Kimi K3, DeepSeek V4 and GLM 5.3 together still take under 5% of API spend. In the medium to long term, token share is the more significant number, and that trend hasn't peaked.

AI isn't eating SaaS budgets — yet

Of the AI spending Menlo could trace, 55% came from net new budgets and just 21% from existing software budgets. SaaS growth slipped only from 13% to 11%. The next threat to SaaS, agents using software headlessly, has barely started.

Our narratives are running ahead of how businesses actually buy AI

Menlo's data broadly backs what the show has been tracking. It is also a reminder of a gap: the trends discussed in heavily enfranchised AI circles aren't yet the lived reality of the average company buying and using AI.
