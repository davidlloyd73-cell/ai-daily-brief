## Big Tech Unites for Open Source AI—and Against Anthropic

_Two open-source stories collide: NVIDIA quietly bets on Ilya Sutskever's secretive Safe Superintelligence, while $8 trillion of tech market cap signs an open letter defending open-weight AI — leaving Anthropic standing alone and getting either villain or hero framing depending on where you sit._

## The One Idea

### Eight trillion dollars of Big Tech just made open-weight AI a coalition — and Anthropic the lone holdout.

As Washington edges toward banning Chinese open models, NVIDIA, Google, Microsoft, Meta, Elon Musk, Palantir and eventually OpenAI co-signed an open letter defending open weights. Read cynically it's everyone talking their book against the two labs running away with the market; read charitably it's a fight against regulatory capture. Either way, Anthropic's refusal to sign turned it into a Rorschach test — villain to some, principled and 'aura-farming' to others — and the messy employee snark on both sides shows just how heated the open-versus-closed war has become.

## The Other Side

Zitron published the same day and does not engage the open-weight argument at all — no mention of the letter, of Kimi, or of Anthropic's refusal to sign. What he does contest is the premise underneath it: that these companies are large, healthy actors whose commercial interests happen to point at a public good. He takes this edition's lead headline, NVIDIA's investment in Safe Superintelligence, and files it under manufactured demand, describing SSI as "a company with no products or plans other than to rent NVIDIA GPUs", and reads the reported $250bn backstop for a SoftBank-affiliate data centre as "the final boss of circular financing". His argument is that hyperscalers cannot stop spending without being graded on what they bought, so the coordination we are watching — investments, guarantees, joint letters — is what an industry does when genuine paying demand is thinner than the capex requires.

Source: Ed Zitron, "The More You Buy, The More You Lose", 28 July 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-more-you-buy-the-more-you-lose/

## The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether coordinated corporate behaviour in AI reflects real demand or manufactured demand. Both accept the same facts — NVIDIA's $750bn of new deals, the SSI investment, and that public positions track commercial positions; NLW's own guest calls the letter eight trillion dollars of market cap acting because it is good for their business. The empirical question that will resolve is whether the backstopped capacity is built and paid for by customers rather than by guarantees, and the observable is whether SB Energy raises the debt and energises meaningful load. The residue is worldview: NLW sees self-interest converging with a public good, Zitron sees an industry that cannot find buyers. NLW is vindicated if open-weight competition and the build-out proceed on their own money; Zitron if vendor guarantees keep paying. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW's show needs the fight to matter, Zitron's subscribers need collapse to be certain — and the signal is that both, from opposite priors, treat commercial self-interest as the honest explanation of the letter. Watch whether that site breaks ground.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Open weights are the only version of this that can ever run inside the NHS

Strip away the Washington politics and there is a practical point for general practice. An open-weight model is one anyone can download and run on their own infrastructure — which is the only arrangement under which patient-identifiable audio or text never leaves an NHS-controlled environment. Every ambient scribe now in use in English general practice sends the consultation to a supplier's cloud, and the DPIA turns on trusting that supplier's processing terms. If the open ecosystem thrives, on-premise or UK-hosted models become a realistic procurement option for ICBs and trusts; if it is squeezed, the choice narrows permanently to a handful of American APIs. That is worth knowing when your practice or PCN next signs a scribe contract: ask where the model runs, not just where the data is stored.

### A ban drafted in Washington is a supplier risk in Harrow

The concrete proposal in this edition is a US ban on Chinese open models. Several cheaper transcription and summarisation tools sold into UK primary care are built on open weights of uncertain provenance, and suppliers rarely volunteer which base model they fine-tuned. If restrictions land, some of those products change model or price at short notice — the same quiet substitution problem as any model swap, but forced by policy rather than engineering. The reasonable step is dull and cheap: ask each AI supplier, in writing, which base model the product uses, and whether they would notify you if it changed. Keep the answer with the DPIA.

### The safety argument is not obviously wrong, and neither is the cynical one

Anthropic's refusal to sign rests on Dario Amodei's worry about frontier-grade cyber capability being downloadable by anyone. If he is right, a health service running on internet-facing clinical systems is exactly the sort of target that finds out first — the 2017 WannaCry week is still the reference point for most of us. If Zitron and the cynics are right, the whole exchange is commercial positioning between firms with capex to justify, and safety language is the wrapper. General practice does not need to pick. Either way the operational lesson is the same one the NHS keeps relearning: patch, segment, and rehearse the paper fallback, because your exposure comes from the estate you actually run rather than from whose model won the argument.
