How to Deslopify Your AI

An Operator's Cut with Nufar on the most practical problem of the agentic era: slop. Agents now produce documents, decks and code in volume, and much of it lands unread on someone else's desk. The episode covers why the machines produce slop, why we let them, how to spot it, and the habits that keep the work yours.

The One Idea

Slop is skipped thinking with the bill sent to the reader. The new edge is using AI well, not using it more.

The first phase of AI at work was learning to produce volumes of content. That skill is now democratized, and the result is slop everywhere: work where the person who should have done the thinking skipped it, so the cost moved to whoever received it. Models default to the average and people default to trusting them, so slop is the path of least resistance. The fix isn't abandoning AI. It's knowing what you think before you open the tool, deciding in advance where you step in, scaling effort to the stakes, and respecting other people's time as much as your own. In an endless sea of content, the good stuff stands out even more.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Slop in clinical notes

The edition defines slop as skipped thinking whose cost lands on the reader. In general practice the reader is the next clinician, so a long, generic, unread scribe note is a patient-safety issue. Reading and editing every AI-drafted note before filing, and keeping notes short and specific, applies directly.

Automation complacency

The cited finding that more trust in AI goes with less critical checking is a known risk for scribe users. A practical safeguard is deciding in advance what you always verify: medication, doses, allergies, safety-netting and any examination findings the tool could not have heard.

Disclosure and detection

Labelling AI-assisted work is sensible for letters and reports as well as notes, including medical examiner and referral documents. Detectors are unreliable, so the control is a named human who can defend every sentence, not software. No Zitron counterpoint was published on this topic.

NLW’s Takes

Phase one was volume. Phase two is using it well.

Calling it 'slop' was once easy to dismiss as anti-AI grumbling. Now AI writing, images, video and code are everywhere, and that content has a real cost. If the first phase of AI at work was learning to produce more, the next is distinguishing yourself by using these tools well.

A concise AI email isn't slop if it does its job and gets out of the way

The burden varies wildly by artifact. NLW argues nobody minds AI emails that are concise and to the point, because their purpose isn't to provoke thought. Nufar agrees effort should scale with stakes: no one wants artisanal meeting notes, but brand, customer-facing and high-impact original work demands real intentionality.

Let AI do the research. Never let it form your point of view.

For his new long-form show How We Got Here, NLW has AI read everything on a topic and generate reports of every length across several models. He uses those to build or challenge his own view. His proof that AI can't own your perspective: ask it to steelman two opposing positions and it argues each with apparent deepest conviction. It has no perspective, only the sum of the available arguments.

NLW almost never agrees with how AI wants to build an argument

The structure and flow of the argument stay in NLW's hands. AI comes back in only to fill his outline with key facts and numbers, which he then talks over extemporaneously without a script. The workflow is a constant back-and-forth: perspective and architecture stay human, and AI makes it viable at production speed.

Without AI the 6,000-word essay wouldn't exist. Making it readable still takes 5–10 hours.

An AI-drafted 6,000-word companion essay will be slop by default. NLW feeds the model his raw talk-over transcript rather than just the outline, then spends 5–10 hours rewriting in a paragraph-level CMS he built for the site, keeping quirks an English teacher would mark wrong because they sound like him. The lesson: there's no perfect efficiency, so budget de-slopifying as part of the cost of doing business.

Don't hide the AI, label it, as Figma's writing rules require

Figma's rules for AI writing say AI-written work is fine but should be labeled at the top so readers have context. That's the right frame for a 40-page report meant to be mined, not presented as finished. NLW says the cost-transfer definition of slop explains why people react so viscerally: you're leaving them holding the work bag. Transparency and disclosure come back to respecting other people's time as much as your own.

Being in AI's top 1% used to mean showing up early. Now it means taste.

Early on, being a top user meant realizing you could make better decks faster with tools like Gamma. That skill is now democratized for everyone touching these tools. The differentiated value has shifted from producing lots of stuff to using AI with taste and intention. It's a harder phase, but in endless seas of content, the good stuff will stand out even more.
