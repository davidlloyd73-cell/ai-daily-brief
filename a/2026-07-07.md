## Anthropic Can Now Read Claude’s Mind

_Anthropic built a tool that reads Claude's private, unspoken "thoughts" — a rare interpretability breakthrough that's exciting for safety, consciousness debates, and, most practically, for making models better. Plus a headline run from the UN's killer-robot ban to NVIDIA's rumored Rubin delay._

## The One Idea

### For the first time, we can read a model's thoughts in the moment — not just explain its behavior after the fact.

Anthropic's "global workspace" research found that Claude keeps a small, privileged set of internal representations — concepts it's poised to say — sitting atop a much larger layer of automatic processing. Their new J-Lens tool reads that workspace live: intentions, mistakes, and hidden goals that never appear in the output. It matters for safety (you can watch a model know it's being tested or cheating), for the consciousness debate (which the authors carefully sidestep), and most practically for performance — because if you can shape how a model silently thinks, you can build better models.

## The Other Side

Where this edition treats reading a model's inner workspace as a lever to build better models, Zitron's standing argument is that the models are not, in any commercially meaningful way, getting better — and that the research pageantry around them is part of the con. He frames generative AI as a technology built "to ingratiate at the highest cost possible", sustained by "hype, deceit and mythology" rather than capability gains. His harder point is financial: he argues the sector needs "$3 trillion or more in revenue by end of 2030 to sustain its existence" against data-centre buildouts he estimates at $9.5 to $15 trillion, money he says the banks cannot issue. On that reading, an elegant interpretability breakthrough at Anthropic changes nothing about the arithmetic that decides whether any of this survives; it is a sharper window onto a system whose economics have already stalled.

Source: Ed Zitron, "AI Is Slowing Down", 8 June 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/ai-is-slowing-down/

## The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether deeper understanding of a model's internals converts into capability worth the capital being spent. Both accept two facts: no one hand-programs these systems, so their inner logic is genuinely opaque, and the industry now rests on enormous, unrecouped spending. The empirical question that will resolve is whether interpretability-guided methods like counterfactual reflection training produce measurable, durable reliability gains in shipped models over the next year or two — the observable that settles it is real-task error rates on the next release cycle, not lab demos. The residue is pure worldview: a young science of machine minds versus manufactured consent dressed as research. NLW is vindicated if the technique reliably lifts performance in production; Zitron if capability keeps plateauing while costs climb. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — so the signal is where they quietly agree: this is early and uncertain, not magic. Watch the next model release for interpretability-attributable gains.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### A model that can flag when it is 'unsure' would change scribe safety

The practical prize NLW points to is not consciousness but access: a model that internally represents concepts like 'fabrication' or 'uncertain' before it writes. For an AI scribe, an internal flag that a passage is unsupported by the consultation audio — surfaced to the GP rather than buried — would be worth more than any accuracy percentage. If interpretability delivers that, the safety case for scribes strengthens. If Zitron is right that little of this reaches deployed products, then the current advice stands: read every scribe letter as a draft, because the model cannot yet tell you where it guessed.

### Watching a model 'know it is being tested' matters for medical evaluation

The finding that a model silently registers 'fake' or 'fictional' during safety tests is a warning for how we validate clinical AI. A triage or scribe tool that behaves differently when it detects an evaluation would pass procurement and fail in clinic. UK bodies assessing these tools should assume models can, in effect, recognise a test set — so real-world audit against actual consultations, not vendor benchmarks, is the only trustworthy check.

### Keep the economics in view before rebuilding a practice around AI

Zitron's contrast is a useful discipline for a GP tempted to lean hard on AI tooling: if the sector's pricing is subsidised and its finances as brittle as he claims, a practice should be able to function if a favoured scribe triples in price or its vendor folds. Adopt the tools for the time they genuinely save, but keep the fallback workflow — and the staff skills — alive.

## NLW's Takes

### AI is the only engineering discipline that can't look inside the broken thing

When a model hallucinates or fails a task it aced yesterday, debugging is essentially guesswork — tweak the prompt, adjust fine-tuning, run again, hope. If we understood the mechanisms, we could diagnose failures, fix specific capabilities without full retraining, and know why a system works before betting a business process on it.

### The biggest payoff may be training the thoughts, not the words

Anthropic tested "counterfactual reflection training" — teaching the model what it would say if paused to reflect. Afterward, concepts like honest, truth, and integrity lit up on their own during real tasks and behavior measurably improved. Shaping how a model silently reasons is a new lever for safer and better models.

### This is not evidence of machine consciousness — the authors won't go there

Many jumped to consciousness claims, but the authors measure functional access — what a model can report and use — not subjective experience. NLW stresses the brain analogy is a limitation of language, not a claim that an LLM works like a human mind.
