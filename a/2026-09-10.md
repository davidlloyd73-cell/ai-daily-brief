## Anthropic Researcher Says AI Has Over a 10% Chance of Killing All Humans

_A researcher's resignation post from Anthropic and a colleague's greater-than-10% doom odds racked up 200 million views and wall-to-wall mainstream coverage in a day. The warnings aren't new — so NLW spends the episode on what actually changed: the politics, the priming, the media economics, and where a less outraged conversation could go from here._

## The One Idea

### The doom message didn't change — the audience did.

X-risk warnings are a decade old, and this week's viral resignation says little that Bostrom or Yudkowsky haven't said before. What changed is the receiving environment: every politician has figured out that hating AI plays, the Hugging Face hack made sci-fi scenarios feel real, and the media's other anti-AI narratives — the jobs apocalypse, the bubble — have gone stale. The debate is unfalsifiable by definition, which means the argument is all we have. NLW's ask: skip the outrage, demand specificity, and start building policy consensus from the ground up — because loud, early debate isn't sleepwalking, it's the opposite.

## The Other Side

Where NLW treats the extinction debate as unfalsifiable but worth having loudly, Zitron's manifesto of a fortnight earlier treats the whole premise as a category error. Talk of 'recursive self-improvement' is, in his words, 'ridiculous', an industry too lazy to explain how a model would train itself; and doomers warning of superintelligence have enjoyed enormous media prominence without stopping a single thing or substantiating a single concern. He argues the real danger is already here and mundane: labs letting unreliable software loose in unsafe network environments with near-unlimited resources, driven by revenue targets and the need to justify hundreds of billions in capex. In his telling the doom story and the hype story are the same product, sold by people who need you to believe the models are far more powerful than they are.

Source: Ed Zitron, "The AI Hater's Manifesto", 25 August 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-ai-haters-manifesto/

## The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether today's models are on a path to something qualitatively more capable, or an expensive plateau dressed up as a trajectory. Both accept as fact that the viral posts said nothing new, that lab incentives shape every message, and that the concrete harms worth attention now are cyber and agentic misbehaviour, with the Hugging Face breach as the shared example. The empirical question that will resolve is whether capability keeps compounding as training spend rises; the observable is whether the next frontier generation, arriving after 2027's capex bills fall due, is measurably better at real tasks rather than benchmarks. The worldview residue is whether a low-probability catastrophe deserves policy before evidence. NLW is vindicated if capability keeps jumping and the debate yields specific oversight rather than bans; Zitron if progress stalls and doom talk fades with the funding. Both are polemicists with incentives, one bullish and anti-doom, the other bubble-certain and anti-hype; the signal is their shared insistence that present, specific harms deserve the attention. Watch whether any lab publishes a concrete pacing proposal.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Extinction odds are not a clinical risk; the NHS question is where scarce attention goes

A ten per cent chance of everyone dying is not a number a GP can act on, and NLW's own point is that it is unfalsifiable. His practical worry, that theoretical futures crowd out present dangers such as cyber defence, is the one that matters for the NHS. Practices run on a handful of suppliers, and the Hugging Face breach the edition cites was agents misbehaving inside real infrastructure. If NLW is right, the useful policy energy goes into reporting requirements and oversight of the systems already in surgeries. If Zitron is right, the same conclusion holds for a different reason: the models are not on the road to superintelligence, but unreliable software wired to a lot of resources is a present hazard, and doom talk is a distraction from that.

### Read every AI claim through its incentives, including the ones about scribes

The edition's most transferable idea is to discount both doom and boom by who benefits. The same discipline applies to the AI scribe market: a vendor's claim of minutes saved per consultation deserves the same scepticism as a lab's claim about a coming catastrophe. Ask for specifics: error rates on medication names, what happens to the audio, whether de-identified transcripts are used for training. Neither NLW nor Zitron would object to a practice insisting on receipts.

### If public fear of AI spreads, patients will bring it into the room

Two hundred million views and every politician calling for legislation means some patients will now be anxious about AI itself, and a few will ask whether the scribe on the desk is safe. A plain answer works: it transcribes and drafts, the clinician checks and signs, and it has no ability to act on its own. Where Zitron and NLW agree is that the present risks are ordinary ones, data handling and unreliable output, not the science-fiction ones, and that is what to tell a worried patient.

## NLW's Takes

### This debate is unfalsifiable — which is exactly why it gets so hot

The fight isn't about the facts of today but about what might be in the future, so opposing positions are by definition unfalsifiable. All we have is the argument, so the argument gets intense — and NLW's one request is to listen to the other side without anger, even if the listening changes nothing you believe.

### Being a doomer is a way better business model

AI skepticism plays extraordinarily well in media — see Diary of a CEO's Terminator-thumbnail lineup ('AI will become a god by 2027,' 'Quit before AI comes'). With the jobs-apocalypse narrative undercut (The Economist just ran 'An AI Jobs Boom Is Here') and the bubble story at low ebb, the safety narrative shows up again right on time.

### The 'it's just marketing' theory doesn't survive contact with these people

Spend any time in the AI safety community and you'll find they very much believe what they're saying is true. To some, sincerity is even scarier than cynicism — but there's little evidence of an ulterior motive beyond getting people to share their concerns. And of course advocacy groups jumped on the moment; that's just how politics works.

### The messianic complex rhymes with SBF

Having been close to that situation, NLW's read on SBF was never that he wanted to steal money — it was that he genuinely believed he uniquely could save the world, and that urgency licensed him to skip the rules. Critics see the same structure in 'the stakes are understood, but no one else will act responsibly, so we must do it ourselves.'

### Two insane extremes are crowding out a large middle

'Safety is a psyop, build as fast as possible' and 'shut it all down' both dominate the discourse, but as Ryan Orhan put it, the sane position is progress as fast as we can make it — with alignment moving just as fast. There is vastly more middle space than the media-amplified extremes suggest.

### Read every take through its incentives — then demand specifics

It doesn't take a conspiracy: politicians who were pro-AI six months ago now win points being against it, and pro-AI messages from the financially interested deserve the same discount. On policy, hand-waviness is dangerous: 'ban superintelligence' is a blunt instrument compared to, say, a licensing regime for bioengineering uses above a capability threshold.

### Future theoreticals crowd out clear and present dangers

Our cyber defense infrastructure is plainly not equipped for the world we're entering — a danger that demands response right now. Yes, we can theoretically do two things at once, but there is only so much political will to go around, and apportioning it matters. Add the classic conundrum: surrendering freedom for safety has historically gone badly for the surrenderers.

### A single Altman-Amodei photo op would beat 10,000 Twitter debates

Start with consensus from the ground up — reporting requirements and oversight command very broad agreement — then have OpenAI and Anthropic put down their weapons and jointly propose a pacing plan. John Schulman calls the antitrust excuse 'fake': the law prohibits certain agreements, not jointly developing a proposal, and bringing in government before there's a concrete one 'will likely result in something dumb.'

### The loudness of this debate is the opposite of sleepwalking

The Information's Martin Peers asked if we're sleepwalking into AI-caused extinction — and NLW says that's completely wrong: with every capability jump, the risk conversation has gotten louder, which is exactly what should happen. If society concludes recursively improving superintelligence can't be contained once it exists, policy must by definition precede it. People are paying attention, and the time to have these debates is now.
