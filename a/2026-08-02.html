Everything You Need to Know About AI Tokens

An Operator's Cut with Nufar Gaspar on the term that's dominating every enterprise AI conversation: the token. From the all-inclusive era to token-maxing leaderboards to today's anxiety-driven self-censorship, this is the primer on what a token actually is, why the bill is so hard to read, and how to spend wisely rather than sparingly.

The One Idea

Spend tokens wisely, not sparingly — and measure cost per accepted task, not cost per token.

After the all-inclusive, token-maxing, and now token-anxious eras, the goal is the token-smart era. Understand what a token really is, recognize that tokens aren't born equal across labs and layers, and stop reading the bill in tokens — which are an unmeterable moving target — and start reading it in dollars per accepted task. Kill the tokens that spin, tune the tokens that produce, and fearlessly defend the tokens that teach.

The Other Side

This edition's premise is that token spend is a management problem — spend wisely, measure cost per accepted task, and the arithmetic comes good. Zitron, five days earlier, argued that the arithmetic does not come good at any level of discipline, because the price you are quoted is not the cost of the thing. His evidence is that heavy usage destroys the seller, not the buyer: Anthropic and OpenAI, he writes, "allow users to burn $8000 to $14,000 a month for $200", and GitHub Copilot only reached scale by letting subscribers "burn more than 25 times their monthly subscription fee" — with user revolts when metered billing arrived. He notes that NVIDIA's promised twenty-five-fold cut in inference cost has been quietly pared back to ten, and that even that has made no AI company profitable. His conclusion is that "using AI only seems to be getting more expensive" for everyone in the chain, and that a cost-per-accepted-task discipline optimises a number that is subsidised in the first place.

Source: Ed Zitron, “The More You Buy, The More You Lose”, 28 July 2026 (from 28 July’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

Both men orbit one variable: whether the price of a unit of AI work reflects what it costs to produce. They agree on more than the framing suggests — tokens are opaque, agentic work multiplies consumption several-fold, and the heaviest users pay a small fraction of what they consume. The empirical question that will resolve is whether inference gets cheap enough to be simultaneously profitable to sell and worth buying; the observable that settles it is published inference gross margins and whether the flat-rate heavy-use plans survive without conversion to metered billing. The residue is pure worldview: whether discipline is the answer or the distraction. Whittemore is vindicated if measured cost per accepted task falls while capability holds and vendors still make money. Zitron is vindicated if the subsidy is withdrawn and the true price makes the work uneconomic. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is that both now say the sticker price tells you nothing. Watch whether flat-rate plans quietly become metered.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Your scribe is on a flat fee today. This edition explains why that may not hold.

Almost every ambient scribe sold into UK general practice is priced per clinician per month, which makes budgeting easy and hides the meter entirely. The edition's own numbers show why that pricing is fragile: agentic work runs five to thirty times the tokens of a simple chat, and when Anthropic reissued Opus in April the price sheet was unchanged while the same text produced about thirty per cent more tokens. If Whittemore is right, inference keeps getting cheaper and the flat fee survives. If Zitron is right, the subsidy is withdrawn and per-seat becomes per-use. Neither requires action now, but it is worth knowing your notice period and whether renewal locks the current price, before the conversation is had for you.

The self-censoring clinician is the real cost, not the tokens

The best line in the edition is Nufar Gaspar's: the most expensive token is the one your best person is afraid to spend. Translated to a surgery, that is the partner who stops using the scribe on the complicated twenty-minute consultation — precisely where it saves the most time and where the note matters most — because a complex case feels extravagant. The arithmetic says the opposite. An email-length exchange costs roughly half a penny; the deep, messy consultation is exactly what you bought the thing for. If a colleague is rationing, the fix is telling them the actual unit cost, not adding a policy.

Cost per accepted task has a clean clinical translation

The edition's central metric — cost per accepted task, counting retries and correction rather than raw consumption — maps almost exactly onto the only scribe question worth asking: what proportion of notes go into the record without meaningful editing? That is measurable in an afternoon. Take twenty consultations across two clinicians, record whether each draft was accepted unchanged, lightly edited, or rewritten, and note the minutes spent correcting. It gives a number for the next contract discussion and, more usefully, tells you whether the tool is stronger in some consultation types than others. The same logic applies to Medical Examiner work, where the acceptable error rate on a scrutiny summary is effectively zero and a draft that needs checking line by line has saved nothing.

NLW’s Takes

Overspending beats underspending on a one-year timescale

NLW: the hand-wringing over gamed leaderboards is overwrought — of course people game systems with real stakes. He'll bet any amount that a company wildly overspending via a token leaderboard will be farther ahead than one that underspends out of fear of proving ROI.

Higher-value use cases inherently consume more tokens

NLW: the direction of travel is clear — more advanced, more useful work requires more intelligence and thus more tokens. Unaddressed token anxiety incentivizes people to stay swimming in daily emails instead of doing the deep research and agentic work leaders actually want.

Spin isn't only mistakes — it's things that quietly stopped being worth it

NLW ran an OpenClaw agent perpetually researching new AI adoption data sources; it did exactly what it was told but simply wasn't valuable enough for the cost. Auditing spin means catching processes that accidentally became spin — like hourly Slack miners or a morning brief nobody reads — not just outright errors.

Model routing is still very early — and preferences still matter

NLW expects routing norms to be solved first for deterministic software engineering and to stay far messier for knowledge work, where preference is about the nature of a response, not benchmark scores. The GPT-5 auto-router frustrated super users — proof people want control — so understanding model capabilities remains a high-leverage skill.
