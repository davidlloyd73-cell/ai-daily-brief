The New Problems AI Is Creating (And How People Are Solving Them)

A weekend deep-dive on the second-order problems of the agentic era — AI slop, token economics, de-skilling, the vanishing pipeline of expertise — and the case that companies aren't the bumbling laggards the media paints, but are already solving these problems in public, one shared playbook at a time.

Source: https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-08-16

The One Idea

We've stopped asking if AI is a thing — and started solving the problems it creates.

A year ago enterprises were still debating whether AI was overhyped. Now the questions have flipped from 'if' to 'how': how to police AI slop with writing policies instead of bans, how to allocate scarce tokens like capital instead of SaaS seats, how to measure value per unit of intelligence, and how to keep building experts when AI eats the grunt work. New technologies solve old problems while creating new ones — and the encouraging story of this year is how fast, and how publicly, organizations are working the new ones.

The Other Side

NLW's lead is that the argument has moved from whether AI is real to how to do it well, and that companies are working the second-order problems — token budgets, writing policies, de-skilling — quickly and in public. Zitron's most recent free piece argues that this sophistication is beside the point if the paying demand it implies never turns up in the vendors' own numbers. He puts Microsoft's fiscal 2026 AI revenue at roughly $34.4 billion, of which about $24.1 billion is OpenAI's own compute spend, leaving around $10.3 billion from every other AI product Microsoft sells, against $261.3 billion of capital expenditure since 2022 — "$10.33 billion in annual revenue is a catastrophic failure." His inference is blunt: "If Microsoft doesn't have the demand, nobody has the demand," and the enterprise playbooks being shared so enthusiastically are a story the market tells itself rather than a line of revenue anyone can point at.

Source: Ed Zitron, “Don't Look Up”, 11 August 2026 (from 11 August’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The single variable both men orbit is whether enterprise AI use converts into money — real, paid, non-lab demand. They accept more in common than the framing suggests: that inference carries a genuine marginal cost, that firms have exhausted annual budgets in months, and that the two frontier labs account for most of what is currently being spent on compute. The empirical question is what non-lab enterprise AI revenue does over the next year, and the observable that settles it is hyperscaler AI revenue reported separately from OpenAI's and Anthropic's own compute purchases. Whittemore is vindicated if that residual line grows sharply while firms raise budgets on measured returns; Zitron if it stays near ten billion for Microsoft while capital expenditure climbs. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is what they agree on despite opposite priors: tokens are an operating expense and value has to be shown per unit. Watch the next quarter's disclosure of AI revenue apart from lab compute.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

The cognitive commons problem is the GP training problem, and it is already here

The paper NLW cites names something general practice will recognise immediately: checking the work requires expertise, expertise is built from years of unglamorous work, and the unglamorous work is exactly what gets automated first. Registrars have always learned by summarising notes, drafting the referral, reading the whole result set and having it picked apart in tutorial. Take that away and you keep the output while losing the apprenticeship that made someone able to spot when the output is wrong. His counter-question is the fair one — grunt work is not a law of nature — but nobody has yet shown what replaces it, and a training practice cannot wait for the literature. The practical move is small and boring: keep some work deliberately unassisted for trainees, and make the review of AI-drafted material an explicit teaching exercise rather than a rubber stamp.

Every prompt is a running cost — which changes what a practice should sign up to

The point about AI being an operating expense rather than a one-off purchase matters more in a partnership than in a large corporate. If Zitron is right that the true cost has never been passed on and the reckoning is coming, a scribe subscription that is comfortable at today's price could be uncomfortable at three times it, and the practice that built its whole documentation workflow around it has no easy way back. If NLW is right and this is simply capital allocation done properly, the same discipline applies for the opposite reason: you want to know the value per unit, not the seat count. Either way the question to the supplier is identical — what is the price when the current contract ends, and could we run for a fortnight without it?

Clay's writing policy translates almost directly to clinical records

The four rules — stand behind every sentence, writing is thinking so do not circumvent it, spend more time writing than the reader spends reading, and longer is not better — read like guidance for consultation notes and Medical Examiner scrutiny summaries. A record padded by a model is worse than a short honest one, and length has never been a proxy for care. The useful framing is that the policy bans laziness rather than the tool: I sign the note, so I have to have thought it. That is also the practical defence when a record is later read by a coroner, a complaints team or a colleague picking up the patient at three in the morning.

NLW’s Takes

Productivity gains are as jagged as the capabilities

Some areas deliver immediate, transparent, huge gains; others stay stubbornly stuck. And integrating agentic work with new human oversight creates a whole new set of work that, in the short term, fills in the time you won back — a transitional phase, not a wash.

Agentic AI broke the SaaS math

For AI's first few years, the equation was headcount times seat cost times twelve months versus value created. Agentic AI makes it less like software and more like a new type of labor — and the real surprise wasn't the shift itself, but how fast enfranchised employees could burn through expensive tokens.

Token budgets aren't panic — they're architecture

The media discourse treating enterprises as incompetent bumpkins frantically slapping on usage caps is infuriating and wrong. Serious organizations are building complete architectures — different models and structures for different problems, different access levels for different people — and pairing caps with pathways to apply for more budget or demonstrate you deserve it.

Blaming 40% of layoffs on AI is 'a complete and utter crock'

The 'AI makes labor redundant' misconception was pushed by two groups: lab leaders (some now walking it back) and business leaders who needed a market-acceptable excuse for layoffs. That excuse isn't working anymore — and every story of companies re-hiring people they fired puts a dagger further into it.

The best AI writing policy doesn't ban AI — it bans laziness

Clay's policy doesn't brand anyone with a scarlet letter for using AI or even create subtle social pressure against it. It's an injunction to respect that the process of producing something is often as valuable as the output — and with 8,377 reactions on the post, expect it to show up at many more organizations soon.

Demanding ROI proof for every use case selects for the smallest wins

As organizations design more sophisticated ways to allocate scarce tokens, requiring everyone to prove ROI for every single use case will yield only the easiest-to-prove use cases — simple productivity enhancements, not the total reimaginings of work where the real leverage lives.

The big TLDR: we've gone from 'if' questions to 'how' questions

Over the past year, businesses moved from the not-particularly-useful question of whether AI is going to be a thing to the far more valuable questions of how to do it well. Keep asking those questions and sharing your answers in public — so everyone doesn't have to solve them alone.
