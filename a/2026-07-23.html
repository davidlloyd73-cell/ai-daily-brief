The One Idea

AI market freakouts follow a pattern — and the pattern is why the bubble hasn't formed.

Since ChatGPT, investors have cycled through the same anxieties: cheap Chinese models, circular financing, revenue not justifying spend, CapEx running too hot, token caps, scaling walls. NLW's field guide walks each one, notes they often coincide with predictable summer seasonality, and lands on a counterintuitive conclusion — the market's relentless determination to call everything a bubble is itself the pressure-release valve keeping a runaway 1999-style bubble from ever taking hold.

The Other Side

Where NLW argues the market's relentless bubble-hunting is a pressure-release valve, that circular financing is nothing like the dot-com trap because OpenAI pays cash for GPUs, and that cancelled data centres reflect hard construction rather than weak demand, Zitron's argument is the mirror image. He contends the AI build-out is a genuine debt-funded asset bubble whose special-purpose vehicles are the equivalent of 2008's collateralised debt obligations: off-balance-sheet structures, reminiscent of Enron, that hide something like 1.65 trillion dollars of obligations while NVIDIA finances the very companies buying its chips and then rents that capacity back. He estimates that 70 to 90 percent of compute demand comes from OpenAI and Anthropic, two loss-making firms, and that roughly fifteen times more data-centre capacity is being built than real demand supports. On his reading the freakouts are not preventing a bubble; they are belatedly noticing a real one.

Source: Ed Zitron, “The Subprime Data Center Crisis”, 22 July 2026 (from 22 July’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The single variable both men orbit is whether demand for AI compute is real and durable enough to service the debt now being raised against it. Both accept the facts of scale: staggering capital spending heading past a trillion dollars, roughly half the S&P 500 AI-exposed, and much of the financing routed through hyperscalers and neoclouds. The empirical question that will resolve is whether paying customers exist beyond OpenAI and Anthropic to fill the capacity, and whether those two can keep paying. The observable that settles it is third-party compute revenue and remaining performance obligations from buyers who are not hyperscalers or the two big labs, plus whether the data-centre SPVs actually meet their debt-service ratios once leases begin. The residue is worldview: NLW reads compulsive bubble-calling as self-correcting; Zitron reads it as recognition of hidden systemic leverage. NLW is vindicated if diverse enterprise demand climbs to absorb the build and spending keeps being funded without defaults; Zitron if demand stays concentrated in two unprofitable labs and the SPVs start missing payments. Both are polemicists with incentives, NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype, so the signal is where they agree despite opposite priors: both concede the spending is enormous and both concede it currently hinges on two loss-making companies. Watch whether compute revenue from anyone other than OpenAI, Anthropic and their hyperscaler backers actually materialises.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Whether it is a bubble or not, do not bet the surgery on the cheapest tool

The whole edition is an argument about AI's finances, and the two camps disagree completely. If Zitron is right that the build-out is a debt-funded bubble propped up by two loss-making labs, then some of today's AI clinical tools are being sold below cost and a shake-out could see prices jump, features withdrawn, or a supplier fold. If NLW is right that the market simply overreacts and the sector grows steadily, the risk is milder but not zero. Either way the practical instruction for a practice is the same: when choosing an AI scribe or triage tool, favour a supplier likely to be solvent in three years, read the exit terms, and make sure patient records and letters can be exported if the product disappears.

Concentration risk applies to clinical suppliers too

Zitron's central claim is that almost all AI demand traces back to a handful of firms and their backers. Most AI scribes and triage products are thin wrappers over a model from OpenAI, Anthropic, Google or a Chinese lab. If NLW is right, that is fine and the models keep improving. If Zitron is right, a wobble at one lab could degrade or interrupt a tool a surgery has come to depend on mid-clinic. It is a fair due-diligence question to ask any vendor which model sits underneath, and what happens to your service if that provider raises prices sharply or pulls access.

The honest healthcare read: this is a money story, not a safety story

Unlike some editions, there is little direct clinical content here. The value for general practice is disposition, not diagnosis: do not over-commit to long contracts or expensive integrations on the assumption that today's generous pricing is permanent, and keep any AI tool as an assistant with a clinician in the loop, so that a commercial collapse upstream is an inconvenience rather than a clinical risk.

NLW’s Takes

Even passive indexers are making a bet on AI

With roughly 50% of the S&P 500 now AI or AI-exposed, NLW argues everyone needs to understand the narrative — even a 401(k) contributor or passive indexer carries significant AI exposure whether they realize it or not.

That MIT '95% of pilots fail' study was garbage science

NLW dismisses the notorious report as 'absolute garbage social science MIT should be embarrassed to be associated with' — but notes it didn't matter, because the headline landed on every Wall Street desk anyway.

The market's bubble obsession is what prevents a bubble

NLW argues that a generation raised on The Big Short calling everything a bubble is a feature, not a bug: every time the market gets over its skis, there's a pressure release. We're not seeing the frenetic 1999-2000 boom-and-bust IPO frenzy.

Cheap models don't matter if you can't serve them

NLW argues this China round resolves once people realize inference capacity matters more than price: Moonshot was completely tapped out of compute on K3's opening weekend, and it's questionable whether all Chinese labs combined can serve even a fraction of US companies' users.

NLW's base case: frontier tokens sell at a premium for five years

He expects every frontier token to be bought at a premium for at least five years even as cheaper workloads come online, because premium state-of-the-art supply stays ahead of demand. A flourishing of routers, fine-tuning and vertical model plays distributes AI's revenue gains and relieves pressure on the big two labs to carry the whole market.
