10 Ways to Think Bigger with Opportunity AI

GPT-6 Astra is the smartest and strangest model yet — coders are retreating to older models while everyone else discovers it can do things that were never part of their job. NLW turns that confounding release into a playbook: a dozen thought starters for opportunity AI, from marketing people can play to proposals clients can reshape to packaging your own judgment as a product.

The One Idea

The new models' value is in things you've never considered — which means you need thought starters, not benchmarks.

Broad model capability has reached a level where new releases like GPT-6 Astra often won't make your existing AI work noticeably better — sometimes they'll even feel like a regression. Their real value is unlocking capabilities you've never used: games, video pipelines, 3D, interactive experiences. But by definition, you haven't considered what you've never considered. Nobody walks around with a complete inventory of things they might make. So this episode is a set of thought starters for opportunity AI — the mindset of asking what you might do now that you never would have before.

The Other Side

NLW's answer to a model that makes familiar work no better, and sometimes worse, is that you should go looking for work you have never done: games, video pipelines, 3D, simulators. Zitron's Manifesto, written three weeks before Astra, reads that instruction as the tell. He says LLMs have got better in ways that are increasingly hard to define and that do not make economic sense for the user, that constant model churn makes it impossible to know whether the model improved or you merely got better at building harnesses round it, and that newer models often burn more tokens to do the same job, so the price of work rises while the price of intelligence falls. The call to experiment he treats, borrowing Robin Sloan's word, as "Jarvising": tinkering that feels like productivity and produces none, sustained by the fact that the only way to find out whether it works is to spend more money. His own experiments "sort of worked" after repeated prodding; the tolerable use, in his telling, is small, bounded, verifiable tasks, and the technology is "not useful enough to be profitable nor reliable enough to be world-changing".

Source: Ed Zitron, “The AI Hater's Manifesto”, 25 August 2026 (from 25 August’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether the value of a new model shows up in work that already exists. Both accept as fact that Astra does not make ordinary tasks noticeably better: NLW says so in his opening, Zitron's whole essay is built on it. Both accept that serious coders have retreated to an older model on cost, and that benchmark gains no longer map onto felt usefulness. The empirical question that will resolve is whether the novel capabilities, games, video pipelines, 3D, interactive proposals, become paid, repeated, non-hobbyist work within a year. The observable is retention: how many of this month's weekend Astra experiments are still running, and being paid for, in a year. The worldview residue is whether tinkering is discovery or displacement activity. NLW is vindicated if a recognisable class of business output, the interactive proposal or the custom video pipeline, becomes routine; Zitron if the experiments stay demos and the bills keep rising. Both are polemicists with incentives, one bullish and anti-doom, the other bubble-certain and anti-hype; the signal is that both concede the efficiency story has stalled at the frontier. Watch whether Astra's coding revenue recovers or whether OpenAI quietly reprices it.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Scribes are efficiency AI, and the frontier no longer moves them much

Ambient scribes are the clearest efficiency use in general practice: the consultation is the same, the letter arrives faster. NLW's point that a new frontier model may make existing work no better, or even worse, is worth remembering when a scribe vendor announces an upgrade to Astra or its rivals. If NLW is right, the gains left in scribing are small and the interesting work lies elsewhere. If Zitron is right, the upgrade may cost the vendor more tokens per consultation for no visible change, and that cost eventually reaches the practice. Either way, judge a scribe on its error rate in your own surgery, not on which model sits underneath it.

The what-if machine is the one thought starter that fits a practice

Most of NLW's dozen ideas, games for marketing, mini documentaries, 3D prototypes, have little to do with a GP surgery. The one that does is the decision simulator. 'We need another salaried session' hides several disagreements about demand, telephone triage capacity and how work is shared, exactly the argument every partnership has. A small model that lets partners change the assumptions and see the consequences is cheap to build now and does not touch patient data. If Zitron is right it will be tinkering that feels like progress; the test is whether a partners' meeting actually changes a decision because of it.

Simulation for judgement is the old idea in new clothes

NLW's eighth thought starter, simulated situations that give feedback on judgement mid-conversation, is what GP training has done with actors and video for forty years, at considerable cost. A simulated difficult consultation or a complaint response that a trainee can practise on a laptop is plausible today and the NHS's Medical Examiner and training bodies will be pitched it. The Zitron caution applies: a simulation that 'sort of works' after prodding is worse than a role-play with a colleague, so ask who validated the scenarios and against what.

NLW’s Takes

Astra is the first model that actually dances inside the efficiency/opportunity divide

Efficiency AI does your existing work better — faster, cheaper. Opportunity AI unlocks entirely new opportunities. That's usually been a mindset distinction rather than a difference in the models themselves; Astra is one of the first models where the difference is real.

Efficiency use cases will become table stakes

There's nothing wrong with efficiency AI — it's the foundation of most AI use and most initial value. But companies that only think of AI as an efficiency technology will miss what the winners see: efficiency gains reset expectations for everyone, while the companies that chase new opportunities — even ones orthogonal to what they do today — race out ahead.

Ask people to 'go find AI opportunities' and you get a blank page

People don't walk around with a complete inventory of things they might make — we carry a smaller inventory shaped by our job, tools, and what people around us do. NLW's own opportunity use cases, like the pipeline that turns episodes into shareable chunks, came from stumbling, experimenting, and attacking existing problems in new ways.

The shortcut: copy what people with other jobs are doing

Many of the 'opportunities' of opportunity AI aren't totally novel — they're things other people can already do that you couldn't. A good shorthand for stretching yourself is to look at what people in different roles are doing that you think is really cool, and bring it into your own work.

Most games fail — the point is you can finally try

Brands have experimented with games before, but they were constrained by development resources; now a solopreneur can experiment this weekend. Game design is still hard — far more games are released than ever become popular, even from professionals. Opportunity AI doesn't guarantee hits; it lowers the cost of the attempt.

Interactive proposals are efficiency and opportunity AI in one

Done well, this is a new way of interacting with clients that wasn't possible before — and it radically cuts the latency of back-and-forth in every negotiation. It has the feel of something that will become completely de rigueur, to the point we'll struggle to remember doing it any other way.

The pattern underneath: build what-if machines

A thread weaves through many of these ideas — interactive proposals, decision simulators — they're all what-if machines. What if we did this? What would the implications be? What trade-offs would it implicate? Making that reasoning manipulable is a core opportunity-AI move.

Efficiency and opportunity AI aren't at war

The goal of opportunity AI is simply to stretch ourselves and ask what we might do now that we never would have before — several of these ideas turn out to be both at once. The point of the thought starters is that you don't have to know in advance what your opportunity looks like.
