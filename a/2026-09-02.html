Why Fable 5.1 Is Worth the Upgrade

Anthropic ships Fable 5.1 and Mythos 5.1 — unambiguously state-of-the-art and pitched as much on price, data retention, and safeguards as raw capability. But real-world costs are messier than the blog post, and the real question isn't whether to switch anymore: it's where the new model fits in your stack. Plus, OpenAI says Astra has crossed its critical cybersecurity threshold.

The One Idea

Stop asking whether to switch models. Ask where the new one fits.

Fable 5.1 is the new state of the art on essentially every benchmark, and Anthropic is selling it as much on cost cuts, zero data retention, and better safeguards as on capability. But with token-hungry defaults blowing through usage limits, the old question — is this good enough to switch to? — is obsolete. The best users are building a personal model architecture: matching each model to the tasks where it's genuinely better, testing against their own standing benchmarks, and refusing the delusion that generally capable models are interchangeable.

The Other Side

Zitron would say the Fable 5.1 launch proves his point rather than Anthropic's. In the Manifesto he argues that everyone talks about price as if price were the problem, when the real problem is the number of tokens burned — and Artificial Analysis finding 5.1 seventy per cent hungrier and dearer per task than its predecessor is exactly the pattern he describes, where the 'cost of intelligence' falls on paper while the cost of actually getting something done rises. He notes the earlier Fable model plateaued at around eleven per cent of Anthropic's usage because of its price, that labs are incentivised to build inefficient models, and that subscriptions are subsidised precisely because ordinary users would not pay the true cost. So the 'personal model architecture' NLW recommends is, on Zitron's reading, users being asked to do unpaid cost-engineering for a product whose pricing and rate limits can change at the vendor's whim.

Source: Ed Zitron, “The AI Hater's Manifesto”, 25 August 2026 (from 25 August’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is the real cost of a completed task, not the sticker price per million tokens. Both accept as fact that Fable 5.1 burns far more tokens than Fable 5, that Artificial Analysis measured a higher cost per task despite cheaper cache reads, and that users are hitting rate limits fast. The empirical question that will resolve is whether cost per finished job falls once defaults are fixed and norms settle, or keeps rising with each generation; the observable is Artificial Analysis-style cost-per-task figures over the next few months, alongside whether Anthropic's usage limits loosen or tighten. The pure worldview residue is whether tokens are a fair price for capability or a subsidy trap. NLW is vindicated if the sub-agent fix and effort-level tuning bring measured cost per task below Fable 5's, and if enterprises keep paying. Zitron is vindicated if each new model costs more per job and subscriptions are withdrawn in favour of metered billing. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is that both now expect subscriptions to end and API pricing to arrive. Watch the next independent cost-per-task benchmark.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Model choice is a procurement question, not a loyalty question

Fable 5.1 scored well on science and medicine tasks and its safeguards now refuse far fewer basic medical questions. If NLW is right, the sensible GP habit is a small standing slate of your own tests — a discharge summary to reconcile, a guideline to check, a dictation to tidy — and to run each new model against them rather than trusting benchmarks. If Zitron is right, the same slate matters for the opposite reason: it shows you when a 'better' model quietly costs more per finished task, which is what an AI scribe vendor will eventually pass on to the practice.

Zero data retention is the bit that matters for the NHS

The most useful line in this edition for UK practice is Anthropic offering zero data retention to eligible customers. That is precisely the condition a DPIA, Caldicott review or ICB information governance lead asks about before any patient-facing use. If NLW is right, this removes the biggest blocker to NHS adoption; if Zitron is right, terms and pricing can change at the vendor's whim, so any contract should pin retention terms down in writing rather than rely on a launch blog post.

Watch the token bill, not the licence fee

Users report the new model exhausting generous subscription limits in an hour. For a practice or PCN buying AI tools by the seat, the risk is the same either way: the licence looks fixed but the underlying cost is metered. Ask any supplier what happens to price and rate limits when the model underneath changes — both men agree subscriptions are likely to give way to usage-based billing.

NLW’s Takes

Don't judge a model's costs in its first hours

People always price a model before anyone has figured out how to use it or the norms have settled — your mileage will likely go farther than the day-one complaints suggest. That said, Jan Velick probably has it right: subscriptions will end, and API pricing is awaiting us. At this point that feels pretty inevitable.

No, the frontier models are not interchangeable

The idea that because multiple models can complete a task they're all interchangeable is like saying if two people can do the same work task, it doesn't matter who does it. There are tasks where that's true — and those are exactly what you optimize with cheaper models — but for high-end important work, the differences between models remain massive.

Keep a standing slate of personal benchmarks

They don't have to be anyone else's tasks — research, writing, strategic thinking, building — just the things that matter to you. Especially for writing and strategy, preference is subjective: no lab can publish a benchmark for iterating on your particular mad ideas, and models others complain about may work great for your tasks.

The question is stack fit, not switching

Like enterprises building multi-model architectures, individuals should know which model and setting to use for which request rather than burning everything on the most expensive frontier model at max settings. One qualification: if you can't afford to shift between models, the 'they're all generally capable' advice holds — it's never been a better time to be locked into one ecosystem.
