## The One Idea

### AI-native finally has substance — and it's not bolting agents onto old processes.

A year ago companies bragged about how many AI use cases they had. Now the agentic transition has actually begun, and the hallmarks of companies redesigned from the ground up are coming into focus: context as code, skills over prompts, loops over prompting, governance as unlock, autonomy that's earned. The through-line across all thirty features: don't constrain agents to mimic your old workflows — build the systems, metrics, and guardrails that let them find better ones.

## The Other Side

Zitron's Manifesto reads a list like Lieberman's thirty features as the disease, not the cure. Building a harness, keeping context files, distributing skills and throwing the whole thing away every three months is what Robin Sloan calls 'Jarvising': tinkering with your assistant until the tinkering becomes the work, producing 'the sense that you're being really, really productive' without anything measurable coming out the other end. He argues enterprises moved onto token-based billing this year and promptly started cutting budgets, because costs rose exponentially while nobody could point to an improvement beyond people saying they felt productive. And he says the constant rebuilding NLW celebrates is not a discipline but a symptom: nothing yet works as a stable product, so customers must keep re-plumbing whenever a model, price or harness changes.

Source: Ed Zitron, "The AI Hater's Manifesto", 25 August 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-ai-haters-manifesto/

## The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether an organisation's AI spend produces output that survives an audit. Both accept as fact that agentic tooling has spread fast in 2026, that costs are volatile and now metered per token, and that companies rebuild their workflows often. The empirical question that will resolve is whether firms that adopt the 'AI-native' pattern show measurable gains, and the observable is dull: token budgets in enterprise renewals over the next two quarters, and whether 'cost per accepted pull request' or a similar metric appears in public company disclosures. The residue is worldview: NLW sees churn as learning, Zitron sees it as churn. For NLW to be vindicated, the throw-it-away-quarterly cadence must produce rising output per pound; for Zitron, budgets must be cut once the feeling fades. Both are polemicists with incentives, one bullish and anti-doom, the other bubble-certain and anti-hype, and they agree on one thing: today's measurement is poor. Watch renewal-season token budgets.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### 'Who owns the result?' is already the NHS rule

The one feature NLW adds to the list is the one general practice never needed telling: every AI workflow needs a named owner, a measurable goal and someone accountable when it goes wrong. DCB0160 requires a clinical safety officer for exactly that reason. If NLW is right, the practices that get value from scribes, triage tools and coding agents will be the ones that treat them as a governed service with an owner, not a gadget a keen partner installed. If Zitron is right, the ownership question is what stops the spend: once a named person must justify the token bill against a measurable outcome, most of it will not survive the conversation.

### Throw it away every three months is not an option in a surgery

NLW's cadence of reimagining workflows quarterly might suit a start-up; a practice with a DSPT return, an IG lead and a clinical system supplier cannot re-plumb its scribe or its triage tool every twelve weeks, and should not try. Zitron's version of the same point is that the constant rebuilding shows nothing is stable enough to buy. The practical reading for a GP is to insist on boring stability from suppliers: a scribe that behaves the same on Monday as it did in June, with model changes notified in advance, is worth more than a clever one that keeps changing.

### Autonomy earned, not granted, is a medical model

Both men would probably accept the ladder NLW describes, from observation to suggestion to acting with approval to acting alone, because it is how trainees are supervised. For the medical examiner's office the gathering of records is a plausible 'observation' rung; the scrutiny itself sits several rungs higher and should stay there. Where they part is whether anything ever earns the top rung. If Zitron is right, the measurement needed to justify promotion simply does not exist yet, and the honest answer is that most tools stay at 'suggestion' indefinitely.

## NLW's Takes

### Blueprint every process — but don't make agents follow it

Mapping how work actually gets done unlocks the tribal knowledge trapped in people's heads and Slack side-chats, and that context is enormously valuable. But the hidden assumption that agents will do things the way humans did is very likely wrong — in many cases the better approach is giving agents the goal and the guardrails and letting them figure out the how.

### The Doctor Strange marketing era is coming — alongside Rick Rubin

Agent swarms deploying hundreds or thousands of creative variations before scaling ad spend still looks inevitable; what was underestimated was how short-term compute constraints would delay it, and as good-enough models get vanishingly cheaper over the next six months, the experimentation normalizes. Expect a barbell: crazy agentic swarms on one end, utter number-denying human taste for brand on the other — machines on one side, Rick Rubin on the other, and somehow it'll work. The same collapsed content costs make weekly SEO/AEO experimentation-and-measurement loops worth running too.
