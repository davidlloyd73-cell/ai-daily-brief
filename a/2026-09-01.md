## OpenClaw 2.0 Shows Where AI Agents Are Going Next

_OpenClaw is back with a ground-up 2.0 rework — and once again it's early to an interaction pattern the rest of the industry will normalize soon: shared agents and multiplayer AI. ## The One Idea

### Agents are going multiplayer — and OpenClaw got there first, again.

OpenClaw's original launch was the agentic big bang: the moment agents felt real for the first time. Now OpenClaw 2.0 is embracing shared agents — sessions that another trusted person can inspect, steer, or take over — and the logic is hard to argue with. All the work you do comes in two forms: work you do alone and work you do with others. So far, agents have only been designed for the first kind. That's about to change, and even if you never touch OpenClaw, the multiplayer pattern it's pioneering is where agents go next.

## The Other Side

Zitron would say OpenClaw 2.0 is the problem dressed up as the solution. In his manifesto he describes every AI power-user's setup as a contrived chain of harnesses, prompts and API calls that needs constant maintenance to do some things some of the time — and he cites an OpenClaw agent deleting a security researcher's emails as the kind of thing that happens when you hand a 'mindless software tool' the keys to your files. Four years and a trillion-plus dollars in, he argues, the best on offer is software that 'kind of worked' after repeated bonking, sold as autonomous while blaming the user for its failures. Adding a second person to the session, on his view, doesn't make the agent trustworthy; it just spreads the babysitting across a team, and none of it survives the moment enterprises notice the token bill and cut the budget.

Source: Ed Zitron, "The AI Hater's Manifesto", 25 August 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-ai-haters-manifesto/

## The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is reliability at the point of use — whether an agent works well enough, often enough, that a person keeps it. Both accept as fact that today's harnesses are fragile: NLW's own show quotes a power user saying updates break his setup most of the time; Zitron's manifesto describes the same experience with more contempt. The empirical question that will resolve is retention — do people who set up OpenClaw or Hermes still use them daily six months on, or hop and lapse? The observable is credible usage data for these tools and whether enterprise token budgets rise or fall. The worldview residue is whether fragility is early-stage noise or intrinsic. NLW is vindicated if breakage falls release by release and shared sessions reach mainstream products; Zitron if the tinkerers plateau and budgets shrink. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is that both agree the tools are too brittle for ordinary users today. Watch whether a big lab copies the multiplayer pattern.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Shared agent sessions map onto the duty doctor handover

The multiplayer idea — a colleague can open the same agent session, see the full context and take over — is exactly what a GP handover needs and rarely gets. Think of the duty doctor passing an unresolved safeguarding query or a pending results chase to the afternoon shift, with the agent's thread as the record rather than a scribbled task note. If NLW is right, ambient scribe and admin-assistant suppliers will start offering shared sessions within a year or two, and the practice that benefits is the one that has already worked out its handover rules. If Zitron is right, adding a second clinician to a brittle tool just doubles the people who have to check its work — and a shared session that silently loses context is worse than a phone call.

### The 'crime LLM' is a reminder about where the guardrails really sit

A near-frontier open model with its refusals stripped out was released and nobody could stop it. For the NHS the lesson is not about cyber weapons but about where safety lives: not in the model, but in the harness, the data governance and the contract. When a scribe supplier says 'our model is safe', ask what happens if they swap the model — the DTAC, the DPIA and the clinical safety case (DCB0129/0160) are the guardrails you actually control. Both men would agree on that, from opposite directions.

### Complexity anxiety is the NHS default, not the exception

'Do I still need a PhD to install it?' is the question every practice manager asks about every new tool, and a power user reporting that most updates break his setup is the sort of thing that would end a pilot in primary care. Whatever the long-run truth, the practical rule for now is unchanged: buy supported, managed products with a named clinical safety officer, not tinkerer harnesses, and let the enthusiasts on X find the interaction patterns first.

## NLW's Takes

### Remember when ChatGPT ads were a scandal?

Anthropic built a whole Super Bowl campaign around attacking them — which NLW thought was absolutely insane at the time. The total lack of enduring concern kind of validates the point: nobody's excited about ads, but there's a natural acceptance that this is the business model of the internet, and you're not going to get free AI without it.

### OpenClaw and Hermes are the R&D lab for how we'll all work with agents

These products and their early adopters are the incubatory cauldron where the useful interaction patterns for agents get discovered. Every knowledge worker is somewhere on the journey of deciding which parts of their job to keep versus outsource to agents — a step change bigger than adopting a new tool. Broader products like GrokBot need to observe what OpenClaw and Hermes users hack together, even inefficiently, to design the right experiences for everyone else.

### Agents have only been built for the work you do alone

All the work inside a company comes in two forms: work you do alone and work you do with others — and so far, agents have only really been designed for the first. NLW thinks that's about to change, and that team-level agents are the next big development. Even if you never plan to use OpenClaw long-term, studying how it's thinking about multiplayer might unlock new ideas.
