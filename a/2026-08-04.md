## Why AI Washing Won’t Work Much Longer

_Palantir posts an 'otherworldly' quarter while Alex Karp torches the 'token industrial complex' — and a new open-weights Qwen release lands just as a former Lululemon CIO calls time on AI wishing and washing. The thread connecting it all: the enterprise is finally asking sophisticated enough questions that faking it is about to stop paying._

## The One Idea

### AI washing won't work much longer — the enterprise is getting too sophisticated to fool.

A year ago enterprises were counting their first AI use cases; now they're debating open-weights policies, fine-tuning strategies, cost provisioning, and routers. That shift is why a release like Qwen 3.8 Max suddenly matters to enterprise IT, not just early adopters — and it's why the PR-driven era of AI wishing and washing that Lululemon's former CIO describes is running out of road. As the board-pleasing shortcuts stop paying, the incentive loop to do AI the wrong way gets cut off, and the real work of redesigning around AI can begin.

## The Other Side

NLW's lead is that enterprise demand has grown up — buyers now argue about open-weights policy, routers and cost provisioning rather than counting their first two use cases — and that this new sophistication is what will finally kill AI washing. Zitron, publishing the same day, argues that the demand being celebrated is largely manufactured. Citing Barclays estimates, he puts OpenAI's and Anthropic's compute spend at around 73% of Amazon's AI revenues, and points to Wells Fargo's figure of roughly $3.9bn for Microsoft 365 Copilot across some 30 million paid seats — a per-seat number he reads as heavy discounting rather than pull. His conclusion is blunt: "the demand for AI-powered software is not there," and hyperscaler growth is mostly the big three funding their own largest customers in a circle.

Source: Ed Zitron, "The AI Demand Bubble", 4 August 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-ai-demand-bubble/

## The Truth Beneath

Both men orbit one variable: whether AI revenue from ordinary buyers — firms that are neither a frontier lab nor a lab's backer — is real and compounding. They agree on more than the tone suggests: that capex is enormous, that the labs' own compute spend is a large share of reported AI revenue, and that much announced enterprise AI is theatre. The empirical question is customer concentration, and the observable that settles it is a hyperscaler disclosing AI revenue net of OpenAI and Anthropic compute spend, alongside revenue per paid seat for embedded AI software. The residue is worldview: whether sophisticated procurement questions show real adoption or a better-dressed purchasing ritual. NLW is vindicated if non-lab AI software revenue grows materially; Zitron if that figure stays in the low tens of billions against a trillion in capex. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is that both now call the cost-cutting AI story a fiction. Watch whether any hyperscaler breaks out AI revenue by customer next quarter.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### "AI wishing" has an NHS accent, and it sounds like a pilot

Julie Averill's phrase for waving AI at a hard problem and skipping the work of solving it will be familiar to anyone who has sat through a transformation meeting. In general practice it usually arrives as a pilot: a tool is procured, a few enthusiasts use it, a slide says the backlog will fall, and nobody redesigns the appointment book, the admin workflow or the roles around it. Her sharper point is the one to carry into any ICB conversation — this kind of work does not happen in a quarter, and believing that it can is the trap. NHS planning runs on quarters and annual returns, so the incentive to declare success early is stronger here than in most industries, not weaker.

### The layoff-and-rehire cycle is the warning worth borrowing

The figure in this edition that should travel is the survey finding that around a third of hiring managers who cut a role citing AI had already rehired for the same or a similar post. Primary care is not going to make redundancies over a scribe, but the same logic reaches us as vacancy control and skill-mix: leaving a post unfilled because a system is coming, or thinning reception and coding staff on the assumption that automation will absorb the work. Real efficiency shows up first as measurable time released — minutes per consultation, letters turned round, coding queries cleared — and only afterwards, if at all, in headcount. Reversing an establishment decision costs far more than making it, and the people asked to stay pay for it in goodwill.

### If Zitron is right the price rises; if NLW is right the choice widens — either way, ask about exit

Zitron's case is that demand outside the labs is thin and today's prices are a subsidy that must eventually be withdrawn. NLW's is that the market is broadening — a two-point-four-trillion-parameter open-weights model priced at a fifth of the frontier is now something enterprise IT actually notices. A practice cannot arbitrate that, but both readings point the same way for procurement. Do not sign anything whose value depends on current per-seat pricing holding, and treat conspicuously cheap AI as a commercial risk to record rather than a bargain. The questions stay dull: what is the notice period, where does the data sit, and can we export the record in a usable form. Note also the DNA-database item — 1995-vintage software running critical evidence with no tamper detection is not a distant problem when you consider how much NHS clinical software is of similar vintage.

## NLW's Takes

### AI is a massive upgrade for cyber defenders, not just criminals

Hundreds or thousands of outdated systems like the DNA database run critical infrastructure, and cost is the biggest reason they've never been properly tested. The ability to do even rudimentary vulnerability testing with AI on a modest budget could be a game changer — the scary-tools headline cuts both ways.

### In one year, the enterprise AI conversation grew up

At KPMG's 2025 symposium, the talk was about whether organizations had one, two, or three AI use cases. This year: governance for ubiquitous coding tools, cost provisioning across models and business units, and whether to have an open-weights model policy. The quality of the questions enterprises are asking has increased dramatically — which means enterprise IT buyers, not just early-adopter developers, now pay attention to releases like Qwen 3.8 Max.

### Why a mid-tier benchmark score doesn't kill the excitement

Open weights mean people can get their hands on the model in a totally different way. And a release that in the past only developers and early adopters would notice is now on the radar of folks inside big mainstream enterprises — that's the real shift.

### Efficiency-only AI companies will get pummeled by opportunity companies

Organizations that view AI strictly as an efficiency technology might eke out a few headline wins, but they'll ultimately be beaten by companies that understand this is a redesign moment — organizational, job-role, and process redesign — not a chance to excite shareholders about Q3 cost cuts. Shortcuts are bound to fail.

### For the first time since ChatGPT, enterprise conventional wisdom is getting directionally correct

The hills are still steep for opportunity-AI advocates inside their organizations. But attitudes toward AI wishing and washing are changing radically, the PR value and board plaudits are drying up — and that helpfully cuts off the incentive loop to do AI the wrong way, leaving the exciting work of actually redesigning around AI's capabilities.
