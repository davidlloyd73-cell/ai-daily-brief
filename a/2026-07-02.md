## AI Companies Are Hiring More

_OpenAI floats handing 5% of itself to the US government on the same day the jobs data delivers a genuine surprise: the companies going hardest on AI are the ones hiring the most. A messy, hopeful counter-narrative to the AI-jobs apocalypse._

Source: https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-07-02

Audio: https://davids-ai-brief.netlify.app/audio/2026-07-02.mp3

## The One Idea

### The companies leaning hardest into AI are hiring more, not fewer, people.

The AI-and-jobs story has been dominated by fear, but the newest data cuts the other way. Ramp found high-AI-adoption firms grew headcount 10% over two years — and 12% at entry level — while low-adoption firms stayed flat. Box's survey echoes it. Meanwhile even a hard new benchmark shows AI completing just 16% of freelance tasks at professional quality. NLW's long-held optimism is getting numbers behind it — even as real, painful displacement in specific roles is still coming.

## The Other Side

Zitron's June 30 piece reads the AI-and-work story as a con running in both directions. Agents marketed as autonomous actually chain people to longer hours — he cites Bloomberg's account of engineers working until 4am alongside the bots they deploy, and a founder who says he has "never worked harder in my life." The replacement threat, meanwhile, is deliberate fear-marketing by labs whose models, he writes, "cost about as much as a person" yet can neither replace one nor turn a profit, with benchmarks "rigged in their favor." On his account, hiring data from AI-heavy firms would show only that the tools demand ever more human supervision.

Source: Ed Zitron, "The AI Industry Is Losing", 30 June 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-ai-industry-is-losing/

## The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether AI, at today's capability, complements human labour or substitutes for it. Both accept more than they let on: NLW's own headline benchmark has AI completing just 16 per cent of freelance tasks to professional standard, and Zitron insists models cannot replace a person — so both agree the substitution case is currently weak. The empirical question that will resolve is whether headcount at high-AI-adoption firms keeps growing as the tools mature; the observable that settles it is the next rounds of payroll data — Ramp and Revelio follow-ups, and the BLS tech-and-finance series — through 2027. The residue is pure worldview: whether higher output per worker is liberation or exploitation. NLW is vindicated if hiring growth persists and broadens beyond the subsidised early adopters; Zitron if it proves a spending artefact that reverses when the cheap money stops. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW sells daily optimism, Zitron sells bubble certainty — so the signal is their overlap: today's AI does a small fraction of real work. Watch the next Ramp/Revelio update against the 28,000-a-month tech job losses.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Entry-level hiring cuts both ways for the NHS

Ramp's data says high-AI-adoption firms grew entry-level headcount 12 per cent — selecting for people who use AI well. If NLW's right, practices and trusts that adopt scribes and triage tools early may end up hiring more AI-literate admin and clinical support staff, not fewer. If Zitron's right, those same tools mostly add supervision burden — every unverified AI note is checking work for a clinician, and the 4am engineers in his piece are a warning about what 'productivity' feels like from inside.

### 16 per cent is the medico-legal point

The Remote Labor Index finding — AI completes 16 per cent of freelance tasks to professional standard — maps neatly onto clinical documentation. The scribe drafts; the GP remains the quality gate and carries the medico-legal responsibility for the other 84 per cent. That is an argument for treating AI output as a junior's draft, never as a finished record — and for coding time to check it into the consultation, not out of it.

### Ford's graybeards are an NHS parable

Ford rehired 350 veteran engineers to fix AI tools trained on design documents alone — and topped the quality survey. The NHS analogue: experienced clinicians' judgement is the training signal these systems need, and letting senior GPs drift out of the workforce while automating on guidelines alone is a false economy. Retainer-style roles supervising and correcting AI may be a genuinely useful late-career job.

## NLW's Takes

### The Overton window on tribute deals is shifting

NLW frames the government stake and corporate contributions as a "strange new era of tributary capitalism." The window on these arrangements is clearly moving — and he steers clear of a hotter take to keep the show moving.

### The real story is how much Anthropic can see

NLW argues the takeaway isn't the anti-distillation effort — it's a reminder of the visibility Anthropic has into the work being done on its systems.

### 16% of tasks is not 16% of jobs

NLW argues the report has something for everyone: work quality is advancing fast, yet getting to a fully economically viable product remains hard. The gap between those two — 84% still needing a human — is where freelancers and firms can redesign their economics and deliverables.

### Optimistic long term — but real displacement is still coming

NLW is encouraged that both narrative and numbers now validate his jobs optimism, but warns some job categories will be wiped out and vulnerable populations near career's end will struggle to pivot. Crafting targeted policy for specific at-risk roles, he argues, is a far more tractable task than fighting an imagined AI job apocalypse.
