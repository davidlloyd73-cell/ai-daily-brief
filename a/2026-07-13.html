How the Escalating AI Wars Benefit You

Apple goes thermonuclear on OpenAI, Musk and Altman trade jabs, and a subsidy price war rages between the labs — but NLW's read is that all this escalating acrimony is, at least for now, a gift to the rest of us. The competition has moved beyond frontier models to the entire ecosystem, and nobody has a handle on what shifts next.

The One Idea

AI competition just got personal — and the chaos is good for you.

The AI race is no longer just about who has the best frontier model. It's about hardware, architecture, learning loops, and geopolitics, all colliding in a liminal in-between moment where nobody knows where value will accrue. That anxiety is driving public spats — Apple's lawsuit, the Musk-Altman feud — but it's also driving a subsidy price war between the labs. NLW's takeaway: take advantage of the competitive upheaval while it lasts, because a smart individual can benefit mightily.

The Other Side

Zitron's answer to the idea that the subsidy war is a gift is that the gift is the whole con. Subscription demand, he argues, only exists because labs sell what he calls '$40 for a dollar' — the very Semi Analysis figures NLW celebrates are, for him, proof there is no real business underneath, just venture capital funnelled through OpenAI and Anthropic to hyperscalers and NVIDIA. Demand is 'decidedly artificial', manufactured by media consent and free compute, and it will vanish the moment the discount does. His conclusion is blunt: when the bubble pops, 'no bailouts, no handouts' — an industry propped up by subsidy is unworthy of rescue and should be allowed to burn.

Source: Ed Zitron, “Let AI Burn”, 7 July 2026 (from 7 July’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is the subsidy — the gap between what a token costs to serve and what a subscriber pays. Neither disputes the facts: both cite Semi Analysis's finding that a $200 monthly plan can burn up to $14,000 of compute. The empirical question, which will resolve, is whether inference costs fall fast enough for today's prices to turn profitable before the capital funding the gap runs out; the observable that settles it is what happens to rate limits and subscription prices over the next year, and whether usage survives the change. The residue is pure worldview: is subsidised usage seeding durable demand or manufacturing fake demand? NLW is vindicated if costs collapse and users stay at honest prices; Zitron if usage retreats the moment the discount does. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW sells daily bullishness to builders, Zitron sells bubble certainty to sceptics — so the signal is where they agree despite opposite priors: today's prices are unsustainable as charged. Watch the first frontier lab to materially tighten limits or raise prices, and what its users do next.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Scribe pricing rides on subsidised compute — check your contract, not the demo

Ambient scribes and other GP-facing AI tools sit on top of frontier models currently sold well below cost. If NLW's right, inference keeps getting cheaper and today's per-licence prices hold or fall — fine to commit. If Zitron's right, the subsidy ends and suppliers either raise prices or quietly swap in weaker models. Practical hedge either way: avoid multi-year single-supplier lock-in, insist on price-review and exit clauses, and ask the supplier which model actually powers the product and what happens if it's repriced.

Nadella's 'you pay twice' applies to every corrected consultation note

The edition's sharpest enterprise idea — you pay for AI with money and again with your proprietary knowledge — lands directly on general practice. Every correction a GP makes to a scribe draft is training 'exhaust' that can leak value back to the model provider. Worth checking the data-processing agreement: does the supplier use your corrections to improve its models, and if so, on what terms? Both men would agree the NHS shouldn't be donating clinical expertise for free; they differ only on whether the resulting models will be worth anything.

Supplier turbulence is now the norm — plan for a vendor falling over

Apple suing OpenAI, labs poaching staff, subsidy wars, Zitron predicting collapse without bailout: whichever reading is right, the AI supply base is volatile in a way NHS procurement isn't used to. If Zitron's right, some suppliers to primary care will simply disappear when funding dries up; if NLW's right, the churn continues but from competition rather than collapse. Either way a practice depending on an AI tool for clinical documentation needs a tested fallback — the old Dictaphone question: what do we do the week the service goes off?

NLW’s Takes

The AI race is entering a phase where hardware, not just models, is the battleground

NLW wants to see OpenAI's response before making up his mind, but agrees the lawsuit signals the core theme: it's no longer just about models, it's about the entire ecosystem around them. The alleged IP theft still seems 'strange and desperate' in a way that doesn't comport with OpenAI's self-image.

If you think the frontier labs will just roll over, you're nuts

NLW pushes back on the assumption that leading labs won't participate in the cheaper-model shift. GPT-5.6's cheaper versions — Terra and Luna — are 'basically better than GLM performance for lower than GLM costs.' He also thinks the pace of change is wildly overstated: most firms are still trying to get employees to use Claude at all.

Take advantage of the competitive upheaval while it lasts

NLW's bottom line: the tectonic plates of AI competition are shifting, and nobody has a handle on what comes next — which is why the spats and acrimony are intensifying. But the subsidy extensions prove that, in the short term, this fierce competition is genuinely good for all of us.
