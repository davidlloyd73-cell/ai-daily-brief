# David's AI Daily Brief

**Wednesday 30 September 2026 · 23 min listen**

## The Most Important New AI Tools from OpenAI DevDay

_OpenAI crammed more than 20 launches into a single DevDay — Dots, the Decisions API, Space, GPT-6.1 Sol, sign in with ChatGPT, and a sleeper open-weight marketplace. None of it changes the big patterns of the past several months; all of it confirms them: cheaper good-enough models, persistent always-on agents, and work AI going multiplayer._

Source: https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-09-30

Audio: https://davids-ai-brief.netlify.app/audio/2026-09-30.mp3

## The One Idea

### DevDay didn't reveal a new direction — it confirmed the one we're already on.

OpenAI's biggest DevDay ever wasn't about something blisteringly new. Sol 6.1 and the Decisions API deepen the move to models cheap and good enough to be used for everything. Dots turns that into persistent, proactive agents that actually do everything. Plugin extensions and sign in with ChatGPT bring everything in and go everywhere. And Space confirms that the next generation of AI won't just be single-player — it's going multiplayer, natively. These are the trends that will reshape how we all use AI in the months to come.

## The Other Side

Zitron argues the AI trade has been 'dead money' for months, citing Fidelity's Jurien Timmer, who sees token spend and GPU rental rates flat to down. He says a large share of the chips sold since 2022 are sitting uninstalled, waiting for power, and that the visible demand is mostly two customers, OpenAI and Anthropic, funded by the very hyperscalers selling them compute. His claim is that there is no broad demand for AI compute at anything like the scale of the capital spending.

Source: Ed Zitron, "Dead Money", 29 September 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/dead-money/

## The Truth Beneath

The single variable both men orbit is real, paid, broad-based demand for AI compute. They agree on the facts that models are getting cheaper, that agents are running more continuously, and that OpenAI and Anthropic take an outsized share of capacity. The empirical question that will resolve is whether usage and revenue spread beyond a few labs and venture-funded startups, and the observable that settles it is whether enterprise token spend and GPU rental rates turn up, and whether diverse customers appear on cloud AI revenue lines. The worldview residue is whether cheap, ubiquitous intelligence creates new demand or simply lowers price. For Whittemore to be vindicated, falling prices must be met by far larger volumes from many buyers. For Zitron, volumes must stay concentrated and funding must tighten. Both are polemicists with incentives: Whittemore is bullish and anti-doom, Zitron is certain of a bubble and anti-hype. The signal is where they agree despite opposite priors, that concentration is real. Watch the next quarter's cloud disclosures for customer breadth.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### If Whittemore is right: cheap judgment models become routine infrastructure

OpenAI's Decisions API, a fast and cheap classifier, is the kind of tool a supplier could put behind inbox sorting, referral triage or coding. The practical rule from earlier editions holds: it can rank and flag, a clinician decides. Ask suppliers whether such a step sits upstream of the summary you are reviewing and whether it was validated separately.

### If Zitron is right: supplier risk is the issue, not capability

If demand is concentrated and funding tightens, the tools a practice depends on could change price, terms or vanish. NHS procurement should assume an AI scribe or triage product might be repriced or withdrawn, so ask about data export, contract exit and how clinical records stay usable if a supplier fails.

### What both positions share

Both accept the tools are getting cheaper. For a practice, that argues for short contracts and pilots rather than long commitments, whichever of them turns out to be right.

## NLW's Takes

### Judgment models are becoming a fundamental primitive

The Decisions API is a recognition that Jev represents more than a new model. LLMs were being rammed like square pegs into round holes for classification and judgment use cases — once you see it, a judgment model sitting alongside your generative model seems obvious in retrospect. Expect every frontier lab to ship some version of this very soon.

### Compute constraints are real, present, and permanent

The $200 Pro tier reopened with usage calculations netting out to a 50% reduction in API-cost terms — the best of a bad set of choices, per OpenAI, to avoid five-hour usage limits. Frontier models are running up against the walls of available compute, and new supply isn't coming online anywhere near the speed demand is growing. The upside: a big efficiency push that should net better, cheaper experiences — with bumps along the way.

### The sleeper hit: an open-weight model marketplace

Enterprises can now spend OpenAI credits on open-weight model inference from Base10 through the Responses API and Codex. This has big implications for OpenAI's moat — it protects them from open-source disruption while letting customers make large spending commitments knowing they can run a multi-model strategy. It's bigger than people are giving it credit for at first blush.

### DevDay confirmed the trends rather than breaking new ground

Sol 6.1 and the Decisions API deepen the era of models good enough and cheap enough to do everything without rationing. Dots turns that into persistent, proactive agents. Plugin extensions and sign in with ChatGPT mean bringing everything in and going everywhere. And Space confirms the next generation of AI will be team and multiplayer mode in a native way — even if that remains nascent so far.
