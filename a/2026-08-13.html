Grok 4.6 Shows How Fast Your AI Options Are Expanding

Grok 4.6 lands with benchmarks that put xAI back at the frontier table — the clearest sign yet that the model race has gone from a two-horse sprint to a crowded field of frontier labs, Chinese open-weight challengers, and dirt-cheap near-frontier options. Plus: Cognition eyes $40 billion, the neo-clouds can't build fast enough, and Washington rethinks open models.

The One Idea

The frontier is crowded again — and your AI options are expanding fast.

A year ago 'frontier model' meant OpenAI, Anthropic, or Google; a couple of months ago it basically meant the first two. Now Grok 4.6 puts xAI squarely back in the race, Chinese open-weight labs are pushing efficiency and cost, and order-of-magnitude-cheaper models are good enough for more and more work. Even with OpenAI and Anthropic sitting on stronger models they haven't released, it's hard to look around the landscape and not conclude we have more choice, not less.

The Other Side

NLW's lead is that the frontier has got crowded and cheap — Grok 4.6 at sixty per cent less per token, Chinese open-weight models at a fraction of the price, and near-frontier performance now good enough for most work. Zitron's nearest engagement with that argument runs the other way: the industry cannot afford for AI to get cheaper, and the moment buyers saw real prices they flinched. He notes that "costs have only ever increased", and that ideas like efficiency "run counter to the greater narrative" because cheaper inference means less compute sold. His evidence is procurement, not benchmarks: Uber exhausted its annual token budget in a quarter and capped staff at $1,500 a month, T-Mobile at $2,000, Brex at $500 a week for engineers and $5 for everyone else. On the held-back-models point he is blunt — the labs "do not have some super secret trick up their sleeves" — and he dismisses benchmark-led capability claims outright.

Source: Ed Zitron, “AI Is Slowing Down”, 8 June 2026 (from 8 June’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

Both men orbit one variable: whether a falling price per unit of capability is real, or a narrative the sellers cannot afford to honour. They agree on more than the framing suggests — that the most expensive model is not what most work needs, that benchmarks are a poor guide, and that buyers are economising. The empirical question is whether the cost of a useful task keeps falling once discounting ends; the observable is per-task cost on real workloads, and enterprise token budgets rising rather than being capped. The residue is worldview: Whittemore reads cheap challengers as competition working, Zitron as a business with no floor under its pricing. Whittemore is vindicated if cheap near-frontier models take real production volume and total spend still grows; Zitron if the cuts prove promotional and per-task costs drift up. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain — and the signal is what they concede despite opposite priors: nobody needs the top model. Watch whether the next Ramp index shows cheap models taking share, or spend simply falling.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

A crowded frontier is the first good news for NHS procurement in a while

Until now, anything AI-shaped that a practice or an ICB wanted came from one of two American suppliers, which is a poor position from which to negotiate anything — price, data residency, or continuity if the supplier is bought or changes terms. If NLW is right that there are now several credible frontier labs plus open-weight models a few months behind, then the scribe you use, and the triage or coding tools an ICB buys, become substitutable, and substitutable is what makes a contract safe. If Zitron is right and the cheap prices are promotional, the same reasoning cuts the other way: the sensible question to ask any supplier is not what it costs this year but what happens to the price when the funding round ends. Either way the practical rule is the same — do not build a workflow on a tool you could not swap out in a fortnight.

Cheaper models matter more than better ones for the work general practice actually does

The argument gaining ground on the show — that as the frontier advances, fewer people need the bleeding edge and need it less often — describes clinical administration precisely. Summarising a consultation, drafting a referral, tidying a discharge summary into a coded problem list: none of that needs a state-of-the-art model, and a near-frontier model at a tenth of the price will do it. That is the difference between AI documentation being a funded pilot and being simply part of the running cost of a surgery. Zitron's counterpoint deserves a hearing though: he cites companies capping staff token budgets after the first real bills arrived, which is exactly the shape of a practice discovering its scribe subscription was priced to acquire it rather than to keep it.

The Samsung number is the shape of the gain, not its size

Three months of verification work compressed into two days, and a second-year engineer completing a month-long task in a day, is the jagged frontier in one sentence: enormous gains on highly structured, highly repetitive work, and juniors suddenly performing well above their experience. Both halves have analogues in medicine, and the second is the one to think about. In a supervised training environment, output that outruns understanding is a governance problem before it is a productivity one — as with any registrar whose letters read better than their reasoning. If we start using these tools with trainees, the thing to assess is not the document produced but whether they can defend it.

NLW’s Takes

China's CapEx boom is running the US playbook on a 3-6 month delay

Tencent tripled AI infrastructure spend to $7.8 billion, flipped free cash flow negative, and reassured markets it could sell compute but chooses not to — uncannily echoing US hyperscaler narratives from Q1. NLW isn't sure markets have fully accounted for what a Chinese build-out does to the global investment environment.

The Fable 5 'flop' data has two big holes in it

First, selection bias: Ramp's numbers come from a spend-management product whose users are literally optimizing away from more-powerful-than-needed models. Second, and more damning: Fable 5 carries a 30-day data retention requirement for US government safety checks, and many employees simply aren't allowed to use it — a detail Ramp's own economist later amplified.

The leaders are holding back — and you still have more options than ever

Lurking behind everything: Anthropic and OpenAI both have more advanced models essentially ready, held back by government pressure, internal concern, or simply the absence of competitive urgency. Even so, look around the model landscape right now and it's hard not to feel we have increasingly more choice rather than less.
