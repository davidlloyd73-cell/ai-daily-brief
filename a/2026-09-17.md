## The One Idea

### The personal agent era may have finally arrived — and Meta's Muse is leading it.

For most of 2026, 'normal people aren't using AI agents' was uncontested — AI looked like a normal consumer technology and a wildly abnormal work technology. In the last month that flipped: computer use got good enough to skip the API plumbing, products like Muse, GrokBot, and Instinct nailed primitives like persistence and goal building, and genuine (not astroturfed) consumer devotion followed. With Muse at number two on the App Store, it may be time to check your priors — even if the rails underneath agents still need to be completely rebuilt.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Patients will arrive with agents before practices are ready for them

If personal agents like Muse really are breaking out with ordinary consumers, some patients will soon ask them to book appointments, chase prescriptions and fill in online consultation forms for them. The NHS App and practice online forms were designed for a human at the keyboard. Practices should expect agent-completed requests that are unusually fluent, possibly inaccurate about symptoms, and sent at volume. Check how your online consultation supplier would spot and handle them, and think about how identity and consent work when the requester is software acting for a patient.

### The ADHD and admin-backlog stories are a health story too

Several of the unprompted Muse testimonials are from people who could not keep up with life admin: bookings, claims, cancellations, forms. For some patients with ADHD, executive-function difficulties or caring responsibilities, that burden is part of the presenting problem. An agent that clears it could be genuinely helpful. It could also create new risks if it holds bank logins and acts on its own. It is worth being aware of when a patient mentions they now let an assistant manage their affairs, especially where capacity or safeguarding is in question.

### Continuous misalignment disclosure is the model for clinical AI incident reporting

OpenAI's new framework publishes misalignment reports as soon as they are observed, before they are fully explained, and lets any employee raise one. That is essentially the culture of a good significant event system, and it is what practices should expect from suppliers of AI tools used in care. Ask your scribe or triage vendor how you would hear about a model-level problem found at another site, how fast, and whether you can report back. The Yellow Card scheme covers medical devices, including software; AI incidents need to reach it too.

## NLW's Takes

### AI: a normal consumer technology, an extremely abnormal work technology

NLW's May framing of the year's core question: nothing in the consumer sphere has come remotely close to AI's disruption of work — leading him and others to wonder whether AI is primarily a business technology.

### This isn't astroturfing — the Muse devotion is spontaneous

NLW, who has a well-calibrated read on the difference between spontaneous accolades and coordinated campaigns, is clear: these are not folks funded by Mark Zuckerberg from Palo Alto. Investor Chao Wang calls Muse 'the next major inflection point in AI, with the last one being coding agents' — and one user deleted Claude entirely.

### One Claude, some pro-user reservations

NLW keeps different model settings for work versus personal tasks and hesitates to hand even model selection to the platform. But he's clearly in the minority: nearly every reaction to the simplification was very positive, especially from people onboarding non-technical users.

### Time to check your priors on personal agents

For months NLW came close to, then decided against, going deep on consumer agents — the fact that he finally did, in anything but a slow news week, is his own data point that something is shifting. His advice and his plan: actually go try Muse, GrokBot, or Instinct and see if they're more valuable than you think.
