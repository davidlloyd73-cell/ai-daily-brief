# David's AI Daily Brief

**Wednesday 04 November 2026 · 25 min listen**

## How the Midterm Results Change AI

_Democrats got their blue wave, taking the House and the Senate, and data centers, not AI in the abstract, were the AI issue on the ballot. The industry now faces Democratic governors in nine of the ten largest states, 41 local data center measures that all look set to pass, and a 2028 cycle that starts today. Does any of it actually change the direction AI policy was already heading?_

## The One Idea

### The blue wave didn't change AI's direction. It confirmed it.

Data centers were the AI issue voters cared about, and backing them was a vote loser in both parties. The new Democratic majorities and governors will bring hurdles: local moratoria, incremental federal rules on whistleblowers, testing, incident reporting and liability, and plenty of hearings. A comprehensive crackdown is unlikely with a president promising to be "Mr. Veto." The era when data center developers could ignore communities was already over. The space between "I don't hate data centers" and "I hate how they treat Texans" is where a deal can be found. NLW argues the build-out is the most winnable political issue he's ever seen, winnable for everyone, unless 2028 hopefuls decide that being virulently anti-AI pays.

## The Other Side

Zitron, writing two days later in a paid piece of which only the opening is free to read, welcomes the result and says the blue wave could burst the bubble. He argues the bubble's supposed inevitability rested on the assumption that the rich and powerful always get what they want, and that its backers never expected voters to say no. Nvidia's growth depends on an unimpeded build-out, he says, and without more capacity the labs cannot sell a story of endless growth; once that sets in, "everything begins to implode".

Source: Ed Zitron, "Premium: Fault Lines", 6 November 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/premium-fault-lines/

## The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether local consent merely raises the price of building data centres or actually stops them. Both accept as fact that voters punished candidates who backed local data centres, that ballot measures passed almost everywhere, and that developers spent years ignoring communities. The empirical question is whether new governors set conditions or impose lasting pauses; the observable is construction starts and permits in Texas and Ohio over the next three quarters. The residue is worldview: whether the build-out is useful infrastructure owed a fair bargain, or speculative excess that deserves stopping. For Whittemore to be vindicated, governors must set hard but achievable rules and building must continue. For Zitron, moratoria must stick, capacity stall and lenders withdraw. Both are polemicists with incentives: Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype. The signal is their agreement that the industry lost the public and cannot build without consent again. Watch what Texas's new governor does on taking office in January.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Consent first, not afterwards

Both sides agree the industry built first and consulted later, and was punished for it. The same applies on a small scale in a surgery. Bring patients and staff along before a scribe or triage tool goes live, explain what is recorded and why, and make opting out easy.

### American rules still shape our suppliers

NLW expects modest US rules on pre-release testing, incident reporting and liability. They do not bind the NHS, but most of our vendors build on American models. Clearer liability for model builders would help when a scribe gets something wrong; until then the clinician who signs the note carries it.

### Little direct clinical relevance, some price risk

This was an edition about US politics. The practical point is cost: if tougher local terms make data centres dearer, as NLW expects, or financing falters, as Zitron expects, AI service prices could move. Prefer shorter contracts with a clear exit and data export.

## NLW's Takes

### The likely wins: common-sense rules with cross-party support

NLW expects the low-hanging fruit: whistleblower protections for lab employees, codifying voluntary pre-release testing of frontier models, incident reporting rules, stronger liability for model builders, and possibly data sovereignty rules.

### Export controls are the wild card, and RSI is on the agenda

China policy doesn't split cleanly along party lines, so a Democratic win doesn't map neatly to more or fewer export controls. Expect debate over pausing, pacing, even prohibiting techniques like recursive self-improvement. Expect lots of executive hearings too, though few will matter unless Democrats go after Elon.

### More powerful AI means more rules, whoever won

NLW argues the shift toward more AI rules was inevitable even if Republicans had outperformed. It's partly a rejection of the last few years of Trump, but also a reaction to models getting more capable. For many people, ramping controls alongside capabilities is simply common sense.

### The era of ignoring local communities is over, and developers already knew it

The election confirmed what had been happening all year. Developers are already dropping NDAs, committing to pay their own way on infrastructure and electricity, and offering more community sweeteners. Some opponents will never accept any data center, but some new leaders will set hard but achievable rules that give communities a say.

### The data center build-out is the most winnable issue NLW has ever seen

Judged from first principles of fairness, the right relationship looks much more like no NDAs, community agency, and data centers paying their own way than the deal of a couple of years ago. Winnable, NLW stresses, not for one side but for everyone.

### The disaster scenario: moratoria spook Wall Street

Moratoria gain momentum and new governors move fast. Wall Street pulls funding, the build-out halts, neoclouds go bust, and a broader downturn follows, since the build-out is "pretty much all we've had going for us." Then 2028 gets fought over a burst bubble. NLW calls it possible, which is why developers will work overtime to find accommodation.

### Did anything change? Sort of, and the 2028 cycle starts now

Mostly, the night confirmed the direction things were already heading, and NLW sees plenty of political room for alignment. The risk: if the new crop of leaders decides that being virulently anti-AI and actually trying to stop it is a winning issue, "I could be eating those words."
