Trump Rails Against AI Slowdown "Hoax"

Forty-eight hours after Dario Amodei's pacing-the-frontier proposal, the negotiation got swallowed by politics: seven Truth Social posts declaring AI doom a 'hoax,' a surprise Oval Office call to Jensen Huang live on stage, and Obama publicly backing the slowdown. Plus: Terence Tao leads 25 Fields medalists in declaring the labs 'severely misaligned' with mathematics itself.

The One Idea

The slowdown debate just went fully partisan.

Dario Amodei's pacing proposal was supposed to open a negotiation between the labs and society over who controls the speed of AI. Instead, Trump spent Monday declaring the whole thing a hoax across seven Truth Social posts, Obama publicly endorsed the slowdown, Kamala Harris piled on, and the safety community watched its nightmare scenario unfold in real time: once AI safety belongs to a political tribe, half the country will oppose it simply because the other half supports it. The next thing to watch is whether this jumps from politics to policy.

The Other Side

NLW frames Monday as the moment the slowdown debate split along party lines: Trump calling AI doom a hoax, Huang calling the extinction odds made up, and Obama and Harris backing the pace-the-frontier proposal. Zitron, writing the day before, rejects the doom story too, but from the opposite side to Trump. In his account the Hugging Face attack was not a rogue AI but reckless human conduct on lab-owned compute, which he says should have ended in prosecution. The extinction talk, he argues, crowds out harms that have already happened: ChatGPT acting as a 'suicide coach', a murder-suicide, gas turbines polluting nearby communities, and the mass taking of creative work. He thinks the language of 'slowing down' concedes that the industry's path is right and only its speed is in question. And he calls a real slowdown financially impossible, since roughly $1.3 trillion of compute commitments and Anthropic's IPO both depend on acceleration. His remedy is dull on purpose: treat LLMs as 'normal software run in a reckless and dangerous manner' and apply the consumer-protection law that already exists, as former FTC chair Lina Khan has set out. 'If LLMs were a toy, they'd be taken off the shelves.'

Source: Ed Zitron, “AI Is Already In Dangerous Hands”, 14 September 2026 (from 14 September’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

Both men keep returning to one question: is AI risk about what the machines might one day do, or about what the companies are doing now? Both treat the extinction framing as unhelpful. NLW reports that Huang, Trump and many safety advocates now see it as a political liability, and Zitron calls it a sales narrative. Both accept that the Hugging Face incident happened, that the IPOs are still going ahead, and that nothing binding has yet been agreed. The question that will settle it is NLW's own next thing to watch, whether rhetoric becomes rules, sharpened by Zitron: will the first real action be a new AI-specific regime, or existing product and consumer law used against a named lab? The thing to look for is the first bill, enforcement action or state attorney-general case that names a frontier company. What remains is worldview. NLW sees a negotiation over who controls the pace. Zitron sees an industry that cannot slow down and a debate aimed at the wrong risk. NLW is vindicated if a pacing framework produces real oversight. Zitron is vindicated if the first consequences come from courts and regulators applying old law to present-day harms. Both are polemicists with incentives, NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype. The signal is that both think the extinction story is a distraction from the real fight. Watch the state attorneys general.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

When AI safety becomes tribal, clinical safety has to stay boring

The week's lesson is that once a risk debate belongs to a political side, half the country stops listening. The NHS already runs its own way of handling digital risk that has nothing to do with extinction or hoaxes: the DCB0129 and DCB0160 clinical safety standards, a named clinical safety officer, a hazard log and DTAC. Whatever Washington decides, a practice's question about an ambient scribe or triage tool stays the same: what can go wrong for a patient, how likely is it, and who notices. Keep that conversation technical and local, and keep it away from the national shouting match.

The graduate jobs data is a warning for the training pipeline

A Census Bureau study found US graduates entering highly AI-exposed fields saw a five-point fall in employment and 13% lower earnings from late 2022. NLW rightly says causation is unproven. Even so, the pattern of fewer junior roles as tools absorb entry-level tasks is worth watching in general practice. If scribes, letter drafting and coding support take the routine work that used to teach administrators, care navigators and new clinicians, practices should make sure trainees still learn the judgement underneath, not just how to check the machine's output.

Existing law already covers a lot of what worries patients

Zitron's argument that dangerous AI products should be judged under ordinary product and consumer law has a close UK parallel. An AI tool used in care that meets the definition of a medical device falls under MHRA regulation, and data protection law applies to it whatever the politics. For a practice, the practical point is that you do not need to wait for a new AI act to ask hard questions. Check whether a tool is registered where it should be, who the manufacturer is, and what their post-market surveillance looks like.

NLW’s Takes

Dario's letter was the opening salvo of a negotiation

Yesterday's argument holds: the pacing-the-frontier proposal plus the other lab leaders explicitly agreeing — even reposting Dario's article — marked a meaningful inflection point, a recognition that the labs will no longer be the sole arbiters of how fast new AI comes to market. What's being negotiated now is how that power gets distributed.

The next thing to watch: does this jump from politics to policy?

Seven Truth Social posts and an Obama endorsement later, the discourse has fully polarized — but so far it's all rhetoric. The signal that matters now is whether the hoax-versus-slowdown fight starts producing actual legislation and rules.
