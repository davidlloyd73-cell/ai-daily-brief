AI Agents Are Moving Into the Real World

Anthropic says Claude found a CRISPR-like enzyme system, Trump renames AI 'superintelligence' from the UN podium, and Altman and Amodei pitch the Security Council — but the main event is the evidence piling up that personal agents are real: Muse tops the App Store and jumps to glasses and a new always-on Charm device, while GrokBot moves into Teslas.

The One Idea

Personal agents are leaving the screen — and the 'nobody wants this' debate is finally meeting evidence.

The argument used to be theoretical: Silicon Valley says consumers want the schlep work automated; skeptics like Ben Thompson say people want to shop, complain, and be entertained — not be productive. Now the evidence is arriving. Muse sits at #1 on the App Store and blew away a New York Times reviewer, Meta is pushing it onto Ray-Bans and a new always-on Charm device, and GrokBot lives inside Teslas. The people actually trying these things keep coming away more impressed than they expected.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

The privacy question ambushing personal agents is the same one facing AI scribes

The New York Times reviewer who trialled Meta's Muse went in doubting whether personal agents were useful and came out doubting whether people would hand over enough personal data to make them useful. That is exactly the conversation practices are already having about ambient scribes: the tool works, so the live question becomes consent, where the recording and transcript sit, and who else's systems it touches. Worth revisiting your own scribe's data flow and patient information leaflet with that framing, rather than assuming usefulness has settled the privacy question.

Claude's 'CRISPR-like' enzyme discovery is a headline, not a finding

Anthropic ran 1,000 Claude agents for 21 hours and found a gene cluster that resembles CRISPR machinery, but geneticists quoted alongside the story say the hard part, working out what it actually does, hasn't been done, and some see IPO-timed hype in the framing. It is a useful reminder for anything AI-flavoured that reaches patients: a striking early-stage result is not a validated one, and 'AI discovered' is not a substitute for peer review before it changes what you tell someone about their health.

Agents that act without being asked need a stop button, same as the scribe

Meta's pitch for its Ray-Ban glasses and new always-on 'Charm' device is agents working proactively in the background while you talk, not waiting to be prompted. If ambient AI in the consultation room moves the same way, from transcribing what's said to quietly acting on it, the practice needs the same basic safeguard it should already have for the scribe: everyone knows how to switch it off, and what happens to the encounter if it fails mid-consultation.

NLW’s Takes

Two questions to watch as personal agents chase the mainstream

One: how much does the bridge into the physical world — glasses, cars, Charm — actually matter for adoption? Two: is the secret sauce the shift from reactive to proactive agents that bring value without being asked? For now, the winds are blowing in agents' favor: people trying these things keep coming away more impressed than they expected.
