## AI Companies Still Haven’t Delivered on Their Biggest Promises

_Dario Amodei almost never posts — but when a prominent investor claimed on All In that Anthropic's CEO believes his company might one day be the only private company left, Dario waded into X with a two-post defense of his views on regulation, messaging, and trust. His most quotable concession doubles as the episode's title: the most accurate criticism of AI companies is that they haven't yet delivered on their big promises. Plus: ZAI's GLM 5.3 squeezes near-frontier performance out of a mid-sized model, Anthropic keeps its best model for itself, and investors float a $2 trillion IPO._

## The One Idea

### AI's trust problem won't be fixed by messaging — only by delivering.

A podcast rumor that Dario Amodei thinks Anthropic could be the last private company standing dragged the famously offline CEO into the public square. His response reframed the whole debate: regulation isn't automatically regulatory capture, AI structurally concentrates power regardless of rules, and the public's distrust is a decades-old crisis that no glitzy campaign can fix. The only thing that will work, he says, is actually curing cancer — and the fairest criticism of AI companies, his own included, is that they haven't yet delivered on their big promises to benefit the world.

## The Other Side

Where the edition treats Anthropic's trust problem as a delivery problem — promises not yet kept, fixable by curing cancer rather than by better messaging — Zitron reads the same week's news as a financing problem being dressed up as a narrative one. He goes straight at the two-trillion-dollar whisper number, calling the investor leak to the FT and the follow-up Reuters piece a pre-IPO marketing campaign rather than reporting, and objects that Anthropic is being priced on revenue it might book in two years while its actual losses go unmentioned. His sharpest line is aimed at the reasoning, not the company: valuing a business on what it could become is, he says, "a euphemism for 'ignore your lying eyes'." He also notes the same Gavin Baker "only companies" claim the episode covers, and treats it as evidence of how unmoored the discourse has become. On his account both frontier labs are unprofitable at scale, and the promises Dario concedes are undelivered are the collateral on which the whole valuation rests.

Source: Ed Zitron, "What Happens If OpenAI Dies?", 18 August 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/what-happens-if-openai-dies/

## The Truth Beneath

Both men orbit the same variable: whether the frontier labs can convert their promises into money before the money runs out. They agree on more than the tone suggests — that the promises are so far undelivered, that Anthropic is growing fast, and that a two-trillion-dollar price implies profits nobody has yet shown. The empirical question is what an actual S-1 discloses, and the observable that settles it is audited revenue, gross margin and compute commitments in a filed prospectus rather than a leaked annualised run rate. Whittemore is vindicated if the filing shows real, diversified, improving-margin enterprise revenue and the medical results Dario promises in months arrive; Zitron if the numbers land closer to the losses he reconstructs and the IPO is delayed or repriced. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is where they agree despite opposite priors: annualised run rates are marketing, and delivery is still owed. Watch for the S-1 itself, and for whether those "early glimmers" in biology are published or merely announced.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### "Show, don't tell" is the same standard we apply to any new treatment

Dario's concession — that the fairest criticism is that the promises haven't been delivered, and that saying AI will cure cancer is a cliche while actually curing cancer is not — is simply the evidential standard general practice already uses. We do not adopt a drug because the manufacturer describes its mechanism beautifully; we wait for the endpoint. The counter from OpenAI's side of the argument is worth holding alongside it: pharma has delivered enormous gains in human health and remains among the least trusted industries, because trust turns on pricing, access, opacity and who holds the power, not on efficacy alone. That is a familiar shape in the NHS. An AI tool can work and still be resented if the practice cannot see what it does with the data or what it will cost next year.

### The best model is one you will never be offered — and that matters for procurement

Anthropic's own risk report describes an unreleased internal model scoring materially above the public frontier, with no plans to release it. Whatever one makes of the safety reasoning, the practical consequence for a surgery is that the capability you are being sold is a deliberately chosen subset of what exists, and it can be changed under you. If NLW is right that options are multiplying and getting cheaper, that is mostly good news: a scribe or triage tool becomes a commodity you can swap. If Zitron is right that the economics are unsustainable, the same fact cuts the other way — the model behind your workflow can be withdrawn, throttled or repriced without your consent. Either reading points to one contractual question: what happens to our records and our workflow if the model we depend on changes or disappears?

### A two-trillion-dollar valuation is a clinical governance issue, indirectly

It is tempting to skip the IPO stories as City noise, but the pricing of these companies determines the pressure they will be under to raise prices, and NHS primary care buys on the assumption that today's per-seat cost is roughly tomorrow's. If Zitron's reading is right and the numbers are being priced on a business that does not yet exist, the correction lands as a cost increase or a withdrawn free tier. If NLW's reading is right and revenue genuinely grows into the valuation, competition should hold prices down. Neither outcome is knowable now, so the sensible practice posture is the boring one: short contracts, exportable records, and a documented way of working for a fortnight without the tool.

## NLW's Takes

### No, this isn't an open-source Mythos-level cyber weapon

Despite the freakouts, being frontier-level at finding vulnerabilities is not the same as being frontier-level at exploiting them or autonomously executing attacks. ZAI is also taking a phased approach, testing with trusted partners before publishing the full weights.

### The gap between what we use and what the labs have is widening

Between Anthropic holding back Model 2 and the new paradigm of government involvement in US frontier releases, the distance between publicly available models and internal lab capabilities is wider than it's been in the past — important context for any debate about how far behind Chinese models really are.

### On messaging, Dario is just wrong

You can't claim to understand that social media clips the most negative parts and then do an endless string of interviews full of easily sound-bitable negative statistics. Public perception isn't shaped by a word count of positive versus negative essays — arguing otherwise shows a radical misunderstanding of the media environment a leader of this significance has to operate in. The trust gap is real and messaging alone can't fix it, but you still have to understand how messaging plays into it.

### A few hundred words on X might be Dario's best medium

Nothing got resolved, but debating what was actually said beats debating suppositions — and these posts prove there's value in participating in social media even for someone who hates it. Dario's 13,000-word essays and long interviews get strip-mined for negative soundbites; a few hundred words on X is much harder to take out of context, and the public gets to participate in a conversation whose stakes belong to everyone.
