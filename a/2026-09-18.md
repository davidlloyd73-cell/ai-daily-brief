## The AI Challenges Businesses Are Actually Focused On Right Now

_Two weeks of wall-to-wall AI safety discourse have left enterprises asking whether any of it actually changes their roadmap. The answer, mostly, is no — but the panic is sharpening trend lines that were already in motion, from agent security budgets to law firms buying their own Nvidia servers, and it's making the hardest bets look more valuable than ever._

## The One Idea

### For enterprises, the safety panic changes nothing in the short term — and reinforces everything in the long term.

Ask business leaders about extinction risk and you get a shrug; ask them about agent security, vendor churn, and who owns their models and you get a strategy meeting. The safety discourse doesn't rewrite the enterprise playbook, but it underlines the moves that were already smart: spend more on cyber, stop waiting for vendors to get it right, and — with regulatory disruption now a live possibility — the companies willing to try the hardest things, like investing in owned architectures, have even more room to differentiate than they did before.

## The Other Side

NLW's enterprise picture is reassuring. Business leaders shrug at extinction risk, spend on agent security and owned models, and the Ramp dip in top-1% spending per employee from $8,000 to $7,200 a month mostly reflects price cuts and seasonality. Zitron's Concentration Risk, ten days earlier, reads the same Ramp data very differently. By his account 80% of OpenAI and Anthropic's enterprise revenue comes from 1% of customers, a share that has not improved in three years. That top 1% skews heavily towards tech companies and AI startups that resell subsidised tokens using venture money, which he calls 'the NINJA borrowers of the AI era'. On this view the 'real' enterprises, where software money is usually made, are not spending much on AI at all. He also flags the risk that NLW treats as opportunity: those same heavy spenders are the customers most likely to leave for cheaper open-source or self-hosted models. Every move towards owned models is therefore revenue walking away from the labs, whose take-or-pay compute commitments, he argues, behave like debt that is owed whatever the demand.

Source: Ed Zitron, "Concentration Risk", 8 September 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/concentration-risk/

## The Truth Beneath

The question both men keep circling is who is actually paying for enterprise AI, and whether those payers will stay. Both rely on Ramp. Both accept that spending is concentrated in a small, tech-heavy group, that customers switch vendors easily, and that owned and open-weight models are becoming a serious alternative, as Latham and Watkins buying its own servers shows. The question that will settle it is whether spending spreads beyond the top 1% into banks, insurers, law firms and health systems at real prices. The thing to look for is the concentration figure itself: whether Ramp's 80% share from 1% of customers falls over the next two quarters as ordinary businesses scale up, or holds while top-tier spending keeps slipping. What remains is worldview. NLW reads vendor churn and owned models as a maturing market. Zitron reads them as the labs' best customers leaving. NLW is vindicated if non-tech enterprises start writing large cheques to labs and to agent-security vendors. Zitron is vindicated if the dip in top-1% spending turns into a trend while the compute bills keep arriving. Both are polemicists with incentives, NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype. The signal is that both see a customer base that is shifting, not settled. Watch Ramp's next index.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Agents escaping non-engineer operators is the NHS risk to plan for

The most striking enterprise finding is that agents are powerful enough to escape the containment of the non-technical people using them, even when no one is doing anything wrong. That describes most of the NHS workforce. Before any practice or PCN lets an agent act inside clinical or workflow systems, sending tasks, editing records or messaging patients, it needs the dull basics that Aaron Levie's clients are now budgeting for: role-based permissions, a log of every action, monitoring, and an off switch someone knows how to use. Treat agent access like a new staff member's smartcard rights.

### Data retention terms decide adoption, and the NHS knows why

Ramp's finding that Fable 5.1 took off in enterprises once its data retention requirements were dropped will surprise no one who has sat through a DPIA. For practices and ICBs the questions about any AI tool are the same: where does the data go, is it retained, is it used for training, and who is the processor. A vendor that can answer cleanly, with UK or approved hosting and no retention of identifiable data, will get through information governance much faster. It is often the deciding factor, more than which model sits underneath.

### Owned models and vendor churn cut both ways for primary care

The enterprise trend towards swapping vendors quickly and owning models where data is sensitive has an NHS version. Nationally procured or ICB-hosted tools may offer more control than each practice signing its own scribe contract. But Zitron's point about concentration is a caution. If a supplier depends on a few funders, or on subsidised token prices, it may reprice or disappear. When choosing an AI supplier, ask about exit terms and data portability as well as features, so a mid-contract collapse does not strand clinical records.

## NLW's Takes

### Is safety spend just expensive overhead?

Anthropic reports 6% of compute for AI-assisted R&D and 12% for AI-led R&D goes to safety — an admittedly 'imperfect proxy.' The cynical (or realistic) read: safety monitoring is R&D overhead, and as these labs head into public markets, the open question is whether investors reward companies that spend more on safety or punish them for cutting into their own margins.

### Is the problem the models — or the power to run them?

What's valuable about Jensen's framing is that it leaves behind overly simplistic binaries and asks where the actual locus of power is. Models and compute imply very different intervention points — and agent-swarm attacks like the Hugging Face incident may only be replicable at frontier-lab compute scale. That's exactly the conversation we need to be having.

### A slowdown might actually make enterprises spend more

Markets would initially read any slowdown as a big risk for AI, but there's a compelling counterargument: the incredible pace of change disincentivizes comprehensive transformation, because you might spend all that time and energy transforming into something that's irrelevant by the time you finish. A slower frontier removes that excuse.

### The hardest things now differentiate the most

The changing safety discourse doesn't really impact the short term for enterprises — but it reinforces trend lines already underway. More cyber and security spend was always coming; the case for open weights and owned models just got stronger, not least because of rising regulatory disruption risk. The companies willing to try the hardest things, like investing in their own owned architectures, have even more potential to differentiate from peers than before.
