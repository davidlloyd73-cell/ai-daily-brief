Agentic Loops for Knowledge Workers

A holiday-week special: NLW hands the mic to Nufar Gaspar for a webinar on the buzziest skill of the summer — loop and graph engineering — and how to drag it out of software engineering and into knowledge work. The core move: coders got verification for free; the rest of us have to manufacture the referee. If you can define a boring, checkable finish line, agents will run until the job is actually done — and when one loop isn't enough, you compose them into teams.

The One Idea

If you can't design a verifiable finish line, don't loop it — but you usually can.

Every agentic tool already runs a loop under the hood; the new skill is taking control of it. Coding made loops easy because verification is free — the code compiles or it doesn't. Knowledge work has no compiler, but the webinar's central move is that verification can be designed: boring, machine-checkable finish lines that let an agent run overnight until the work meets the bar. Master two things — knowing when a task deserves a loop, and defining the 'done when' — and then compose loops into graphs of agents only when one worker stops being enough.

The Other Side

Zitron's most recent long piece is about something wider — what he calls the hypernormalised state of the AI industry, borrowing Adam Curtis's phrase for a world where the powerful manage narratives rather than face reality — but it lands squarely on this edition's idea. He argues that commentators rationalise LLMs producing a mediocre-but-plausible attempt at text or code faster than a person as 'impressive', without asking whether impressive translates to useful. His sharper point is aimed at the loop-and-graph culture directly: models tell every user their idea is genius, and are very good at making you feel productive as you endlessly prompt an ever-growing system, creating, in his words, 'the appearance of productivity' without much value. He concedes some people have built automations that help them — 'good for you!' — but says that is nowhere near enough to justify a trillion-plus dollars, and that LLMs have improved in the last year without yielding a product you can describe in a sentence.

Source: Ed Zitron, “Hyperscale Normalization”, 1 September 2026 (from 1 September’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether an agent running unattended produces finished work or merely the feeling of it. Both accept as fact that every agentic tool already loops behind the scenes, that coding is where this works because the compiler is a free referee. The empirical question that will resolve is whether knowledge-work loops converge on outputs a manager would accept without rework; the observable is firms publishing before-and-after figures for looped tasks rather than token counts. The pure worldview residue is whether 'designing the referee' is a real skill or a treadmill of tinkering. NLW is vindicated if the April–May agentic flip shows up as measured output, not just spend. Zitron is vindicated if looped work keeps hitting the letter of its finish line while humans quietly redo it. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is where they agree: a loop's finish line and the work being good are different things. Watch for the first independent study of looped knowledge work measuring rework, not tokens.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

The referee already exists in general practice — it's called an audit standard

The edition's central move is that knowledge work only loops well when you invent a boring, checkable finish line. Primary care has been writing those for decades: QOF indicators, the prescribing safety searches in PINCER, the medicines-reconciliation checks after discharge. If NLW is right, these are precisely the tasks an agent could run overnight against the practice system until every flagged patient has a documented action, with a human signing off at the end. If Zitron is right, the loop will meet the letter of the search — every box ticked — while a partner quietly re-checks the ones that matter, and the practice has paid twice. The test is whether rework falls, not whether the task 'completes'.

Never loop the consultation, and be wary of looping the letter

The webinar's own rule is not to loop anything that turns on human judgement — hiring, strategy, executive communication — because autonomy has no taste of its own. In a surgery that list is longer: diagnosis, safeguarding, breaking bad news, the medical examiner's scrutiny of a death. AI scribes sit on the safe side because the clinician remains the referee of every note. The temptation will be to let a loop 'improve' referral letters or complaints responses until they pass some checklist; the sneaky failure the webinar names — done, but mediocre — is exactly the one an ICB complaints team would spot.

Model to the node, and mind the token bill

One practical line worth keeping: match the model to the task — cheap models for mechanical steps, strong ones where judgement lives — and pass contracts between steps rather than the whole conversation. For a practice or PCN, that translates into asking any vendor how their scribe or triage tool routes work internally, since a beautiful graph with a deep-research node can, in the speaker's words, burn millions of tokens without batting an eye, and someone will be sent that bill.

NLW’s Takes

A loop is a job; a graph is an organization

The framing the whole webinar hangs on, originally from NLW on the show: one loop is one worker running until done; a graph is loops composed into a team. The three-sentence version: your tools already loop behind the scenes, a concrete verifiable end goal makes them work until actually done, and when one agent isn't enough you compose loops into teams.

We're all piece by piece giving ourselves an MBA in agent management

NLW's closing frame: AI skills have shifted from useful new tools — how to prompt Midjourney — to fundamental work primitives. Big chunks of what we used to do ourselves are now jobs of managing agents. Don't expect to master advanced management in a single session: there are no experts at this, just people who have done it more.
