How to Navigate the Next Wave of AI Competition

OpenAI cutting Cursor off after the SpaceX acquisition looks like lab-war psychodrama — until you line it up with Windsurf, the xAI blocks, and Satya Nadella's reverse information paradox. The pattern is clear: the sovereignty conversation enterprises have been having about models now has to extend to harnesses too.

The One Idea

The lab wars have come for your harness.

OpenAI cutting off Cursor isn't a one-off act of pettiness — it's the established pattern of frontier-lab competition, from Anthropic's Windsurf cutoff to the xAI blocks. For enterprises, the lesson extends the open-weights conversation from cost to control, and from the model layer to the harness layer: if you don't want to be subject to the whims of the companies that control your models and your harnesses, you can't be reliant on any one company for either.

The Other Side

Zitron would say the harness wars are a sideshow to a much larger dependency problem: it isn't enterprises who are dangerously reliant on OpenAI, it's the entire AI economy. By his arithmetic, OpenAI lost $20.9bn in 2025 on $13.07bn of revenue, carries over $800bn in compute obligations through 2030, and must raise on the order of $100bn or more essentially every year to survive — sums he argues no combination of SoftBank, private credit or an IPO can supply. Its spending is load-bearing for hyperscaler cloud revenue, neoclouds and chipmakers alike, so the question of which lab cuts off which coding tool matters far less than whether the lab writing the cheques 'runs out of money at some point'. Worrying about your harness vendor's whims, on his view, is planning the deckchairs.

Source: Ed Zitron, “What Happens If OpenAI Dies?”, 18 August 2026 (from 18 August’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The single variable both men orbit is concentration risk — how dangerous it is to depend on one frontier lab. Both accept as fact that the labs cut off partners at will and that enterprises have organised themselves around a handful of providers. The empirical question that will resolve is whether OpenAI can keep funding its obligations: Zitron says it needs tens of billions within roughly three months, so watch whether a major raise or financing event lands by late November, and on what terms. The residue is pure worldview — whether the value being created ultimately justifies the spend. NLW is vindicated if enterprises that diversify models and harnesses ride out further cutoffs while the labs keep growing; Zitron if a funding crunch, not corporate pettiness, is what finally forces that diversification. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW sells strategic urgency, Zitron sells collapse — but note where they converge despite opposite priors: neither thinks you should depend on any one lab. That agreement is the signal. Next thing to watch: November 12th, when OpenAI's models go dark in Cursor, alongside any OpenAI raise announced before it.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

The harness lesson applies to clinical AI too

The scribe you dictate into is a harness; the model underneath is a vendor relationship you don't control. If NLW is right, the sensible question for any practice or ICB procuring ambient scribes is whether the supplier can swap its underlying model without disrupting service — ask that at renewal. If Zitron is right, the sharper question is whether the supplier's model provider will still exist on current terms in two years. Either way, the answer to both is the same clause in the contract: model portability and a documented fallback.

NHS procurement already knows this story

A GP who lived through fundholding, 111 and successive reorganisations will recognise the pattern: national dependence on a single supplier looks efficient until the supplier's incentives change. The frameworks now being drawn up for AI in the NHS should treat model-layer and harness-layer dependency as separate risks — a trust can own its workflow software and still be exposed if the intelligence inside it is rented from one lab. The unions-as-intermediaries story is worth noting too; the NHS equivalent — clinical staff bodies brokering between suppliers and frontline teams — barely exists yet for AI.

Cheaper tokens cut both ways for the NHS

The Jevons point — that an 80% price cut drove a 13.8-fold usage jump and a third of users stayed at full price — matters for NHS budgeting. If NLW is right, falling token prices make whole categories of admin automation viable for practices that couldn't justify them last year. If Zitron is right, today's prices are subsidised by unsustainable lab losses and will rise when the money runs short — so any business case built on current per-use costs should be stress-tested against a doubling.

NLW’s Takes

Unions are the intermediaries this debate has been missing

NLW's read: labor unions are uniquely suited to intercede between communities and the tech companies building data centers — deeply rooted in those communities while standing to benefit economically from the transformation. An extremely positive development that could bring calm and rationality the discussion has lacked.

If you don't have an open weights policy, go figure it out

This isn't about abandoning frontier closed models — even sophisticated users haven't moved most use cases over. But the ability to integrate and route certain tasks to lighter, cheaper, more controllable open-weights models is a key capability, and like any capability, it takes time to build. Start now.

Prediction: the open-models discourse becomes an open-harnesses discourse

DeepSeek's new harness is built around one core idea — "everything is a plugin": models, tools, skills, sessions, sandboxes, and orchestration all mixable and replaceable. The specific product matters less than the category: open harnesses are now a tool enterprises will have access to, and expect a lot more of this conversation.

Yell at OpenAI — but don't depend on the outcome

A highly balkanized world of models and harnesses is inherently worse for everyone, so market pressure to change the policy is worth applying. But the enterprise lesson stands regardless: if you don't want to be subject to the whims of the companies that control your models and harnesses, you can't be reliant on any one company for either.
