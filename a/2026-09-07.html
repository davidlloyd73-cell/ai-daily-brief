The Multiplayer AI Sprint: Build Your Team’s First Shared Agent

A Labor Day special: agents have spent 2026 transforming individual work, but the half of knowledge work that happens between people remains untouched. NLW makes the case that the frontier is shifting from single player to multiplayer AI — shared context, shared sessions, shared agents — and launches a free four-week sprint to help your team get there first.

The One Idea

The next frontier of agents is multiplayer: from individual leverage to team capability.

Agents have only been able to touch the roughly half of work we do alone — but the majority of knowledge work runs through team context. Every, Anthropic's Claude Tag, the OpenClaw 2.0 rebuild, and now Y Combinator's fall request for startups all point the same direction: agents are moving out of individual silos and into shared spaces, with team-owned context, visible work, and live participation. To help teams get ahead of the shift, the show is launching the Multiplayer AI Sprint — a free, four-week, self-directed program to inventory your team's AI usage, build shared context, map overlapping work, and ship your first shared agent.

The Other Side

Zitron's most recent long piece borrows Adam Curtis's idea of hypernormalisation: an industry and its fans constructing a simpler, reassuring story and managing appearances rather than facing what the numbers say. The shared context repository at the heart of NLW's sprint is, in his telling, exactly the trap: models that tell every user their idea is genius let people feel productive while endlessly prompting 'an ever-growing system (like a Wiki of your work)' that produces the look of productivity and very little value. He also points out that after three years and hundreds of billions of dollars there is no productivity payoff visible at national scale, and that the usual answer to 'what does AI change?' is still 'coding' plus a user count.

Source: Ed Zitron, “Hyperscale Normalization”, 1 September 2026 (from 1 September’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether collaborative context makes agents more useful or merely more visible. Both accept as fact that most knowledge work is coordination rather than solo creation, that Anthropic, OpenClaw and Y Combinator are all now building shared-session tools, and that the aggregate productivity statistics remain unmoved. The empirical question that will resolve is whether shared agents change team output, and the observable is Anthropic's own claim that nearly two thirds of its product team's code now comes from a shared agent: either that figure is reproduced by unrelated firms within a year or it stays an in-house anecdote. The worldview residue is whether 'work about work' is waste to be automated or the social fabric of a job. NLW is vindicated if outside teams report shipped work, not sessions opened; Zitron if shared repositories become a new place to tinker. Both are polemicists with incentives, one bullish and anti-doom, the other bubble-certain and anti-hype, and they agree the present-tense evidence is thin. Watch for a non-lab company publishing that sixty-five per cent figure.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

A practice is a multiplayer workplace, which cuts both ways

NLW's figures put most of the working day in coordination rather than solo work, and general practice fits: duty doctor handovers, tasks passed to reception, results workflows, the MDT. A shared agent that sits in the practice's channel, knows this week's rota, the local pathways and what was agreed at last week's meeting is a plausible thing to want. If NLW is right, that is where the next visible gain comes from, not from a faster scribe. If Zitron is right, building that shared 'wiki of the practice' becomes a new hobby for the two keen doctors and nobody else touches it, which is what happened to most intranets.

Shared context in the NHS means shared patient data, so score for permission first

The sprint's four-axis scoring, shared need, staleness cost, permission sensitivity and checkability, is sensible, but in a surgery the permission axis dominates. A team agent with access to the clinical record is a different beast from one that drafts the practice newsletter. Start with candidates that touch no identifiable data at all: rota, protocols, referral criteria, the practice handbook. Zitron's warning about the 'appearance of productivity' is fair for those too; the check is whether staff actually stop asking each other the same questions.

The evidence gap both men admit

The only hard number in this edition is Anthropic's own, that nearly two thirds of its product team's code now comes from a shared agent. Zitron notes that outside coding, and outside the labs, the evidence is anecdote and user counts, and there is no productivity signal in the national figures. For NHS reasoning that is the honest position: shared agents in clinical teams are an experiment, so treat any pilot as one, with a baseline measured before it starts.

NLW’s Takes

Agents have only been able to impact half of work

2026 is undisputedly the year of agents — but so far they've changed how we work only on an individual level. Most of us split work between solo and team contexts, and the best, most dynamic AI-using teams are about to shift from single player AI to multiplayer AI.

There are still no AI experts — just people who have practiced more

The pedagogy behind every AIDB program is simple: to learn how to use AI, you just have to use AI. The programs exist to provide a framework for actually going and doing the work.

Managing a fleet of agents is now part of being a knowledge worker

Individual agents aren't going away — everyone now gets to be a manager of an extensive team of agents that can spawn sub-agents. That's just part and parcel of being an effective knowledge worker. But it only covers the work we do in our own silos.

The next frontier of agent design is the shared spaces teams inhabit

That means team-owned context — one single repository shared across the team, not duplicated documents in everyone's folders. It means shared sessions, observable work, and live steering and handoffs, with multiple people giving input at the same time in the same session.

Squint at it and the direction is obvious

You'll still run entire fleets of individual agents — but that only represents one part of the work we do. It's only natural that agents come for the other part: the work that lives between us. Teams that work through this shift now will be positioned to lead as multiplayer AI tools come online.
