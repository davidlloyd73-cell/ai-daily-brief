Why the Data Center Fight Has Little to Do With AI

Texas hits pause, New York signs a moratorium, and 219 local bans pile up — but ten days of on-the-ground reporting from the Midwest suggests the data center revolt is less about AI itself than about NDAs, broken promises, and a world people feel is being imposed on them without consent. Plus: a White House safety-testing framework so secret even AI companies won't be told how it works, and SpaceX's first earnings as a public company.

The One Idea

The data center backlash is about agency, not AI.

For most communities fighting data centers, AI is a widget — useful for drafting an email, nowhere near essential like cars, energy, or housing. What they're actually fighting is a world being imposed on them: NDAs that gag their city councils, billion-dollar promises they don't believe, and the shadow of failed projects like Foxconn. It's not Skynet, it's the oligarchy — and until builders treat this as a democratic process rather than a business process, the backlash will keep recreating itself.

The Other Side

NLW's lead is that the revolt has little to do with AI: it is about agency, NDAs and broken promises, and it can be answered by builders engaging communities openly and paying generously for local buy-in. Zitron's argument cuts underneath that. He reads the same build-out as a debt-funded asset bubble, financed through special purpose vehicles and variable interest entities that keep hundreds of billions of pounds' worth of obligations off hyperscaler balance sheets, against Bloomberg's estimate of over five hundred billion dollars in outstanding AI data centre debt. On his numbers the capacity being planned needs roughly $435bn a year in compute revenue to make sense, while actual spend is nearer $100bn and comes overwhelmingly from OpenAI, Anthropic and the hyperscalers funding them. His conclusion is that data centre construction is "a debt-funded asset bubble with ruinous consequences" — which would make local distrust not a communications failure but a correct reading of the deal.

Source: Ed Zitron, “The Subprime Data Center Crisis”, 22 July 2026 (from 22 July’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

Both men orbit one variable: how much of the announced build-out will ever be built and paid for. They agree on more than the tone suggests — that ERCOT's 474-gigawatt queue is padded with speculative and duplicate filings, and that communities have sound historical reason to disbelieve job promises. The empirical question is attrition, and the observable that settles it is the conversion rate from interconnection request to energised, revenue-generating capacity, alongside the first restructuring or impairment of a data centre special purpose vehicle. The residue is pure worldview: whether local distrust is a communications failure that better engagement can repair, or an accurate reading of an unsound financial scheme. Whittemore is vindicated if projects that negotiate openly and pay generously get built and the promised benefits arrive; Zitron if a large share stalls and the debt is impaired. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is that both call the queue substantially fictional. Watch what Abbott's ERCOT audit publishes about withdrawals.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

The NDA problem has an NHS twin, and we have lived it

The finding that carries furthest from this edition is not about data centres at all: it is that city councils signed non-disclosure agreements, could not tell residents what was being built, and then lost the argument to rumour. One official's line — we could not get ahead of social media because we had signed an NDA — will be recognisable to anyone who has watched a service change land in a borough. Walk-in centre closures, PCN reconfigurations and estates decisions are routinely negotiated in confidence and announced as settled, and the confidentiality is usually defended as commercial necessity. The lesson is that secrecy does not buy calm, it just guarantees the vacuum gets filled by someone else. If we want patients to accept AI in the consulting room, the practice-level version of this is telling them plainly what the scribe is, before they hear it from a leaflet or a neighbour.

A safety framework nobody is allowed to read is not a safety framework

The White House scheme invites labs to submit frontier models for up to thirty days of pre-release government testing, but the criteria, the participants and the mechanics are all undisclosed — firms not in the room will not even be told the policy. Neil Chilson's objection, that secrecy invites abuse and rules should be written in public and in law, is the same objection a clinician should make about clinical AI assurance. In the UK we at least have named routes: MHRA registration for software as a medical device, NICE evaluation, DTAC. They are slow and imperfect, but they are readable, and a practice can point to them when a patient asks who checked this. Any assurance process we cannot show a patient is one we should not be relying on.

If Zitron is right the price rises; if NLW is right the supply grows — either way, do not build a rota on today's tariff

Zitron's case is that the data centre build-out is debt-funded against demand that does not exist, so the compute underneath our tools is currently subsidised and will have to reprice upward. NLW's case is that the constraint is political rather than financial, and that projects which earn community consent will get built, keeping capacity growing. A surgery cannot arbitrate that. But both readings say the same thing about procurement: do not commit to a workflow whose viability depends on today's per-seat scribe price holding for three years. Ask for the notice period, the price-review clause, where the audio and transcript are stored, and whether the record exports in a form we can keep. Dull questions, but they are the ones that survive either outcome.

NLW’s Takes

The backlash isn't about AI — it's about agency

People feel unable to control their lives, skeptical of anyone in power, and no longer believe technology is designed to serve them rather than the oligarchs who build it. Data centers are both the last straw and something tangible to fight. Builders can't control that context — but they do control how they engage with communities, and they are doing an absolutely abysmal job.

Winning communities over is now mission critical — and it's on the builders

You cannot just yell at people for having conspiracy theories or dismiss their concerns. The skepticism is legitimate and rooted in what communities have seen and experienced — and when the core problem is people not feeling heard, making them feel heard comes before any argument about substance.

'It's inevitable' and 'China will' are not acceptable answers

The only acceptable answer to why AI — and by extension data centers — gets to exist is an explanation of how more data centers improve people's lives as they live them. If that case isn't made loudly and convincingly, nothing else in the debate matters in the slightest.

Data center building is now a democratic process, not a business process

Every project must earn local support on its actual merits, negotiated fully in the open — the days of backroom deals are over, and it doesn't matter that transparency is inefficient. Friction, slowness, and back-and-forth aren't byproducts of democratic process; they're structurally integral to it — they're what happens when people are actually being heard.

Community benefit is now core infrastructure — budget for it

The mindset has flipped 180 degrees: from expecting tax breaks for showing up, to spending real money on town buy-in. Covering their own electricity cost increases is the floor — data centers will need to be extremely generous, foot bills beyond their own, and dramatically increase the budget share for local incentives. If that breaks the bank for these projects, you need to get out of show business.

This is a failure of imagination, not an impossible problem

Raising your voice against things that affect your life is quintessentially American — the messiness is democracy in action. Some communities will never work for data centers, but many more win-wins are available where this infrastructure becomes a valued part of a community's next phase. The failure so far is one of understanding and imagination, and both can be fixed.
