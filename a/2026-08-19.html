The AI Backlash Is Getting Stupider. But Also Smarter.

A beer brand is selling urine-mailing as anti-data-center catharsis and a centrist governor who courted $20 billion from Amazon is now calling developers bullies — yet between Shapiro's criteria-based executive order and OpenAI's voluntary training pause, the anti-AI conversation may finally be producing something to actually negotiate with.

▶ Listen — today’s brief (4 min)

The One Idea

The AI backlash is getting dumber and more productive at the same time.

The memes are peaking — a viral ad about mailing urine to data centers, campaign spots branding opponents 'Data Center David,' and polls showing Americans would rather live near a nuclear plant than an AI data center. But look past the rhetoric: Shapiro chose a strict executive order with specific, meetable criteria over a blanket moratorium, and OpenAI voluntarily paused frontier training over a named, concrete risk. Rules that can be debated beat bans that can't — and that's a thin thread of opportunity worth grabbing.

The Other Side

Zitron is not writing about politics at all, which is rather the point: his most recent substantial piece argues that the thing Pennsylvania and Wisconsin are busy negotiating over may not get built. His case is that essentially all the demand sits with two loss-making labs, that stripping OpenAI out of Microsoft's numbers leaves roughly $10.33bn of genuine third-party AI revenue, and that on the best reading the whole industry has about $110 billion of annual demand against 190 gigawatts of planned capacity needing trillions to fill. "If Microsoft doesn't have the demand, nobody has the demand," he writes. On that account the backlash is arguing about the siting of buildings whose financing, not their permits, is the binding constraint.

Source: Ed Zitron, “Don't Look Up”, 11 August 2026 (from 11 August’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The single variable both men orbit is whether the announced data-centre buildout actually gets built and paid for. Both accept as fact that construction plans have run far ahead of what third parties are currently paying for, and that the opposition is now genuinely bipartisan. The empirical question that will resolve is whether the gigawatts in planning convert into energised, revenue-bearing capacity, and the observable that settles it is the share of announced projects reaching commissioning on schedule, and who signs the offtake. The residue is pure worldview: Whittemore treats the buildout as a fact to be governed well, Zitron as a financing structure that collapses before governance matters. Whittemore is vindicated if criteria-based orders like Shapiro's are met and projects proceed under them; Zitron if quiet cancellations outrun the permits. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is where they agree despite opposite priors: almost nobody outside the two labs is paying for this yet. Watch cancellations against permit grants.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

The NDA point is the one that translates directly to NHS procurement

NLW's strongest line is that banning state-agency non-disclosure agreements matters more than any of the environmental clauses, because what people actually resent is having no agency while something large is decided around them. That is a familiar shape in the NHS. Practices and PCNs are routinely asked to adopt AI tools — scribes, triage front-ends, coding assistants — under commercial terms nobody at practice level ever sees, and often without a published statement of what the model does with the audio, where it is processed, or who has audited it. If a US governor can make transparency a condition of doing business, an ICB can reasonably ask a supplier to publish its DPIA summary, its retention period and its subprocessor list before a single consultation is recorded. Worth asking at the next practice meeting who has actually read the terms for the scribe we use.

OpenAI pausing training over a named risk is a better model than the vague reassurance we get from health-tech vendors

The detail that matters clinically is not the pause itself but its structure: a specific triggering incident, a named threshold, and a stated proportion of compute spent on monitoring. Compare that with the assurance a practice typically receives about a clinical AI product, which is a marketing claim and a CE or UKCA mark. The transferable question for any tool touching patients is not "is it safe" but "what would make you stop, who decides, and how would we hear about it?" If a scribe's transcription accuracy degraded after a model update, would the supplier tell us, and would we notice first? Most of us would not. A named, published rollback trigger is a fair thing to ask of a vendor, and it costs them nothing if they already have one.

Public trust in AI is now a clinical variable, not a policy abstraction

A poll in which more people object to an AI data centre than to a nuclear power station is not really about kilowatts. If a substantial minority of the public do not see AI as progress, some of that arrives in the consulting room — as a patient declining consent for the scribe, or quietly doubting a letter they suspect was machine-drafted. If Zitron is right that the sector is financially unsound, that scepticism is well founded and we should be cautious about building workflows that assume the tool persists. If NLW is right that the constructive path is transparency and negotiated rules, then the same applies at our scale: say plainly at the start of the consultation what is recording, why, and that they may decline. It takes eight seconds and it is the whole of the trust argument in miniature.

NLW’s Takes

The Journal's 'tepid growth' story ignores the seven weeks since the quarter ended

The Wall Street Journal reported OpenAI's Q2 revenue of $6.7 billion (18% growth) and widening operating losses — factually accurate, but it's mid-August, and OpenAI executives have been publicly sharing numbers since: Greg Brockman told CNBC July revenue grew 20% month over month. Leaving out on-the-record data only serves to reinforce a narrative — 'if you ever wonder why people have trust issues when it comes to mainstream media, it's crap like this.'

A company that might 'out-compete everyone' shouldn't get the standard founder-control pass

Founder super-voting shares are common in tech — but Anthropic, by its own framing, is building systems powerful enough to shape the trajectory of society, and reports say its founder believes it could be the only private company left standing. That's a scenario where people are going to want shareholders and the public to have more control over its decisions, not less.

The backlash is getting more meme-driven — and more workable — at once

The anti-data-center movement is undeniably getting dumber, or at least more performative. But the latest shifts — criteria-based rules instead of bans, a lab voluntarily pausing over a named risk — suggest there may be more room for understanding and collaboration going forward than there has been up to now.

The NDA ban is the most important piece

What comes through in data-center reporting isn't just blanket AI opposition — it's people feeling they have no agency as the world changes around them, and NDAs are the living embodiment of that. I don't care if it makes it harder to do business: the only way to start rebuilding trust is to have these dealings happen out in the open with full transparency.

I'll take rules that can be debated over a blanket ban, a hundred times out of a hundred

Moratoriums are gaining popularity precisely because they're blunt: if the fear is change itself, nothing satisfies like declaring change isn't allowed to happen — but emotional satisfaction doesn't make good policy. The Kelce ad probably says more about the average person than the nuance of Shapiro's EO does, but in a strict-yet-negotiable order there's a thin thread of opportunity, and you better believe I'm gonna grab it.
