The New Enterprise Battle Over Who Owns the Model

Thinking Machines ships its first model — and it's the availability, not the benchmarks, that matters. Inkling is a base model built for fine-tuning, and it lands right as Microsoft trains its salesforce to attack the frontier labs on data trust. The new enterprise fight isn't which model you rent — it's who owns the one you customize.

Source: https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-07-16

▶ Listen — today’s brief (3 min)

The One Idea

The next enterprise battle is over who owns the model you fine-tune.

Thinking Machines released Inkling — a mediocre-on-benchmarks but strategically pointed open-weights model designed to be the base for its Tinker fine-tuning platform. It arrives the same week Microsoft trains its salesforce to argue that companies shouldn't trust OpenAI or Anthropic with their data. Two things converge: enterprises increasingly want cost control and data sovereignty, and multiple labs are racing to sell fine-tuning as the answer. Whether closed (Microsoft Frontier Tuning) or open (Inkling + Tinker), the question is no longer just which frontier model to rent — it's how much independence you want over the model itself. The bitter lesson may still win, but the experimental period is real and there's alpha for nimble teams.

The Other Side

Zitron's case cuts underneath the whole model-ownership debate: to him it is a rounding error beside the fact that enterprise AI has no measurable return. He argues the boom rests not on ROI but on "vibes" and a "cult-like psychosis," and that no architectural choice — open weights, fine-tuning, closed tuning — changes the economics, because "without OpenAI, the AI industry doesn't exist." He notes API costs already "became a 'huge issue'" for enterprise customers, and that four years in there is still no agreed accounting of what these models actually cost to run. He would read Inkling and Microsoft Frontier Tuning not as sovereignty on offer but as fresh ways to keep spending on something that has not shown a return.

Source: Ed Zitron, “The OpenAI Bubble”, 15 July 2026 (from 15 July’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The single variable both men orbit is enterprise AI economics — whether the cost of running these models is ever justified by the value enterprises get back. Both accept the same facts: token costs are large and real, and the unit economics are genuinely murky. The empirical question that will resolve is whether enterprises running fine-tuned or open-weights stacks show durable, auditable ROI; the observable that settles it is published enterprise unit economics and renewal data — does cost-per-outcome fall and does spend keep being renewed? The residue is pure worldview: NLW sees an experimental period with alpha for nimble teams, Zitron sees a capital misallocation heading for collapse. NLW is vindicated if fine-tuning lowers cost-per-outcome and enterprises keep paying and expanding; Zitron if spend keeps climbing while returns don't and a major player runs out of money. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW bullish and anti-doom to an audience of buyers, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype to subscribers who reward it — so the signal is where they agree despite opposite priors: enterprise token cost is a real, unsolved problem. Watch whether any enterprise publishes hard fine-tuning ROI numbers, or whether the bills just keep rising.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

"Who owns the model you fine-tune" has an NHS translation: data sovereignty

The edition's core fight — rent a frontier model, or run and customise your own — lands squarely on the question every practice faces with an ambient scribe: where does the consultation data go, and who can train on it? An open-weights model you host, or a supplier who fine-tunes on your data but keeps it on your infrastructure, is the commercial version of what information governance already demands. If NLW's right, this competitive pressure gives buyers real leverage to insist on models that stay inside the data controller's walls. If Zitron's right, none of it is worth the spend regardless — so the safe move is unchanged: demand a clear data-processing agreement and confirm nothing leaves the practice unbidden, whoever's economics win.

Beware the 'fine-tuning is the answer' pitch in health IT

Two of the edition's own voices — Simon Smith and Zitron — warn that fine-tuning is far more effort and ongoing cost than vendors admit, needing constant curation as edge cases and model updates arrive. That caution matters for any practice or ICB tempted by a bespoke, 'trained-on-our-data' clinical tool. If Zitron's right, a big general model plus good prompting and local context often beats a hard-won custom model as token costs fall. If NLW's right, there's still real value for well-resourced teams — but general practice rarely has the data-engineering capacity to maintain a fine-tune, so the honest default is a well-governed general model, not a custom one you cannot keep current.

The Microsoft-attacks-rivals story is a reminder to judge tools on evidence, not sales scripts

Bloomberg's report that Microsoft is training salespeople to tell customers a rival model is "slower and less accurate" while sidestepping overall quality is a clean illustration of vendor incentive at work. In the surgery the parallel is exact: scribe and triage-tool claims should be checked against independent testing and your own audit, not the supplier's comparison slide. Whichever side of the bubble argument you take, the discipline is the same — measure accuracy and time saved on your own consultations before trusting anyone's marketing.

NLW’s Takes

The pitch's subtext: don't trust OpenAI or Anthropic with your data

NLW reads Microsoft's move as aligning with Nadella's argument that the frontier labs have an incentive to build competing services — and as a foot in the door for its own models before upselling customer-specific fine-tuning via Microsoft Frontier Tuning.

Why "American and non-distilled" could suddenly matter to enterprises

NLW's read: as enterprises pay more attention to open-weights models, the fact that Inkling is an American model not primarily distilled from a closed lab becomes meaningful — especially once lawyers and risk teams weigh in on data sovereignty.

Microsoft's fine-tuning still requires trusting Microsoft

NLW notes Microsoft Frontier Tuning on MAI models rides on decades of built-up enterprise trust — an easier leap than trusting OpenAI or Anthropic — but it still looks very different from an open-weights option like Inkling that offers real independence and sovereignty.

Recognizing the problem doesn't mean today's answers are right

NLW cautions that acknowledging real challenges in data sovereignty and token cost doesn't mean Microsoft Frontier Tuning or Tinker is the ultimate solution. The flip side: as an open-weights model, Inkling addresses both the cost and sovereignty sides at once, whereas a closed fine-tuning solution may be caught in between.

Enterprises' choice has exploded far beyond "OpenAI or Anthropic"

NLW argues the decision now spans models, the harnesses they live in, and complex multi-model architectures including fine-tuning approaches. Not every flower will bloom, and the juggernauts may absorb the best ideas — but the experimental period will shape the eventual solutions, with real alpha for nimble teams.

You don't have to sign up — but you can't ignore this

NLW's bottom line for buyers: no need to rush into Tinker or Microsoft Frontier Tuning or switch everything to OpenRouter, but if you make enterprise buying decisions and aren't at least experimenting with or closely watching these fine-tuning and open-weights options, you're missing real opportunity.
