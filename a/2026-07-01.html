Fable is Back: Here's What You Should Try First

Two threads today: the industry's frantic hunt for cheaper inference — from OpenAI's secret 50% trick to Base44's own model — and the return of Fable 5 after 19 days offline. NLW closes with a practical guide to what you should actually try Fable on first.

Source: https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-07-01

▶ Listen — today’s brief (3 min)

The One Idea

Fable 5 is back — but the real story is what you do with it in the one free week.

After 19 days offline, Anthropic's Fable 5 returns for all global users, with 50% of weekly usage subsidized until July 7th. The conventional wisdom says save it for your hardest technical problems. NLW pushes back: from first-hand use, Fable is dramatically better at strategic thinking (it holds its ground under pushback instead of caving) and at rubric-driven writing (fewer AI-isms, better instruction following). Meanwhile the entire industry is racing to cut inference costs — the connective tissue behind OpenAI's secret technique, Base44's own model, and the shift toward open-weight deployment.

The Other Side

NLW reads the wave of inference cost cuts — OpenAI's undisclosed 50 percent trick, founders reporting 75 percent savings overnight, Base44 rolling its own model — as the industry engineering its way out of the compute crunch, a bullish sign. Zitron argues the exact opposite: nobody has ever shown the labs are actually profitable on inference, and cheaper tokens do not fix that. He notes Anthropic's own March affidavit implies it spent roughly three dollars of compute for every dollar of revenue, that Coatue found 85 percent of its 2025 revenue was API calls yet it still loses money, and that subscriptions let users "burn between $8 and $13.50 of every dollar." His conclusion: "If the margins were good, they'd be sharing the margins." Falling inference costs, to him, are a race to subsidise demand, not a path to sustainability.

Source: Ed Zitron, “AI Is Too Expensive”, 19 May 2026 (from 19 May’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

Both men orbit one variable: whether the cost of running these models can fall to genuinely unsubsidised profitability, or whether cheaper inference is just subsidy in a new suit. Both accept that token prices are collapsing fast and that the leading labs still post enormous losses. The empirical question is whether falling unit costs actually reach positive gross margin on real, paid usage; the observable that settles it is an audited inference margin — cost per token served against price charged — disclosed rather than asserted. The residue is worldview: NLW reads a 50 percent efficiency trick as engineering escaping the crunch, Zitron reads the same figure as proof they must keep cutting because the economics never worked. NLW is vindicated if a lab publishes real inference profit; Zitron if capex and compute deals keep outrunning it. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain with a paid newsletter — and the signal is where they agree: prices are falling. Watch whether any lab discloses an audited inference margin rather than a run rate.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Cheaper inference could quietly cut the running cost of your AI scribe

The edition's lead is that inference costs are dropping fast — OpenAI claims a 50 percent efficiency gain, and several founders reported cutting their AI spend by more than 75 percent in a day with no loss of quality. If NLW is right that this is real engineering progress, the ambient scribes and letter-drafting tools a surgery already uses should get cheaper or more capable at the same price over the next year. If Zitron is right that the labs are simply subsidising tokens they still lose money on, today's low prices are a customer-acquisition loss-leader that can be withdrawn — so a practice should treat a very cheap AI vendor as a flight risk, not a permanent saving, and keep the contract terms and an exit plan in view.

'Fall back to a smaller model' is now a clinical-safety question, not just a billing one

A recurring theme is models being quietly downgraded for routine or low-engagement use — OpenAI testing its cheaper technique on its least-active users, and Anthropic's note that some routine tasks 'fall back' to a smaller model. For a scribe summarising a consultation, the practical question is whether you can tell which model actually produced a given note, and whether quality is being silently traded for cost. When procuring, ask the vendor to state which model transcribes and summarises, whether that can change without notice, and how the tool flags low-confidence output — because a summary that drops a red-flag symptom is a safety issue regardless of who is subsidising the compute.

Own-model and open-weight trend may favour on-premise NHS deployment

Base44 built its own fine-tuned model to control cost and latency, and AWS noted enterprises shifting toward open-weight models. If that trend holds, it strengthens the case that clinically sensitive tools could eventually run on models hosted within NHS or UK data boundaries rather than sending consultation audio to a US frontier lab — relevant for information-governance and DPIA discussions at PCN level, though nothing here changes the current position that most scribes still call an external frontier model.

NLW’s Takes

There's still no free lunch on inference

NLW's caution: this is likely a smaller breakthrough than the headline suggests, and it's being tested on OpenAI's least-engaged users — which could be a reasonable first step or a hint of quality-degradation risk. Don't treat it as a silver bullet for the compute crunch.

Applaud the direction, but go a lot harder than half-price

NLW is glad data center operators are starting to cut communities into the benefits — but says making residents become customers to get a discount is far too small a gesture. Still, encouraging signs that these relationships are being rethought.

We're living with a framework that requires heavy judgment

NLW's read: there's a semblance of a practical framework now, but a lot of subjectivity goes into assessing risks. The best-case outcome is an efficient process with faster review for incremental updates — a bad outcome would be every capable release triggering the same weeks-long review and slowing the pace of breakthroughs.

Fable 5 blows the field away on strategic thinking

NLW's first-hand experience: unlike GPT-5.5 and Opus 4.8, which cave immediately under pushback and over-interpret instructions, Fable 5 accepts part of a critique while holding its ground on other parts — behavior he's never seen from another model. And it doesn't burn many tokens, so it won't chew through the 50% usage limit.

Fable is far better at rubric-driven writing

Contrary to Every's vibe check, NLW found Fable 5 much better at instruction-following, with fewer AI-isms and less try-hard prose. His suspicion: when you have a clear rubric or examples of good writing, Fable meets that standard far better than prior models — even if it's not necessarily better at blank-page writing.
