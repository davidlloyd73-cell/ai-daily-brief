## 6 Questions Every Enterprise Has to Answer About AI

_Sam Altman lands in Washington into a far messier conversation than the one he planned for — hacked models, open-weights fights, and a deceleration petition — while NLW brings back six questions from the KPMG symposium that capture how completely the enterprise AI conversation has flipped from 'if' to 'how' in a single year._

## The One Idea

### Enterprise AI has crossed from 'if' to 'how' — and the questions have gotten much harder.

A year ago enterprises were still debating whether agents were real and how to prove ROI. Now the paradigm shift has happened, and the questions are foundational ones organizations will spend the next half-decade answering: how to redesign for agents, how to think in architectures instead of models, how to provision and observe token costs, how to actually train non-technical people, how agents reshape what you sell, and how to build for constant change. Almost none have answers yet — but they're finally the right questions.

## The Other Side

Zitron's answer to the enterprise-maturity story is that it is the wrong conversation. He argues that debating what AI does to productivity and jobs distracts from the arithmetic: hundreds of billions of dollars of committed spend against roughly $110 billion of trailing twelve-month revenue across the entire industry, OpenAI and Anthropic included. His central claim is that the longer the bubble inflates the harder the economics get — building and running infrastructure keeps getting more expensive, so hyperscalers and neoclouds must eventually charge considerably more for compute, and the only two customers large enough to buy it are the two that cannot pay. He calls large language models "a definitively niche technology", and on that reading enterprise arguments about token budgets and observability are haggling over a price that has not yet been set honestly.

Source: Ed Zitron, "Premium: AI Is Getting Way Too Expensive", 31 July 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/premium-ai-is-getting-way-too-expensive/

## The Truth Beneath

Both men are circling one variable: the true unit cost of inference relative to what buyers will actually pay for it. They agree on more than either would admit — enterprise token spend is rising fast, budgets written before the agentic era have been blown, and almost nobody can yet see what their own consumption costs. The empirical question that will resolve is whether inference carries a positive and improving gross margin once depreciation on this capex cycle lands; the observable that settles it is audited lab cost-of-revenue alongside hyperscaler AI segment margins. The residue is pure worldview: whether soaring spend is demand or subsidy. NLW is vindicated if enterprises renew and raise budgets at unsubsidised prices; Zitron is vindicated if renewals shrink the moment prices carry their real cost. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — so the signal is their shared conclusion that cost, not capability, is now the central enterprise question. Watch the first full year of renewals priced to cover depreciation.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### Priced in tokens, not seats — which is a budgeting problem, not a technology one

The single most useful thing in this edition for anyone signing an AI contract is the observation that AI spend behaves less like software and more like labour: it is metered by use rather than by headcount. A practice that budgets an ambient scribe at so much per clinician per month is buying on the old model, and the vendors know the old model does not cover their costs. If NLW is right that consumption is the real unit, expect scribe and triage products to drift towards per-consultation or tiered-usage pricing as contracts renew. If Zitron is right that compute has to be repriced upwards, expect the same drift but faster and less gently. Either way the practical step is the same and it is cheap: find out now what your current tool actually costs per consultation, and ask the supplier in writing what happens to the price at renewal.

### The enablement bill is the part the NHS will underfund

NLW's own bully-pulpit point is that when AI was only about prompting you could skimp on training, but once the job becomes supervising something that acts on your behalf, training stops being optional. That is exactly where general practice sits. A scribe is easy — you talk, you check, you sign. Anything that drafts referrals, sorts documents or actions results is a different object, and the people handed it are receptionists, care coordinators and salaried GPs with no protected time. His point that organisations are giving up on polished courses and doing messy in-house training is worth taking seriously: the person in your practice who has quietly worked out how to use the tool well is your training resource, and half an hour of their time in a staff meeting is worth more than any supplier webinar.

### "What is an audit if agents do it persistently?" — a question with a medical-examiner edge

One of the six questions is what happens to professional services when the work can be done continuously rather than in samples. Audit is the example given, but the same logic reaches mortality review, significant-event analysis and coding accuracy: these are all sampling exercises that exist because looking at everything was impossible. It is not fanciful to imagine a system that flags every case meeting review criteria rather than the ones that happen to be noticed. That would be a genuine improvement in scrutiny and also a considerable increase in workload, and nobody has yet said who does the resulting work. Worth holding onto before anyone in an ICB describes continuous review as an efficiency.

## NLW's Takes

### NLW: AI optimism needs a voice — even if Zuckerberg is a flawed messenger

NLW argues the only acceptable answer to 'why build AI' is that it'll be awesome and worth the risks, not 'someone else will.' He thinks Zuckerberg's power to be optimism's face is limited by public views of social media — but says loud, sustained optimistic discourse is immensely important and he'll amplify it.

### The upskilling bill is coming due

NLW's bully-pulpit issue: when AI was just about prompting, you could skimp on training. Now that work is shifting from 'I do my work' to 'I manage agents that do my work,' the need for training is radically heightened — and non-technical people are being handed inherently technical tools.

### The paradigm shift has happened — and the questions are finally the right ones

Last year's event was full of 'if' questions: is this real, how do I prove ROI? Now the shift from assisted to agentic AI is here, and NLW says it should feel good that the questions — token budgets, observability, redesign — are the foundational ones enterprises will answer for the next half-decade, even if almost none have answers yet.
