How Business AI Use is Changing

Three fresh enterprise AI datasets from Ramp, Databricks and Arcana Research point the same way: price competition has handed buyers real leverage, and the leading companies are using a lot more AI without paying proportionally more for it. Plus: Nvidia's board appears to shut the door on Jensen's return, Commerce finally moves on chip diversion, and OpenAI's embedded safety evaluators fall short of what was promised.

Source: https://aidailybrief.ai/e/2026-11-17

▶ Listen — today’s brief (4 min)

The One Idea

Business AI has become a buyer's market, and the smart buyers are using it.

Across Ramp, Databricks and Arcana Research, the convergent story is stronger than the divergent one. Model competition is pushing token prices down, enterprises are switching and mixing providers far more readily, and the leaders are growing usage much faster than spend. The open question is whether the top 1% of spenders, at hundreds of times the median, are outliers or a preview of where everyone else is heading. That answer will shape the size of the market and whether all this CapEx turns into ROI. For enterprises themselves, the practical takeaway is that you probably have more negotiating leverage than you are using, as long as you've built the infrastructure to switch.

The Other Side

Zitron, writing the same day, reads the concentration of spending as weakness, not a preview. He says about 80 per cent of OpenAI's and Anthropic's revenue comes from one per cent of their customers, most of them unprofitable AI start-ups, several funded by Nvidia. He cites the same source Whittemore uses, Ramp, as showing Anthropic's growth slowing to 15 to 25 per cent a quarter from 47 per cent, and asks how those customers will afford tokens once funding tightens. His summary is that "the vast majority of people do not want to pay for AI". He does not discuss falling token prices or switching between providers.

Source: Ed Zitron, “The NVIDIA Bubble”, 17 November 2026

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether the heaviest one per cent of AI spenders are the future or the whole market. Both accept as fact that spending is extremely concentrated, that Ramp's data show spend growth slowing, and that engineering teams account for most of it. The empirical question is whether the median company's spending rises towards the top; the observable is the median spend per employee in Ramp's index over the next year, now about sixteen dollars a month. The residue is worldview: whether flat spending with rising use shows a healthy buyer's market or sellers cutting prices to hold customers. For Whittemore to be vindicated, median spend must climb while prices fall and use spreads beyond coding. For Zitron, the top spenders must cut back as venture money tightens, with the median unmoved. Both are polemicists with incentives: Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype. The signal is that both say spend growth has slowed sharply. Watch Ramp's median figure.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Token prices fell a fifth; has your scribe price moved?

The average price suppliers pay the two main labs fell 21 per cent in October. Scribe and triage vendors buy those tokens. NLW's advice to businesses applies to a practice or PCN: you probably have more leverage than you are using. At renewal, ask whether lower model costs are being passed on. Zitron would say the cuts show sellers under strain, which is a reason to keep contracts short.

Leverage depends on being able to leave

The buyers getting deals are those who can switch. In general practice that means data portability: can consultation notes, templates and audit logs be exported in a usable form, and does the tool sit on top of the clinical system or lock into it. Ask before signing, not at renewal.

Independent auditors chosen by the audited

OpenAI has let outside safety evaluators in, but chooses them and controls what they see; one evaluator said the access does not meet its own bar. The parallel in health is familiar. We would not accept a manufacturer selecting and briefing its own inspector. DTAC and clinical safety sign-off should rest on evidence a supplier cannot curate.

NLW’s Takes

Handpicked auditors aren't independent auditors

Even if Anthropic's version is better, labs are still choosing their own evaluators and deciding what access they get. That's reasonable for a business, but its incentives differ from the societal need auditors are supposed to serve. Embedded independent auditing is one of the rare safety ideas with broad consensus, so it deserves agreed-upon standards set by a genuinely independent force.

2026 was the year agents got real, just not for the average enterprise

Year-end retrospectives will likely crown 2026 the year of agents. Yet Arcana ranks autonomous agents fourth on its heat index of what companies are excited about, and dead last on its adoption index of what's actually deployed.

The market question: are the 1% outliers or a preview?

Each dataset has caveats: gateway-only customers, a narrow tech-forward sample, a small Global 2000 pool. But the convergent story outweighs the divergent one. The big question is how far the median buyer drifts toward the 1% spending hundreds of times more. That will say a lot about market size and whether the CapEx turns into ROI.

You probably have more leverage than you're using

Competition is vicious, and model providers are desperate not to become generic commodities. There are deals to be had, but only if you've built the infrastructure to actually switch.
