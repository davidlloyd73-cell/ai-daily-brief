The AI Model Tier List

A viral model tier list lands the same week that a misread FT chart, AT&T's open-model migration, and a flipped Vercel token graph all point the same direction: the question is no longer simply who has the best model, but how you assemble a stack that routes the right task to the right one — and NVIDIA, quietly, is buying its way into the open half of that future.

The One Idea

The model war is no longer about who's best — it's about what belongs in your stack.

Frontier models have crossed a threshold where they can all do a lot, and usage volume has exploded — so individuals, teams, and enterprises are shifting from 'which model leads' to model efficiency and complete model architectures that route the right task to the right model. Theo's tier list is the fun version of that conversation; AT&T serving 40% of employee queries with open models, routers cutting coding costs 56%, and open tokens flipping to 62% of Vercel's gateway are the serious one.

The Other Side

Zitron works from the same evidence and inverts all of it. He reads the AT&T migration not as a smarter stack but as the first move in cutting AI spend altogether — enterprises will save money "first by moving to open source models, then not using them at all", because in his view even open weights are expensive and of questionable use; he notes AT&T's headline 80-90% savings apply only to unspecified "certain applications" out of more than a thousand internal uses. He argues the whole debate about which model is cheapest misses the mechanism: "the problem is the amount of tokens that get burned", and labs are incentivised to build models that burn more of them. NVIDIA's Poolside deal, which the Brief reads as buying into the open-model future, he reads as vendor financing — NVIDIA "effectively becoming the GE Capital of AI", propping up customers who would otherwise fail. His sharpest structural point is one the Brief never addresses: "distillation requires a model to distill from", so a cheap open tier cannot outlive the frontier training spend it is supposedly replacing.

Source: Ed Zitron, “The AI Hater's Manifesto”, 25 August 2026 (from 25 August’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is whether the cheap end of the model market is a durable architecture or a symptom of a subsidy. Both accept the same facts: frontier capability has bunched up, most work does not need the top model, enterprises are actively moving volume to open weights, and open inference is not free because a token costs compute whoever produced it. The empirical question that will resolve is what happens to that open tier when frontier training slows — the observable is whether new open-weight models keep matching last-generation frontier quality once labs cut training budgets, and whether routed enterprise workloads grow or get switched off entirely at renewal. The residue is pure worldview: Whittemore sees firms discovering efficiency; Zitron sees firms beginning to leave. Whittemore is vindicated if routed open-model stacks become standard IT and total useful workload keeps rising as prices fall; Zitron if the migration turns out to be the penultimate step before abandonment and the open tier degrades without frontier models to distil from. Both are polemicists with incentives — Whittemore bullish and anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and anti-hype — and the signal is where they agree despite opposite priors: neither disputes that most people do not need the frontier, and neither thinks open inference is actually cheap. Watch whether the next generation of open weights arrives on schedule, and at what quality.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Routing by task is already how a surgery thinks — it just isn't how the software is sold

The useful transferable idea from the tier list is not the ranking, it is the admission that different jobs want different tools. AT&T is reported to serve forty per cent of employee queries with open models and keep the expensive frontier ones for genuinely hard work, cutting coding costs by fifty-six per cent for a two per cent quality drop. General practice already reasons this way about people: the HCA does the blood pressure, the GP does the undifferentiated chest pain. The problem is that NHS primary care buys AI as single-product subscriptions, not as a stack, so there is no lever to route the cheap high-volume work — transcription, coding, letter drafting, inbox triage — differently from the small amount of work where you want the best available model and would happily pay for it. Worth asking any scribe vendor which model actually sits behind the product, whether it changes without notice, and what the quality-cost trade-off was that they chose on your behalf.

The Fable data-retention story is the real procurement lesson, not the pricing

The Financial Times chart was read as enterprises refusing to pay for the best model. NLW's counter is that the model carries a thirty-day data retention policy, and that alone is enough for a large number of organisations to refuse it outright. For a UK practice this is the whole conversation in miniature. Capability is not the binding constraint on what a surgery can adopt; data processing terms, retention periods, sub-processor lists and whether the DPIA survives contact with the ICB are. A tool two tiers down on somebody's ranking but with zero retention and UK hosting is the better clinical tool, because it is the one you are allowed to use on a real patient. When a vendor's demo is impressive, the next question is not how good the model is but where the audio and the transcript go, and for how long.

If Zitron's right / if NLW's right — the same defensive move either way

If NLW is right, the open tier keeps improving, routing becomes ordinary IT, and the cost of ambient documentation falls steadily — good news for a service that will never fund premium licences at scale. If Zitron is right, open models are the first stage of cost-cutting before abandonment, and his structural point bites: distillation needs something to distil from, so if frontier training spend collapses the cheap tier stops improving and then stops being maintained. For a Harrow practice the response is identical under both. Do not build a working week that only functions with one named product; make sure the clinical record holds the finished note in a form the practice owns rather than the vendor; and treat any price you are quoted now as an introductory one, because both men agree the current economics are being subsidised from somewhere. Buy on demonstrated workload relief this month, on terms you could exit in ninety days.

NLW’s Takes

NVIDIA is assembling the whole open-model stack

Poolside for research talent, a stake in data-labeling startup Mercor (used for RL on the last two Nemotron models), and now a reported investment in Perplexity at a $30 billion valuation — a 50% markup — after initially floating a licensing-plus-hiring deal. Across all of these moves, NVIDIA is putting serious consideration into research, talent, training data, and the app layer as the open source frontier grows in importance.

The real story is the diversification of model stacks

The tier list matters not because it's fun to debate (though it is) but because individuals and increasingly businesses aren't picking one model — they're building infrastructure that moves between models by task. There's even a whole category of router companies built for exactly this, the best known of which, OpenRouter, was just acquired by Stripe for $7 billion.

The 'businesses won't pay for Fable 5' chart misses the real reason

The Financial Times chart showing limited enterprise spend on Fable 5 was read as businesses consciously rejecting the price. But Fable 5 carries a 30-day data retention policy — a provisional safeguard from when the model came back online after being shut down by the government — and that alone is enough for a huge number of enterprises to say absolutely not. Note how aggressively OpenAI has been pushing zero-data-retention for frontier models this past week.

NLW's workflow matches: run both, then commit

On any given day, the host is jockeying between Fable 5 and GPT 5.6 Sol — for many tasks initiating in both, going back and forth a little, then deciding which one to hone in on. That usually, but not always, ends up being Fable.

Expect a lot of models to fall into an uncanny middle

As labs compete on deliberate performance-efficiency trade-offs rather than just the state of the art, many models will end up neither frontier enough to justify a premium nor efficient enough for high-volume tasks. And price intuitions can mislead: Theo notes Kimi K3, despite being open weight, actually costs slightly more than Sol on extra high, because Sol does more with fewer tokens.

'What's the best model' is no longer the only question that matters

Increasingly, the job is understanding where different models fit for different reasons — and even slow-moving, single-ecosystem enterprises should set up environments where small groups can test approaches and hunt for these new efficiencies. Whether the days of getting excited about state-of-the-art releases are over, we'll find out soon: chatter says Fable 5.1 is coming shortly, while OpenAI's Astra has slipped to September.
