## 5 Rules for Better AI Writing

_Stanley Druckenmiller published a Wall Street Journal op-ed so full of AI-isms that a detector scored it 100% — and when the mob came, his answer was 'of course I used AI.' With the Journal backing him up, the question stops being whether AI writing is legitimate and becomes how to do it well. NLW offers five rules, plus a use-case-by-use-case crib sheet for emails, memos, social, marketing copy, and op-eds._

## The One Idea

### The purity test for AI writing will die. The quality test won't.

Druckenmiller's unapologetic 'of course I used AI' — and the WSJ's shrug — signal that AI writing is now simply a fact of life. But acceptance of AI writing is not acceptance of bad writing: the easier the inputs get, the higher the burden on the outputs. Quality reads as effort, effort's absence is glaringly legible, and writing is still thinking. Different types of writing need different rules — and laziness in work will always come across as laziness in work.

## The Other Side

Zitron's manifesto, published the day before this edition, attacks precisely the ground NLW is standing on. His claim is that the embarrassment people feel about admitting they used AI to write is not a dying purity taboo but accurate self-knowledge — "you know what you're doing". He draws a hard line between outsourcing work and outsourcing thought: using a model to write a thirty-line Python script or search a long document is, in his words, "inoffensive"; handing over the composition of an argument is not, because the words are where the thinking is meant to show. On his account the tell is not fixable AI-isms but the absence of a person underneath, and scrubbing the syntax only hides it better: "If your work is not better than an LLM's, you're bad at your job." Where NLW reads Druckenmiller as a craft failure with five remedies, Zitron reads it as the system working exactly as designed — a tool that lets people who don't do the work resemble people who do.

Source: Ed Zitron, "The AI Hater's Manifesto", 25 August 2026 — https://www.wheresyoured.at/the-ai-haters-manifesto/

## The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is detectability — whether a reader can tell that no one thought hard behind the prose. Both accept the same facts: the op-ed was AI-drafted, the Journal defended it, executive ghostwriting long predates AI, and this particular piece read as lazy. The empirical question that will actually resolve is whether that legibility decays; the observable is blind reader-preference and detector-accuracy testing on next-generation output against human-drafted equivalents in the same genre. The residue is pure worldview: whether words you did not compose can carry your thinking at all. NLW is vindicated if detectability collapses while the quality bar he describes holds; Zitron is vindicated if the tell persists and carries a reputational cost even when the argument underneath is sound. Both are polemicists with incentives — NLW bullish and professionally anti-doom, Zitron bubble-certain and professionally anti-hype — so the signal is where they agree despite opposite priors: visible low effort is contempt, and the thinking is the part that cannot be delegated. Watch whether other mastheads follow the Journal with an explicit contributor AI policy.

## GP's Corner

_Claude's notes on what today means for UK general practice and the NHS - commentary, not from the podcast itself._

### The scribe is summarisation, not composition — keep the line clear

Both sides here agree on one boundary even though they agree on nothing else: compressing what was said is a different act from deciding what it means. That maps almost exactly onto a consultation. An AI scribe turning a fifteen-minute consultation into a structured note is doing the safe half; the unsafe half is letting the generated impression, plan or differential stand as your reasoning. The practical habit is the one NLW describes for meeting notes — let the tool do the summary, then come in over the top and say plainly what actually mattered. If Zitron is right, that human overlay is the only thing of value in the record; if NLW is right, it is still the only bit a reader, or a coroner, will judge you on.

### Letters and reports: where a legible lack of effort costs something real

Referral letters, insurance reports and Medical Examiner correspondence are closer to the op-ed than to the email. A consultant colleague who spots the house style of a chatbot in a referral will discount the clinical judgement in it, fairly or not — that is Zitron's point about contempt for the audience, and NLW's rule three in different words. If Zitron's right, the discount is permanent and reputational. If NLW's right, it fades within a couple of years and only genuinely thin letters get punished. Either way, the safe practice today is unchanged: dictate or draft the clinical reasoning yourself, use the model for structure and tightening, and read every number and every drug name before it leaves the surgery.

### Bereaved families are not an audience for generated prose

One asymmetry the podcast does not reach. In Medical Examiner work and in condolence correspondence, the reader is looking for evidence that a person attended to their relative. There is no version of that letter where an obvious AI cadence helps, and the cost of getting it wrong is not embarrassment but harm. Whatever the wider purity test does, this is a case for writing it yourself.

## NLW's Takes

### Rule 1: Different types of writing, different types of rules

An email is not a strategy memo, a strategy memo is not a LinkedIn post, and a LinkedIn post is not an op-ed. They're trying to achieve different things, so how you engage AI around each will differ — which makes "can or should AI write this" mostly the wrong question.

### Rule 2: The purity test will die. The quality test won't.

The people saying this whole conversation will feel quaint in a couple of years are probably right. But the increased easiness of the inputs of writing will increase the burden on the outputs even more — accepting that people use AI to write doesn't mean accepting bad writing.

### Rule 3: Quality reads as effort — and lack of effort is obvious

The backlash to Druckenmiller wasn't really about prose quality. The AI-isms were so glaring and so fixable that it seemed like he didn't care enough to change the syntax on a couple of sentences — and if he didn't care to do that, how much thought went into the argument underneath?

### Rule 4: Longer is not better — usually the opposite

We have the phrase "work slop" because models have been better at saying a lot than saying the right thing succinctly. Great writers always knew this — Pascal in the 17th century: "I've made this longer than usual because I have not had time to make it shorter." The first wave of AI writing was defined by eye-bleeding length; the next generation won't be.

### Rule 5: Writing is thinking — and outsourcing it is the constant risk

Deciding your thesis, supporting arguments, and narrative construction is an exercise whose real product is the substance underneath the words. Using AI doesn't automatically mean outsourcing your thinking, but the risk has to hover in your field of view because it's so easy to fall into. NLW's own example: he built this episode's five rules manually in Notion rather than handing the concept to Claude — slower, but right for this context.

### NLW's own social pipeline hit its ceiling within weeks

He built a full pipeline from episode disaggregation on aidailybrief.ai to generated X and LinkedIn posts — and it worked, increasing sharing versus doing zero social before. But within weeks it slammed against the walls of what AI writing can do for social. The lesson: realistic expectations.

### Marketing copy: annoyingly bad — corrections become copy

Marketing wants uniqueness and distinction, so the cost of common AI-isms is highest here. The maddening failure mode: tell the model "stop presuming so much about the reader" and the next draft includes a headline reading "We assume nothing about the reader." The better use for now is rapid iteration — ask for 20 taglines to spark your own.

### Op-eds: do the effort sandwich

The whole point of an op-ed is to convince — and when readers can instantly tell it was low effort, they switch off. Get extremely clear on the thesis and supporting points before turning it over to AI ("the five-paragraph essay from grade school, baby"), then go back through and scrub the incredibly obvious AI-isms.

### AI won't change this: laziness in work reads as laziness in work

AI writing isn't going anywhere, and it will unlock opportunity — people who never used writing for communication will start to. What it won't change: there are no shortcuts for doing things well, even with tools that help us do things well faster and better.
