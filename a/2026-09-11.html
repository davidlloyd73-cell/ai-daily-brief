What to Use the Latest AI Tools For

Two episodes' worth of releases this week, and the second batch is less about individual features than about where everything is headed: voice as the new interface and complex model architectures that optimize for good-enough-and-cheap. NLW walks through eight or nine launches with a simple frame — who's it for, and what should you actually do with it.

The One Idea

Sometimes a launch matters for what you can do with it — sometimes for what it tells you you'll all be doing soon.

This week's second wave of releases points at two clear trends: we're going to manage more of our computer interactions by voice, and model stacks are increasingly optimized for cost and good-enough capability rather than pure frontier performance. NLW's practical read: start shifting your workflows to voice now, and pay attention to verticalized tools that understand how specific kinds of people actually work — because that's where the industry is pushing everyone anyway.

The Other Side

NLW reads this week's launches as a healthy market finding its shape: voice as the next interface, cheap good-enough models for teams building a sensible stack, and a compute crunch that proves demand is real. Zitron's Concentration Risk, three days earlier, says the money underneath that picture is circular. By his reckoning, using Ramp data, eighty per cent of OpenAI and Anthropic's enterprise revenue comes from one per cent of customers, mostly venture-funded AI startups that buy tokens at list price and then subsidise their own users at a loss; he calls them "the NINJA borrowers of AI". Those two labs in turn justify the hyperscalers' capex, and the hyperscalers justify NVIDIA, so a Microsoft 38-gigawatt target or a Huang growth forecast is not evidence of demand but of dependency, with some 200 billion dollars of take-or-pay compute commitments falling due in 2027. Cheaper models are not a comfort in his telling: the tech customers who pay the bills are exactly the ones most likely to defect to good-enough open or self-hosted models, which is more exposure for the labs, not less.

Source: Ed Zitron, “Concentration Risk”, 8 September 2026 (from 8 September’s edition)

The Truth Beneath

The variable both men orbit is who is actually paying for the tokens, and whether that payer is durable. Both accept as fact that the subsidy era is ending: NLW quotes Schumer's line approvingly and Zitron's whole essay is about what happens when it does. Both accept that demand currently outruns supply, that Microsoft and NVIDIA are staking five more years of capex on it, and that good-enough models are getting sharply cheaper. The empirical question that will resolve is whether spending survives the subsidy: the observable is whether OpenAI's paused Pro tier and the general repricing of tokens move revenue towards non-tech enterprises, or whether the venture-funded startups that Zitron says make up most of it simply raise less and buy less. The worldview residue is whether a compute wall is a growth signal or a warning. NLW is vindicated if hospitals, banks and small firms keep paying at real prices for voice and vertical tools; Zitron if the 2027 take-or-pay bills arrive with the same one per cent still footing them. Both are polemicists with incentives, one bullish and anti-doom, the other bubble-certain and anti-hype; the signal is that both expect the free-token years to end within months. Watch the revenue mix at OpenAI's next disclosure.

GP’s Corner

Claude’s notes on what today means for general practice and the NHS — commentary, not from the podcast itself.

Voice in the consulting room is already here; the question is who pays list price for it

NLW's instruction to shift to voice now will sound familiar to any GP running an ambient scribe: the shift from typing to talking happened in general practice before it happened in offices. The new piece is full-duplex voice at five cents a minute in the API, which makes a scribe that can be interrupted and asked questions mid-consultation cheap to build. If NLW is right, the next generation of scribes will be conversational rather than transcribing, and practices should expect vendors to pitch that within a year. If Zitron is right, the vendor pitching it is a venture-funded startup buying tokens at a loss, and the fee a practice pays today is not the fee it will pay when the subsidy stops. Either way, ask a scribe supplier what happens to their price if their model costs double, and whether they can swap to a cheaper good-enough model without the practice noticing.

Cheap good-enough models are the right fit for NHS drudgery, and the safer bet

Much of the AI work a practice could usefully automate is basic and recurring: coding letters, summarising discharge summaries, sorting the workflow inbox, drafting referrals. NLW's point is that this is exactly the work the new cheap models such as DeepSeek's Flash or Cognition's SWE-2 are built for, at a quarter of frontier cost. Zitron would add that the cheaper models are the ones that undermine the labs' revenue, which is an argument for a practice, not against it. The NHS should not be locked to a single frontier supplier for routine tasks; commissioning that specifies the job and allows the model to be swapped is the sensible reading of both men.

Compute rationing reaches the front line before the headlines do

OpenAI pausing new two-hundred-dollar subscriptions is a small thing on its own, but it is the first time a lab has openly rationed access because it ran out of capacity. Any NHS tool wired to a single API is exposed to the same rationing: slower responses on a busy Monday morning, rate limits, or a quiet price rise. Medical Examiner work and scribe drafting both tolerate a delay; a triage tool does not. Neither NLW nor Zitron disputes that supply is tight for at least a year, so contracts for anything clinical should say what the fallback is when the model is unavailable.

NLW’s Takes

Start shifting to voice now — the industry is pushing you there anyway

NLW argues the move from typing to talking is a generational shift held back mostly by historically bad native voice recognition like Siri. Beyond obvious wins for contact centers and missed-call service businesses, he sees openings for B2B sales inbound (talk through needs before a demo), language learning, tutoring, and job-interaction simulation.

NLW's practical voice tip: set up Whisper Flow and go for a walk

Even outside Devin, NLW implores listeners to intentionally shift to voice — via ChatGPT's native voice feature or a tool like Whisper Flow on your computer. His claim: you'll gain speed and be able to do more of your work while doing other things, like walking.

Finance AI is a power tool for junior bankers, not a replacement

NLW argues senior bankers won't build their own models and decks just because agents got better — accountability and iteration still fall to juniors. The real signal: firms like UBS now require prospective junior bankers to demonstrate AI proficiency as a hiring requirement.

The real OpenAI trend: verticalization that understands how specific people work

Taken together, OpenAI's updates hone in on specific business users, connecting the tools and context they need to make moving workflows into ChatGPT easier and more inviting. It's an industry-wide pattern — GrokBot also added native Salesforce, HubSpot, Gong, Clay, and Granola integrations for sales teams.

The rise of the monothread: compaction has gotten good enough to just keep going

NLW notes that context-window handoffs that plagued 2025 workflows have eased dramatically — Block's Kensar describes threads that build entire sync protocols and deploy across repos without breaking.
