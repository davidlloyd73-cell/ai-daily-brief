THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Friday 30 October 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 174

On the line today: The Budget's AI-and-NHS review gets a name and a deadline; the MHRA tells a US medtech conference how it intends to regulate AI devices; £15m of prize money and £24m of cyber defence sit in the small print; the EU's AI Act simplification is now law, with a December deadline approaching; and Anthropic's labour-market data is worth a second look. From the Eye: a Washington desk job, the occupations AI cannot touch, and a new prohibition.

1. The Budget names its AI reviewer

Wednesday's Budget commissioned an independent review of how AI and technology could improve NHS productivity, led by NHS England non-executive Mark Bailie, reporting in early 2027 to feed the next spending review. Alongside it sits £100m from the Transformation Fund for robotic-assisted surgery, with a stated aim of lifting robot-assisted procedures in England from 70,000 to 500,000 a year by 2035, plus a national registry to track outcomes. I have these from a trade summary of the Budget, not the Treasury documents, so treat the figures as indicative.

The view from the surgery: A review reporting in 2027 is a review that will be quoted in 2028. The robotic figure is a sevenfold increase in nine years, which is ambitious even for hospitals. Primary care is not mentioned in anything I have seen.

2. The MHRA goes to Washington

At the AdvaMed conference on 20 October, the MHRA's deputy director for AI and software, Jessica Paulsen, set out the regulator's direction: lifecycle regulation of AI devices rather than one-off approval, continued use of the AI Airlock sandbox, and real-world testing with Manchester University NHS Foundation Trust and NHS London. She said the UK is "moving from policy into practice". This builds on the MHRA 2030 Strategy and the government's acceptance of all 44 recommendations from the national commission on AI in healthcare. The agency says it will keep making its own decisions, despite closer working with the FDA.

Why it matters: Lifecycle regulation is the right idea for software that changes after approval, and it is the bit clinicians rarely hear about. The test is whether a practice can tell, in plain English, which version of a tool it is running and who is watching it.

3. A date to put in the diary

The EU's Digital Omnibus on AI entered into force on 27 July (Regulation 2026/1744). Transparency obligations began on 2 August; certain AI-generated-content marking duties for systems already on the market apply from 2 December this year. High-risk obligations for stand-alone systems, including employment and education, slip to 2 December 2027, and for those embedded in regulated products such as medical devices to 2 August 2028.

The view: The simplification buys vendors two more years. For anyone buying clinical software in the UK, the sensible assumption is that suppliers selling into both markets will build to the EU standard anyway. Ask them which one they are building to.

4. Prize money and a cyber budget

Two smaller Budget items deserve a line each. A £15m AI Challenge Prize, announced before the Budget, has a first round aimed at NHS problems, with the largest single award £2.5m. Separately, £24m extends the public sector's AI cyber defence programme to hospitals, councils and schools.

The view from the surgery: The cyber money is the more useful of the two. Every ambient scribe and triage tool we adopt is another route into patient data, and the practice usually carries the can when something leaks. Prizes make headlines; patching does not, but it keeps the lights on.

5. What the jobs data actually said

Worth rereading, because the debate keeps resurfacing: Anthropic's labour-market research from March found little evidence that AI had raised unemployment in the most exposed occupations, but a slowing of entry into them for younger workers. The job-finding rate for 22 to 25 year olds entering highly exposed roles fell about 14% relative to 2022, which the researchers themselves call only marginally statistically significant. Current AI use covers around a third of tasks in computer and maths roles. This is not new today; I include it because it is the best-sourced number in a noisy argument.

Why it matters: Medicine's version of this is the junior pipeline. If the routine work that trains juniors is automated first, the question is how anyone becomes a senior.

From the Eye

Item one The MHRA is recruiting a dedicated liaison officer based in Washington DC to sit alongside the FDA, a post created under the Pioneer Programme unveiled in June: a regulator with its own embassy. Item two The same Anthropic analysis found roughly 30% of occupations have no meaningful AI coverage at all, with cooks, mechanics, bartenders and lifeguards named, which is a comfort to anyone who has watched a lifeguard. Item three The EU Omnibus quietly added a new prohibition on AI systems designed to generate non-consensual intimate imagery and child sexual abuse material, the sort of clause that makes you wonder why it needed adding.
