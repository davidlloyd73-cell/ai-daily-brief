A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Sunday 5 July 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 067

On the line today: The 10-Year Plan turns one, and the promise to build "the most AI-enabled health system in the world" meets the ledger. A careful study of what ambient scribes actually get wrong lands in the same week the rollouts accelerate. An AI that reads hidden heart disease off a bog-standard ECG picks up its first FDA clearance. Azeem Azhar's team finally puts a real number on the AI economy — and it's smaller than the hype and hungrier for electricity than anyone admits. And the world's governments convene in Geneva tomorrow to agree on how to govern all of it, roughly a decade late. From the Eye: chatbots top the year's hazard list, Anthropic quietly overtakes OpenAI, and a menopause app becomes a unicorn.

1. The 10-Year Plan turns one — mind the gap between the slide and the surgery

Fit for the Future is a year old this weekend, and with it the pledge to make the NHS "the most AI-enabled health system in the world." The year-one scorecard is a study in the gap between ambition and plumbing. The wins are real but administrative: a Microsoft Copilot trial across 30,000-plus staff reportedly saved each of them about 43 minutes a day on paperwork, and roughly 70% of trusts are expected to hit the core digitisation standard by this spring. The transformation — analogue to digital, hospital to community, sickness to prevention — remains mostly the first of those three, and mostly on the admin side.

The view from the surgery: Forty-three saved minutes is not nothing, and I'll take it. But "most AI-enabled in the world" is a claim about front doors and interoperability, and we are still a service where the pathology lab, the GP record and the hospital EPR don't reliably speak to each other. AI can't route a result it can't read.

2. What the scribe actually gets wrong — a study worth reading before you switch it on

As ambient scribing scales — south west London alone is onboarding up to 20,000 clinicians — a mixed-methods analysis of real end-user feedback in a large hospital system has catalogued the failure modes, and they are not abstract. Incorrectly recorded medication names, doses and instructions made up 18.5% of safety-related reports; the scribe both dropping real conditions and hallucinating ones the patient never had accounted for another 15.4%. A separate procurement audit of 20 approved platforms found every single one threw up inaccuracies at the testing stage.

Why it matters: The medication-error signature is the one that should stop you. A scribe that mishears "amlodipine 5" as "amlodipine 15" isn't making a documentation error, it's making a prescribing hazard, and it does it in a confident, well-formatted paragraph you're inclined to trust. Read every drug line before you sign. Every time.

3. A hidden-heart-disease detector — off an ordinary ECG

Pathway Labs' EchoNext has taken what's billed as the first FDA clearance for multi-condition AI in cardiology: fed a standard 12-lead ECG, it flags high risk of six forms of structural heart disease — left- and right-sided failure, valve disease, severe hypertrophy suggestive of an infiltrative cardiomyopathy, and pulmonary hypertension — conditions that normally need an echo to find. Trained on more than 700,000 ECG–echo pairs, it caught 77% of structural problems in a head-to-head against cardiologists' 64%.

The view: This is the shape of AI that could genuinely help primary care — squeezing more signal out of a test we already do, cheaply, rather than inventing a new pathway. The catch is the same one that sank the AI-stethoscope trial: a screen that flags "high risk" is only useful if there's an echo slot to send them to. Detection without capacity is just a longer waiting list with better triage.

4. The AI economy, finally counted — and it runs on electricity

Cut through the trillion-dollar valuations and Azeem Azhar's Exponential View has done the unglamorous work: the first bottom-up, de-duplicated measure of what people and firms actually spend on generative AI. The answer is about $110bn of sales over the past year, an annualised run rate north of $175bn — enormous, but a fraction of the capital being poured in on the assumption it'll be larger still. The binding constraint they keep returning to isn't chips or talent; it's power. Data centres are hitting grid limits, and electricity is becoming the thing that decides how fast any of this can grow.

Why it matters: When the revenue is real but an order of magnitude below the investment, you're either early or in a bubble, and the honest answer is nobody yet knows which. For the NHS the lesson is narrower: the compute your ambient scribe leans on has a power bill and a carbon cost that nobody puts on the consultation.

5. Geneva convenes to govern the thing that's already here

The UN's first Global Dialogue on AI Governance opens in Geneva tomorrow, member states gathering to hammer out an international approach to a technology that has comprehensively outrun its rulebooks. It arrives as the EU has just deferred its own high-risk AI obligations by 16 months and Washington leans toward "grade it, don't legislate it." The Dialogue's ambition is coordination; the reality is a patchwork of national regimes moving at wildly different speeds.

The view: International AI governance in 2026 has the flavour of a fire brigade agreeing on hose fittings while the building is alight. Useful, necessary, and unmistakably behind the event. Worth watching less for what's decided than for who bothers to show up.

From the Eye

Item one — the hazard nobody prescribed. ECRI's 2026 health technology hazards list puts "misuse of general-purpose AI chatbots in healthcare" at number one, ahead of every device and drug-delivery risk — the danger being not a rogue algorithm but a clinician or patient trusting an expert-sounding answer from a tool never validated for medicine. Item two — the quiet overtaking. Somewhere in the export-control drama, Anthropic's valuation slipped past OpenAI's — roughly $965bn to $852bn — the first time the challenger has led the incumbent on paper, and barely a headline for it. Item three — where the money actually went. Digital health pulled in $4bn in Q1, but 12 companies took 59% of it, and among the unicorns minted was a menopause-care app; the future of medicine, this quarter, was hot flushes and mega-rounds.
