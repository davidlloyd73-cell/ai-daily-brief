THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Thursday 2 July 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 064

On the line today: A US export ban that pulled two frontier AI models offline for nineteen days was lifted this week — a first-of-its-kind reminder that the model under your software is now something a government can switch off. A UK study pits an AI triage tool against GPs and finds it safe but cautious, and least liked by the over-60s. Anthropic launches Claude Science to sell AI into pharma R&D, and quietly starts its own drug programmes on the side. The FDA clears yet another brain-MRI tool, on a list that's now four-fifths radiology. And Britain will spend July telling the world how to grade AI models rather than passing a law about them. From the Eye: a quiet American chronic-care experiment, a funding rebound, and the safety worry topping this year's list.

1. The AI model a government switched off — then switched back on

On 30 June the US Commerce Department lifted export controls on Anthropic's Fable 5 and Mythos 5, and both were back on the Claude platform by 1 July. The models had been forced offline on 12 June after officials judged them a security risk: Amazon researchers had found a jailbreak that coaxed Fable 5 into flagging software vulnerabilities and, in one case, writing code showing how one could be exploited. Anthropic rebuilt its guardrails, Commerce's own AI-standards centre tested them, and Secretary Lutnick signed the release. Nineteen days dark.

The view: Strip out the branding and this is the story of the year so far — the first time a state has yanked a frontier model off the shelf on national-security grounds and then let it back once patched. These systems are now infrastructure a government can turn off. The lesson for us is dependency: if a clinic's scribe or triage tool rides on one company's model, someone else's security review can take your Tuesday morning away without warning. Ask your vendor what their fallback is.

2. The triage AI that's safe, cautious, and least liked by the old

A UK mixed-methods study benchmarked an AI triage tool against GPs on same-day appointment requests and landed a reassuring headline: 84% agreement with GP urgency ratings and no dangerous under-triage — where the machine differed, it erred towards caution. The catch is in the detail. Patient satisfaction fell steadily with age, the over-60s notably less happy, and the authors flag an almost complete absence of safety data broken down by age, ethnicity, language or deprivation.

The view from the surgery: Over-triage is the safe failure mode on the slide and the costly one in the corridor — every precautionary upgrade is a slot, a call-back, an alarmed patient. And the age gradient should give anyone pause: the person most likely to be sitting in front of me with three problems and a carrier bag of boxes is precisely the one these tools serve worst and studies measure least.

3. Anthropic wants to sell AI to your drug company — and become one

The same week, Anthropic launched Claude Science, a version of its model wired into some sixty scientific databases and tools and pitched squarely at pharmaceutical R&D; the company says it will do for the lab what Claude Code did for programming. It's available to paid subscribers with up to $30,000 of credits on offer, and — the quieter half — Anthropic says it will use the tool to run its own drug programmes for rare and neglected diseases. Analysts read the timing as an enterprise-revenue play ahead of a possible IPO.

Why it matters: Two things worth holding apart. Genuinely useful for the computational-biology grind, quite possibly. But "will do for science what it did for code" is a marketing sentence, not a result, and an AI lab that both sells the drug-discovery tool and runs its own drug pipeline is a conflict pattern we've seen before. For the prescriber at the end of the chain the question is unchanged: show me the molecule that reached a patient, not the demo.

4. Another brain-scan tool cleared — on a list that's four-fifths radiology

The FDA granted 510(k) clearance to Brain Mets, software that detects and measures brain metastases on routine MRI. On its own, unremarkable. In aggregate, telling: the agency's roster of cleared AI devices has now passed 1,300, and close to 80% of them are radiology.

The view: The regulator's AI catalogue is, to a first approximation, a radiology catalogue. That's where the clean, labelled, gradable data lives — images you can score against a known truth. It is the opposite of general practice, which is messy, longitudinal and spoken aloud. So the tools keep clustering where validation is easy rather than where the need is greatest, and primary care waits for the leftovers.

5. Britain's answer to AI: grade it, don't legislate it

Rather than an AI Act, the UK's pitch this summer is standards. In July, at a meeting of the international network of AI Security Institutes that Britain chairs, the government is due to publish best practice on the science of evaluating AI models — how you actually test whether one is safe and capable. It's of a piece with the rebrand from "Safety" to "Security" Institute and a deliberate tilt from safety language to growth.

Worth watching: There's a respectable case here — good evaluation methods may do more real good than a statute out of date before royal assent. But "we'll set the standards" only bites if someone is made to meet them. For medical AI that gap is the whole game: it's one thing to publish how a model should be graded, another to require that the thing drafting your notes was ever graded at all.

From the Eye

Quiet money, quiet model — while the headlines chased frontier models, America's Medicare innovation arm switched on its ACCESS Model this month, a ten-year voluntary scheme paying providers to manage chronic disease with technology; the unglamorous plumbing that decides whether any of this reaches a patient with three long-term conditions, and precisely the sort nobody live-tweets. The money's back — digital-health startups pulled in around $4bn of venture funding in the first quarter, up a billion on last year and the strongest opening quarter since the pandemic, across 110 deals; worth noting where it's going — imaging, admin, documentation — and where it still isn't, which is the front door of primary care. The worry at the top of the list — patient-safety analysts have named "navigating the AI diagnostic dilemma" among healthcare's leading safety concerns for 2026, the fear being not that the software is wrong but that clinicians stop checking it — automation complacency dressed up as efficiency, and the failure mode that won't show on any vendor's slide.
