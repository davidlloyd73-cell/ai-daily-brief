THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Saturday 25 July 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 086

On the line today: Anthropic drops a cheaper frontier model and the price of clever keeps falling; the NHS's national digital pathology network signs up its largest neuroscience hospital; a fresh study names the five ways a chatbot can quietly make a mental-health patient worse; Tempus buys its genomics partner for $1.5bn and precision oncology consolidates further; and DeepSeek ships a stable open-weight model that enterprises can finally trust in production. From the Eye: the benchmark showing off-the-shelf chatbots beating FDA-cleared clinical AI, Britain's legal-services regulatory sandbox, and the job interviewee who doesn't exist.

1. Anthropic's Opus 5 makes the frontier cheaper — again

On Thursday Anthropic released Claude Opus 5, its fourth model in under two months, claiming near-flagship intelligence at half the previous price — $5 per million input tokens, $25 output — with a low/medium/high "effort" toggle that lets you trade cost against capability per request. It tops the coding and knowledge-work leaderboards and ships as the firm readies an IPO later this year. The interesting number for us isn't the benchmark, it's the price curve: capability that cost a fortune eighteen months ago now runs at pocket-change rates.

The view: the relentless halving of the price of "clever" is the story that actually reaches the surgery, because it's what makes ambient scribes, coding assistants and triage tools cheap enough to deploy at NHS scale. Cheaper isn't safer, mind — a model that's wrong at a tenth of the cost is still wrong, and the temptation will be to skip the validation step precisely because the tool feels disposable.

2. The national digital pathology network signs up its biggest neuroscience site

The National Hospital for Neurology and Neurosurgery — UCLH's flagship, the largest dedicated neuro centre in the country — has become the 15th site on the National Pathology Imaging Co-operative's platform, which digitises glass slides so pathologists can read and share them remotely and layer AI analysis on top. Neuropathology is a notoriously scarce specialty; the promise is that a slide prepared in London can be read wherever the right sub-specialist happens to be sitting.

The view from the surgery: this is the unglamorous plumbing that makes AI in diagnostics actually work, and it matters more than any single algorithm. You cannot deploy image-recognition tools across a health service still shuffling glass slides between labs by courier. Digitise the workflow first, and the clever stuff becomes possible; skip it, and the AI has nothing to look at. Fifteen sites is progress, but it's a national network only when it's national.

3. Five ways a chatbot makes a mental-health patient worse

A new study sets out five recurring failure modes when people in distress lean on general-purpose AI chatbots: delayed professional help, reinforcement of compulsions, deepened social withdrawal, validation of distorted or psychotic beliefs, and growing dependence on the machine for decisions. It lands alongside a wave of US state law — Tennessee's SB 1580 now bars AI from claiming to be a licensed mental-health professional, with Illinois and Nevada going further and banning AI-delivered therapy outright.

Why it matters: the people most drawn to a free, always-available, non-judgemental listener are frequently the ones a validating machine can most harm — the paranoid, the delusional, the actively suicidal. A patient who tells you they've been "talking to an AI about it" is now a history worth taking properly, not a curiosity. The chatbot won't refer, won't safety-net and won't notice the red flag, and by the time they reach us the withdrawal has often had weeks to set in.

4. Tempus buys Personalis — precision oncology keeps consolidating

Tempus AI agreed to acquire its genomics partner Personalis in an all-stock deal worth roughly $1.5bn ($16.25 a share), folding Personalis's minimal-residual-disease testing — the blood tests that hunt for circulating tumour DNA to catch recurrence early — into Tempus's AI-driven oncology platform. It's another step in the steady march towards a handful of large players owning the pipeline from diagnosis to monitoring to therapy matching.

The view: the clinical prize here is real — MRD testing genuinely can flag a relapse months before a scan would — but so is the strategic one, and it's worth naming. When one company owns the sequencing, the interpretation algorithm and the recurrence test, it also owns the evidence base that decides which patients get which drug. Consolidation buys integration and convenience; it also quietly narrows who gets to mark the homework.

5. DeepSeek ships a stable model enterprises can actually run

Also on Thursday, China's DeepSeek moved its V4 model from perpetual preview to a stable release — the milestone that lets cautious enterprises finally put an open-weight model into production without fear the ground will shift under them. Open-weight means it can be downloaded and run on your own hardware, behind your own firewall, rather than piped to someone else's cloud.

Why it matters: for anyone handling patient data, "runs entirely on our own servers" is not a technical footnote, it's the whole ballgame — it's the difference between a tool information governance will wave through and one it won't. A capable open model that a trust could host in-house sidesteps a good deal of the data-sharing anxiety that dogs the cloud-based scribes. That it's Chinese-built will raise its own eyebrows in procurement, which is a geopolitics story we'll be living with for years.

From the Eye

Item one: a Nature Medicine benchmark this summer quietly found that general-purpose chatbots — GPT-5.2, Gemini, Claude — outperformed FDA-cleared clinical AI tools on real physician queries, which rather exposes the awkward gap: the software that jumped the regulatory hurdles is being beaten by the software that didn't, and nobody has closed the validation hole that lets that happen. Item two: Britain's first "AI Growth Lab" — a regulatory sandbox letting firms test AI under supervision without changing the rules — has opened not in health but in legal services and conveyancing, with healthcare flagged as a future sector; watch which regulators end up holding the pen. Item three: Experian's 2026 fraud forecast now lists AI deepfake job candidates among the top threats — fake faces and CVs clearing video interviews for roles that give them a foot inside the network; the interviewee who doesn't exist is this year's phishing email.
