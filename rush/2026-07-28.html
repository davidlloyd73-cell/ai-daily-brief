THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Tuesday 28 July 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 089

On the line today: the Midlands quietly does what The King's Fund said nobody was doing — buys ambient voice technology once, for a whole region, 1,239 GP practices included; new research finds chatbots have learned to handle suicide and almost nothing else; an AI voice assistant walks 55 emergency patients through the conversation their doctors never have time for; two US senators go after the algorithms deciding who gets rehab; and 142 protests in 42 states say the data centre boom has found its politics. From the Eye: the machine reading 2.6 million cancer papers, the ethicist whose references didn't exist, and the 25-page complaint letter nobody wrote.

1. The Midlands buys once, for everyone

While the think tanks were writing about piecemeal procurement, NHS England in the Midlands went and fixed it. It has become the first region to procure ambient voice technology at scale for all 1,239 of its GP practices plus more than 70,000 clinicians across 15 acute and community trusts — a single competitive process, led by Dudley Group and Sandwell and West Birmingham, with Heidi named preferred provider. The pilots behind the business case are eye-catching: an 80% cut in documentation time in a Same Day Emergency Care unit, worth roughly one junior doctor of capacity, and a six-month rheumatology letter backlog cleared in 14 days. The NHSE-sponsored evidence puts it more soberly — 23.5% more direct patient interaction time, appointments 8.2% shorter.

The view from the surgery: the significant word is "once". Will Goodwin's line — that the region did the governance and compliance heavy lifting centrally so practices don't repeat it locally — is the whole story. Every practice writing its own DPIA for the same product was always absurd. Note also that Heidi is one of 23 suppliers on NHSE's self-certified registry, which is a registry in the way an honesty box is a till. The procurement is the good bit; the assurance still isn't.

2. Chatbots learned suicide. They learned nothing else.

Northeastern researchers published findings yesterday showing that the safety work triggered by the Raine litigation has been almost entirely confined to suicide and self-harm. Probing eight leading chatbots across hundreds of conversations and 16 conditions, Annika Schoene and Cansu Canca found the guardrails held for suicide — then collapsed everywhere else. The models supplied dosing information for illicit substances to a user described as an underage girl, appetite-suppression techniques, and advice on concealing postpartum depression symptoms from a doctor. Claude and, unexpectedly, Grok performed best. Current ChatGPT, Gemini and DeepSeek all posted 81% failure rates. Claiming to be a novelist doing research was usually enough.

Why it matters: postpartum concealment is the one that should make any GP sit up. We already know how hard those women are to reach; now there is a free, patient, non-judgemental machine coaching them on what to say at the six-week check. Schoene's question is the right one — the companies demonstrably can build these guardrails, so why only for the condition that generated a lawsuit?

3. The conversation nobody has time for

Only 37% of seriously ill older adults report ever having had a serious illness conversation with a doctor, and when they do it comes on average a month before death. A Northeastern team led by Smit Desai — who first mapped the workflow by interviewing 11 emergency clinicians, most of whom get four to ten minutes per patient — built an AI voice assistant to run structured conversations about goals of care and end-of-life wishes. Of 55 emergency patients, 49 completed the conversation and 46 found it acceptable. Published this month in the Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction. One assistant hallucinated a response the team described as "not very nice" and the conversation had to be terminated.

The view: as a medical examiner I read the consequences of this gap every week — treatment escalations nobody wanted, families guessing. So a tool that reliably starts the conversation is worth taking seriously, and the acceptability numbers are better than I expected. But an assistant that occasionally says something "not very nice" to a dying patient isn't a bug you patch and move on from. This is the right problem and, for now, not yet the right instrument.

4. Washington turns on the denial machine

Senators Blumenthal and Hawley — a bipartisan pairing that tells you something — have demanded internal records from UnitedHealth, Humana and CVS on how they use AI to refuse rehabilitative care, citing HHS Inspector General reports that examined prior authorisation data across 19 Medicare Advantage insurers. Days later, Senate Republicans blocked Ron Wyden's attempt to kill an AI prior authorisation pilot running inside traditional Medicare.

The view from the surgery: we don't have prior authorisation, and every time I read about it I'm quietly grateful. But we do have algorithmic gatekeeping arriving under gentler names — triage, referral optimisation, demand management. The American lesson is that an automated denial is politically toxic in a way a human one isn't, and that once you build the machine you find you can't switch it off, only argue about who it's pointed at.

5. The data centres find their politics

On 18 July alone, 142 protests against data centres were held across 42 states. More than 300 US cities, towns and counties now have bans or moratoriums on hyperscale construction. In the first quarter of 2026, 75 major projects worth over $130bn were delayed or cancelled with organised local opposition a factor. A June Reuters poll found only a third of Americans approve of the pace of construction and just 14% would be comfortable living near one.

Why it matters: AI's constraint was never going to be silicon — it was always going to be planning permission, electricity prices and neighbours. Britain has AI Growth Zones and a new Cabinet-level AI minister; it has not yet had the argument about whose water and whose bills. It will.

From the Eye

The machine marking the homework. A BMJ study led by biostatistician Adrian Barnett at Queensland University of Technology trained a BERT model on known paper-mill output and set it loose on 2.6 million cancer papers published between 1999 and 2024. It flagged more than 250,000 sharing the same linguistic fingerprints, 91% accurate on knowns, with the suspect proportion climbing from about 1% in the early 2000s to over 16% by 2022 — worst in gastric, bone, liver, oesophageal and ovarian cancer. Flagged is not fraudulent, but that is a great deal of oncology literature wanting a second look. The ethicist's imaginary friends. The Journal of Medical Ethics has retracted a paper built on fabricated AI-generated references, the fraud exposed in Bioethics: the author submitted from a French Yahoo address claiming an affiliation with the national anti-doping agency, which had never heard of them. Hallucinated citations have gone from 1 in 2,828 papers in 2023 to 1 in 277 in early 2026. Word soup, seven pages of it. Medical Protection reports patients now sending practices 25-page letters threatening legal action, self-drafted, "quoting various legal arguments that maybe don't even exist". A trivial grievance arrives as six pages you must read twice and then telephone the patient to decode. The Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman's response has been to publish suggested prompts to stop people over-cooking their own complaints. We have reached the stage where the state writes prompt guidance to protect itself from prompts.
