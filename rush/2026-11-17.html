1. The chatbot orders every test in the book

A NEJM AI paper from Stanford, picked up by MedCity News this month, priced the care plans that language models proposed for real visits against what the treating physicians ordered. At 2026 Medicare rates the AI plans averaged $314 a visit against $97, most of the gap being tests suggested where the doctor ordered none. Twenty general models and four specialist medical systems were tested, and the specialist ones were no more restrained. Telling the model to be parsimonious but safe brought the excess down to about 1.4 times. I have summaries, not the paper, and sources date it to mid-October.

The view from the surgery: A model that has never had to justify a referral or sit with uncertainty will investigate everything. Watchful waiting is a skill, and here is a number for what it is worth.

2. Twice as fast, no more accurate

Healio reported on 11 November from the American College of Rheumatology meeting on the ALLIANCE trial. Eighty-two physicians at seven hospitals were randomised to work up cases with or without Prof. Valmed, a clinical language model. Time per case fell from about 206 seconds to 94. Accuracy on the top diagnosis was roughly 33% with the tool and 35% without. The cases were Cogan syndrome, dermatomyositis and familial Mediterranean fever, and the lead author, Johannes Knitza, says three cases is a major limitation. It is a conference abstract, not yet peer reviewed.

Why it matters: Three rare cases tell us little either way, but it is a useful corrective to vendor slides that quote speed and stop there.

3. Faster scanners, same number of radiologists

Government figures published on 3 November show a net 125 extra CT and MRI scanners in England between March 2024 and March 2026, 107 of them in community diagnostic centres. AI acceleration software is now on 54% of compatible NHS MRI scanners, and services using it report 14% more scans on average. The Royal College of Radiologists has done the other half of the sum: by its analysis, 100 such scanners generate an extra 32,500 hours of reporting, the work of about 40 full-time consultants.

The view: The scan was never the slow part. Anyone who has chased a report for a worried patient knows the wait is for a radiologist to read it, and this adds to that pile.

4. Brussels told to do its own testing

The European Commission's two senior advisory bodies, the Group of Chief Scientific Advisors and the European Group on Ethics, issued a joint statement on 16 November. As summarised on the Commission's site and in secondary coverage, it urges the EU to build its own capacity to evaluate advanced AI models instead of relying on companies' self-assessment, and to enforce the AI Act strictly. MLex reported on 12 November that Finland and Estonia had written to EU leaders asking for the same. The Commission's existing plan has such capacity operational by 2027. I have not read the statement itself.

The take: We would not license a drug on the manufacturer's word alone. The awkward part is 2027, by which time the models being evaluated will be two generations old.

5. Nvidia's bad Monday, with results tonight

Nvidia closed at $156.14 on Monday, down 4.1% and its lowest since July 2025, with the Nasdaq off about 2.5%. Reports cite new US licensing rules on chip exports to five more countries, and word that the board is not considering bringing Jensen Huang back. Results come after the US close tonight, with about $109 billion of revenue expected. Anthropic is due to price its flotation tomorrow. The AI Daily Brief's 7 November episode, on a Menlo Ventures report, was titled "Companies Are Spending 5x More on AI Now"; I have only the listing. The model writing this brief is Anthropic's.

Why it matters: Customers are spending five times more while the chief supplier's shares fall. That says the doubt is about who keeps the profit, not whether the demand is real.

From the Eye

Item one The European Commission's Digital Fairness Act, proposed on 11 November, would make AI shopping agents disclose when they are paid to recommend a purchase, according to Pinsent Masons and others; nobody has yet proposed the same for a health chatbot that recommends a clinic. Item two Wavenet, a telecoms supplier, is marketing an AI receptionist for GP phone lines, built on InTouchNow.ai, which can be set as first point of contact for triage; the company describes it as a Class I medical device, the lowest risk class. Item three A tracker of AI hallucinations in court lists a High Court decision of 29 October, King v Director of Public Prosecutions, involving fabricated case law, with no sanction recorded; that rests on the tracker alone.
