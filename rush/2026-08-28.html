THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Friday 28 August 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 117

On the line today: Kaiser's mental health clinicians describe what happens when the triage layer is thinned out and an algorithm fills the gap, and California is legislating about it; Pew puts a number on how many Americans now ask a chatbot about their symptoms before they ask anyone else; the Dutch regulator fines Uber the best part of a billion euros for letting software end someone's livelihood without a human in the loop; a study of five years of company filings finds nine in ten executives think AI has done nothing for their productivity; and Instagram's boss concedes in open court that his flagship teen safety feature was used by 1.8% of teenagers. From the Eye: Amazon closes the human workforce it once sold as artificial intelligence, a wristband that watches your parents for $20 a month, and the quiet enclosure of public data.

1. What happens when you thin out the triage layer

Capital & Main, co-published by the American Prospect yesterday, spoke to more than a dozen mental health clinicians at Kaiser Permanente in California and Rogers Behavioral Health in Wisconsin. One triage clinician at Walnut Creek described going from a team of nine to a team of three in three years, with automated routing absorbing the difference; on a typical day a third of her two dozen calls are now with patients who have been bounced around the system. Clinicians report weeks-long delays, patients with prior suicide attempts missed by the filtering, and severely suicidal patients placed in intensive outpatient groups. Kaiser says licensed clinicians make all triage and treatment decisions. Two California bills are pending: AB 2575, protecting staff who override an AI recommendation from retaliation, and AB 1979, outlawing clinical decisions made solely on AI output.

The view from the surgery: the striking phrase in that piece is "service recovery" — the clinicians' name for the growing share of their day spent repairing trust the routing system broke. That is the hidden cost nobody models. And note the shape of the harm: it isn't a wrong diagnosis, it's a wrong destination, which is exactly the failure mode our own triage-by-app enthusiasm invites. We built Harmoni's triage on the principle that the algorithm sorts and a clinician decides. Thirty years on, that still looks like the load-bearing distinction.

2. A third of American adults have already asked

Pew surveyed 3,488 US adults between 22 and 28 June and published on 25 August. Thirty-four per cent now use AI chatbots for at least one health task: 28% for quick health information, 25% to work out what is causing symptoms, 22% for information they could not otherwise afford. Adoption runs highest among Asian Americans (56%) and the under-30s (44%). Nearly half rate the answers extremely or very helpful — but users are markedly less willing to call them accurate than convenient, and only 29% are very comfortable handing over personal health data. A companion report published the same day found Americans more likely to say chatbots hurt than help people using them for loneliness, depression or stress.

Why it matters: the gap between "helpful" and "accurate" is where our morning surgery now lives. A quarter of the adult population arriving with a differential they did not construct themselves is not a future problem. And the discomfort about data is instructive — people will tell a chatbot about their symptoms while doubting both its answer and its discretion, which is a fairly precise description of a consultation nobody would defend if a human conducted it.

3. Nearly a billion euros for a decision no human made

The Dutch Data Protection Authority has fined Uber €825m — roughly $966m, the second-largest GDPR penalty ever issued — for deactivating and suspending drivers' accounts by automated means between 2018 and 2022 without meaningful human review, and for failing to tell them the decisions were automated at all. The case originated in a 2020 complaint by the Human Rights League on behalf of more than 170 drivers, and was worked jointly with France's CNIL. Deputy chair Monique Verdier: "a computer should not make decisions on its own that have such major consequences." Uber is appealing.

The view: this is Article 22 of the GDPR growing teeth, and Article 22 is not an employment law — it is a general prohibition on solely automated decisions with significant effects on a person. Read it next to item one. A referral rejected, a triage priority downgraded, a prescribing request auto-declined: each is a decision with significant effects, and the UK's post-Brexit data reforms have been busy loosening exactly this provision. The Dutch have just priced the alternative.

4. Nine in ten executives say it hasn't worked yet

Mark Ma at the University of Pittsburgh, working with the Atlanta Fed, analysed millions of Glassdoor reviews, thousands of financial filings and hundreds of corporate AI announcements across five years of US public companies. About 90% of executives report that AI has not yet raised productivity at their firm. Stock reactions to layoffs explicitly attributed to AI averaged approximately zero, and management optimism across some 10,000 earnings calls bore no significant relationship to measured productivity. Employee sentiment about AI on Glassdoor was substantially more negative than the overall tone of reviews — and the more insecure the workforce, the worse the returns.

Why it matters: the finding that should travel is the last one. Announcing that AI will replace people appears to destroy the conditions under which AI helps the people who remain. Anyone drafting a workforce plan around the £10bn should sit with that for a moment, because the NHS version of this — telling staff the technology is coming for their jobs while asking them to train it — is already being drafted somewhere.

5. Eighteen months of headlines, 1.8% of teenagers

Adam Mosseri, head of Instagram, testified in Oakland on Tuesday in the child-safety trial brought by California, Colorado, Kentucky and New Jersey — the first in the federal social media MDL to reach a jury. Shown an internal document, he conceded that uptake of the 2021 "Take a Break" feature, launched to considerable publicity as a wellbeing measure, bottomed out at 1.8% of teenagers. Asked whether parents had any way of knowing that only 1–2% used it, he answered "Correct." Asked whether Meta had ever disclosed the figure, "Correct." Bloomberg reports Meta and 29 state attorneys general have since discussed a mid-trial settlement.

The view from the surgery: file that number away, because you will need it. The next time a platform or a vendor announces a safety feature for adolescents, the question is not what it does but what proportion of users ever touch it — and whether they will tell you. On present evidence the answer to the second is no, unless someone puts them under oath.

From the Eye

Item one — Amazon is closing Mechanical Turk on 30 September after 21 years, along with SageMaker Ground Truth and Augmented AI, exiting human-data infrastructure entirely. Bezos called MTurk "artificial artificial intelligence" — half a million people paid pennies a task to do what the machines could not. It has been eaten by AI-native labellers, and by the awkward 2023 finding that many Turkers were quietly routing their tasks through an LLM. The humans pretending to be machines were outsourcing to machines pretending to be humans, and now the whole arrangement has been closed for tidiness. Item two — the founders of Fitbit have launched Luffu Link, a screenless $250 LTE wristband shipping early 2027 that runs background AI over activity, sleep, heart-rate variability and voice notes, flags changes and routes alerts to family members, with GPS geofencing for help requests. It requires a $20-a-month family subscription for up to four people. Remote monitoring of frail elderly relatives is about to arrive as a consumer subscription product, entirely outside any care pathway, and the alerts will land with families who will then ring us. Item three — X has sent legal letters to Nitter, the open-source front end that let people read public X posts without an account, alleging scraping and unlawful API circumvention; the maintainer took the main instance offline before the deadline and paused development. It is a small thing, but researchers and public-health monitoring projects used exactly that route to watch what was being said about vaccines, outbreaks and drugs. The public square is still public. It just requires an account, and increasingly a licence.
