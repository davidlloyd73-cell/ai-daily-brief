1. MPs ask for a watchdog that can say no

The Commons Business, Innovation, Science and Trade Committee published Guardrails for Growth: Making AI Safe yesterday. As reported by The Register and others, it lists ten gaps in Britain's approach and asks for an AI Safety and Security Bill in the next session. The bill would create a statutory regulator, separate from the AI Security Institute, able to demand independent checks of safety claims, pause high-risk training, delay or block a release and suspend a service. It also wants mandatory incident reporting. The institute currently depends on voluntary cooperation. I found no government response, and I have not read the report itself.

The view from the surgery: Medicine settled this argument decades ago. The MHRA can pull a device and the GMC can suspend a doctor, while the body testing the most powerful software in the country can only ask politely.

2. A family doctor reads the small print

NPR ran a column on 17 November by Dr Mara Gordon, an American family physician, headed "I'm a human doctor with human intelligence. But I sure do like my AI notetaker". She likes the tool. She also reports that most scribe software has the doctor sign an agreement accepting liability for everything in the record, whoever drafted it. In one of her visits the note said a patient had been misdiagnosed, which was not what was said in the room. I have excerpts, not the full text.

Why it matters: The supplier writes the note and the contract, and the contract says the mistakes are yours. Read the note before you file it, every time, including at ten to seven.

3. Apple asks 200,000 people whether to trust the chatbot

Apple announced on 15 November a US research study, run with OpenEvidence, into how people use and judge conversational AI for health questions. Coverage in Digital Health News and ICT Health says up to 200,000 adults can take part, and that the study looks at trust and the quality of responses. I could not open Apple's own announcement, so I do not know what outcomes are measured.

The view: The useful question is what people do after the answer, whether they book, wait or stay at home. Whether they found it trustworthy is a satisfaction score.

4. One echo clip, and a diagnosis we often miss

The Journal of Cardiac Failure published an AI-themed issue this week. According to its press release of 17 November, a study of 280 outpatients found that adding an AI analysis of a single routine echocardiogram clip to the H2FPEF clinical score identified 78% of patients with confirmed heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, against 48% with the score alone. I have the press release summary only, and it gives no specificity figure.

Why it matters: This is the breathless, overweight, hypertensive patient with a "normal" echo whom we all have on our lists. A 280-patient study with sensitivity and no false positive rate is a promising start and no more.

5. Anthropic prices at $83

Anthropic priced its flotation last night at $83 a share, above the $70 to $78 range, selling about 980 million shares and raising roughly $81.3 billion, according to CNBC and the Associated Press. That passes SpaceX's $75 billion as the largest on record and values the company at about $1.5 trillion. Trading starts on Nasdaq today under the ticker ANT. Azeem Azhar's Exponential View podcast of 5 November went through the prospectus; I have the listing only. The model writing this brief is Anthropic's.

The take: A price above the range tells you about demand for the shares on one evening in November. The prospectus numbers, and whether customers keep paying, will take years to judge.

From the Eye

Item one Since 16 November the FDA has used "Super Intelligence" in place of "artificial intelligence" in new public communications, following a September executive order, per the regulatory society RAPS; existing guidance stays as written, so the paperwork will carry two names for the same software for years. Item two The Washington Sun, citing three unnamed sources, reports that bank executives and NSA officials privately told Congress this week's American outages came from rushed fixes for flaws that AI models had found; the NSA publicly says routine maintenance and the banks say software bugs, so treat it as unconfirmed. Item three A newsGP poll of 1,114 Australian GPs, published 12 November, found 49% using an AI scribe, while the Australian regulator's review of scribe products declined to say which findings applied to which product.
