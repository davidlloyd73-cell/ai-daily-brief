THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Saturday 11 July 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 072

On the line today: The FDA has, for the first time, cleared a device in which the patient talks directly to the large language model — an insulin-titration tool for type 2 diabetes — and the medical world is arguing about whether the AI is the interface or the decision-maker. Closer to home, medico-legal defence bodies warn of a "widening gulf" that could leave doctors, not manufacturers, holding the liability when AI advice goes wrong. NHS England's new ambient-scribe guidance quietly settles the consent question. Brussels blinks and delays its high-risk AI rules by sixteen months. And every major chatbot confidently diagnoses a disease that does not exist. From the Eye: a watermark that slipped, doctors' faces flogging supplements, and the quiet arithmetic of AI-assisted fraud.

1. The FDA lets patients talk to the model

American regulators have cleared UpDoc V1.0, the first FDA-authorised medical device in which a patient interacts directly with a large language model. The product is narrow by design: adults with type 2 diabetes speak or type to the system and receive insulin-titration guidance within a tightly bounded clinical indication, validated in a Stanford trial. It is already live at Cleveland Clinic, UCSF and Allegheny Health Network. Tellingly, the FDA cleared it via the 510(k) route against a humble predicate — a drug-dose calculator — reframing the debate from whether patient-facing LLMs can be authorised to how narrowly they must be scoped to get there.

The view from the surgery: This is the line we've been watching for — not AI whispering to the clinician, but AI advising the patient with no doctor in the room. Insulin titration is a shrewd first case: rule-bound, measurable, low ambiguity. The unresolved question, as STAT put it, is whether the LLM is merely the interface or quietly the decision-maker. For now the scope is a keyhole. But keyholes widen, and the precedent is now set in regulatory concrete.

2. The liability gap that lands on your desk

Medical Protection has issued a blunt warning about a "widening gulf" between how fast AI is entering clinical practice and how slowly the law is keeping up. Under current product-liability frameworks, AI systems are not clearly defined as "products" — which means developers and suppliers may be shielded, while the clinician who acted on a defective algorithm's suggestion absorbs the legal responsibility if a patient is harmed. The RCGP has pressed the UK government to publish its promised AI regulatory framework urgently, and the GMC's position is unchanged: you remain liable for AI-informed decisions, full stop.

Why it matters: This is the sharp end for us, not the headline writers. We are being nudged to adopt scribes, triage tools and decision support at pace, on the implicit promise that the technology carries its own weight. It doesn't — not legally. If an ambient scribe mishears a dose or a triage model downgrades a red flag, the name in the coroner's court is yours. Until Parliament closes the gap, treat every AI output as your clinical opinion, documented as such, not the machine's.

3. NHS England settles the scribe consent question

As the ambient-scribe rollout accelerates — four South West London trusts alone are putting the technology in front of 20,000 clinicians — NHS England has published fresh guidance on doing it safely. The headline clarification concerns consent: explicit patient consent is not required to use an ambient scribe in the course of individual care, but transparency is. Clinicians must tell patients at the start of each encounter that the tool is listening, and patients must be given a real opportunity to object. Data-protection and record-keeping obligations sit alongside.

The view: This is sensible and, mercifully, practical — a blanket consent form for every consultation would have killed the workflow the technology is meant to rescue. But "tell them it's on and let them say no" is only as good as the telling. Rattled off as a mumble between calling the patient in and taking the history, it isn't informed anything. Build it into the opening line the way you'd introduce a student sitting in. The patient who objects is rare; the patient who was never genuinely asked is a complaint waiting to happen.

4. Brussels blinks on the high-risk deadline

The EU has given final sign-off to its Digital Omnibus, the package that simplifies — and substantially delays — the AI Act. The Council's green light on 29 June, following Parliament's endorsement, pushes the compliance deadline for standalone high-risk AI systems from 2 August 2026 all the way to 2 December 2027, with AI embedded in regulated products deferred to 2028. Much medical AI sits squarely in that high-risk Annex III category. The transparency rules on labelling synthetic content are also pushed back, to December 2026.

Why it matters: The world's most ambitious AI rulebook has just hit the snooze button by sixteen months, under heavy industry lobbying and American pressure. Read it either way you like: pragmatic breathing room for firms drowning in compliance, or a regulator flinching precisely as the technology it aims to govern accelerates. For health-AI developers eyeing the European market, the runway just got longer — and the patient protections those rules encode arrive later than promised.

5. The disease that isn't, diagnosed by machines that are sure

A neat, sobering experiment did the rounds this month: a researcher invented a condition called "bixonimania," fed it to the major consumer chatbots, and watched ChatGPT, Gemini, Copilot and Perplexity all confidently describe its symptoms, causes and management — for an illness that does not exist. No model paused to say it had never heard of it. The fabrication propagated cleanly across every system tested.

The view from the surgery: We knew these tools hallucinate; what's instructive here is the confidence and the consistency of the error. A patient who arrives having "researched" their symptoms is now consulting a source that will invent a plausible-sounding diagnosis rather than admit ignorance. The same failure mode applies to us if we lean on a general-purpose model for a clinical query. The lesson isn't "never use it" — it's that fluency is not knowledge, and a machine that never says "I don't know" is most dangerous exactly when you'd most want it to.

From the Eye

Item one — the watermark that slipped. Buried in the EU's Omnibus is a quiet postponement of the Article 50 rule requiring AI-generated content to be labelled — pushed to December 2026. So the very transparency measure meant to help people tell synthetic from real has been delayed at the precise moment deepfakes are proliferating. Timing is not Brussels's strong suit. Item two — the doctor who never endorsed it. The American Medical Association called in April for formal protection against deepfake impersonation of physicians, after clinicians — Sanjay Gupta among them — found their faces and voices synthesised to flog longevity supplements and unapproved devices, some fakes convincing enough to fool their own acquaintances. Your likeness is now a marketing asset you don't control. Item three — the arithmetic of the scam. The FBI logged nearly $4.9bn stolen from older Americans through fraud in a single year, average loss $83,000 — and generative AI is now industrialising exactly the believable-voice, believable-face con that empties those accounts. The technology cuts both ways, and the vulnerable end is the one we send home after clinic.
