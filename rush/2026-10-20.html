A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Tuesday 20 October 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 165

On the line today: The MHRA publishes its 2030 strategy and ministers accept all 44 recommendations on regulating healthcare AI for its whole working life; Google's AMIE chatbot gets its first real-patient outing in The Lancet; a Wantage practice's AI triage bot draws the sort of complaints we can all predict; the AI Daily Brief argues model internals may be more readable than we feared; and Meta's new Muse Wonder models split opinion between benchmark and bench work. From the Eye: a bankruptcy, a ban on handwriting code and a worrying figure on rogue agents.

1. Regulate it for life, not just at the door

The MHRA published its strategy to 2030 on 14 October, creating a Centre for AI and Digital Health and promising a more adaptive framework for AI-based devices. It follows the government's response to the National Commission on AI regulation in healthcare, in which ministers accepted all 44 proposals. The central shift is from approving a device once to checking it throughout its time in service. Draft guidance on AI devices that change after approval is due by December, and the AI Airlock sandbox gets three more years of funding focused on post-launch surveillance. Note that none of the coverage I found mentions ambient scribes specifically.

The view from the surgery: Lifecycle monitoring is the right instinct, because a scribe or triage tool that drifts after an update is exactly what a practice cannot see for itself. The test is who pays for the surveillance, and what a practice is told when something changes under its feet.

2. AMIE meets real patients

Google's AMIE conversational diagnostic system has now been tried prospectively at Beth Israel Deaconess in Boston, where 100 adults chatted to it by text before urgent care visits, and 98 completed both the chat and the appointment. A physician watched every conversation live and none needed a safety stop. Its list of seven possible diagnoses contained the final one in 90% of cases, and its top three in 75%. Supervisors logged one hallucination, and in one case a physician thought the patient was made anxious when lymphoma appeared on the list.

Why it matters: This is a single-site, single-arm feasibility study with a doctor looking over its shoulder, and the authors do not claim better outcomes. Still, a pre-visit history that reaches the clinician before the patient does is the least frightening use of this technology, and the one I would trial first.

3. "AI Andi" and the wall

At Newbury Street Practice in Wantage, every call has gone through the AI Andi triage bot since July. One patient told the BBC she queued twenty minutes before it decided it could not help, and a 65-year-old said it concluded she was ringing about a pregnancy. The practice cites 79% positive feedback in the first month, says the system has passed NHS assurance, and accepts it has been harder for the less digitally confident. The local MP has taken up the complaints. A Fakenham practice in Norfolk dropped its AI receptionist last month.

The view: A system that sits in front of every call needs a human exit in the first ten seconds, and the practice should publish its abandoned-call and failed-handover figures, not only the satisfaction score. Averages of 79% hide the 21%, and that is where the harm lives.

4. Are the black boxes opening?

On 18 October The AI Daily Brief argued that AI models' internals are far more interpretable than assumed, and that past difficulty reflected weak tooling rather than inscrutable models, with pushback included in the episode. Two days earlier it flagged Anthropic's appointment of its first outside safety evaluators as an under-noticed story of the week.

Why it matters: I have only the programme's own summary, so treat the strong claim as a claim. If interpretability does mature, it is the route by which a regulator could eventually audit a clinical model's reasoning rather than just its outputs, which is what item one will need.

5. Muse Wonder: top of the table, middling at the desk

Meta launched its Muse Wonder models, claiming frontier status, with Artificial Analysis scores ahead of competitors. The top Full model is available only through Muse Code subscriptions. The AI Daily Brief reports reviews of real work are mixed, and the episode examines Zuckerberg's framing of AI as "normal technology".

The view from the surgery: We have been here with every diagnostic tool sold on its headline accuracy. Benchmarks measure the exam, not the clinic, and the same discount applies to anything arriving in the NHS with a leaderboard attached.

From the Eye

Item three The security firm Opsin cites a finding that 93% of misaligned agents had unrestricted system access, a useful number to put to any supplier proposing to plug an agent into a practice's clinical system.
