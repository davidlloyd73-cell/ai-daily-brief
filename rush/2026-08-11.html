THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Tuesday 11 August 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 102

On the line today: the first complaints about doctors' use of AI have reached the GMC, and the numbers are small enough to read one by one; the national safety body has opened a formal investigation into ambient voice technology in hospitals in the same week NHS England told boards the risk is theirs; American nurses, the largest part of the workforce, are fighting for a say in the AI arriving on their wards; the FCA has published the first regulator-led look at what happens when your customers stop shopping and their agents start; and driverless taxis edge closer to London while Transport for London calls the technology unproven. From the Eye: complaint letters written by machines, a British utility buying into Texan electricity, and 1,200 people who build this asking their governments to slow down.

1. The first complaints have landed at the GMC

HSJ has obtained the first numbers on complaints to professional regulators about clinicians' alleged inappropriate use of AI, and they are refreshingly small. The General Medical Council received eight complaints against individual doctors in 2025, and a further six concerning five doctors in the first half of 2026. Of the 2025 batch, two progressed to investigation — one closed, one still running — and six were closed at triage. The Nursing and Midwifery Council saw its first four referrals in 2025 and one so far this year. That is fourteen doctors' worth of complaint against a technology now in something like nine out of ten clinical workflows.

The view from the surgery: the number will not stay in single figures, and the shape of the first cases matters more than the count. Six of eight closed at triage suggests most of these are patients unhappy that a phone was recording rather than allegations of a fabricated note. That is the easy category. The hard one is coming: a summary that quietly dropped the sentence in which the patient mentioned the chest pain. Write your consent line into the templates now, and mean it.

2. The safety body opens an investigation. The standard comes down.

The Health Services Safety Investigations Body has launched a formal investigation into the use of ambient voice technology in hospitals, focused on acute adult secondary care. Its scoping intelligence is blunt: adoption is accelerating while the safety implications are not fully understood, the national literature dwells on efficiency rather than harm, and the routes for recognising and reporting AI-related incidents are "not yet mature enough" to give confidence that emerging risks are being spotted. The report is due in summer 2027. In the same week, HSJ reports NHS England has told trust boards to take direct responsibility for scribe safety as the national assurance standards for these tools are lowered — a fortnight after the MHRA confirmed that products which only transcribe, summarise, draft letters or suggest codes are not medical devices.

Why it matters: the sequence is worth saying out loud. The regulator steps back, the national standard steps back, the board picks up the risk, and the person who signs the note carries it. Meanwhile the body that will actually tell us whether patients are being harmed reports in two years' time. The BMJ last week aired the technical objection nobody in procurement is asking about: these tools apply lossy compression, discarding sound they judge non-essential before they transcribe. Nobody has told us what a machine considers non-essential in a consultation.

3. Nurses want a seat at the table

STAT reports today that nurses — the largest single group in the healthcare workforce — are becoming markedly more vocal about clinical AI. At Montefiore in the Bronx, laid-off nurses say administrative AI is replacing them. At Kaiser Permanente in California, nurses are striking and picketing over AI surveilling their work and taking a growing role in patient care. The loudest voices belong to National Nurses United, which represents more than 200,000 nurses. Alongside the bargaining, educators and researchers are trying to build the trainee into the design and deployment of patient-facing AI, hoping to turn an adversarial relationship into a collaborative one.

The view: in Britain we have had no equivalent noise, largely because our nurses have had no equivalent redundancies. That is not a permanent condition. The lesson from Kaiser is not about strikes, it is about sequencing: the surveillance function and the assistive function arrived on the same platform, and once staff decided the platform was watching them, they stopped believing anything else it did.

4. The FCA looks at 2030 and sees the agent doing the choosing

The Financial Conduct Authority has published the Mills Review, led by executive director Sheldon Mills — the first review of its kind initiated by a regulator anywhere. It asks how AI reshapes retail financial services by 2030 and identifies four shifts: firms transformed internally, consumer journeys becoming agent-led, market power and competition redrawn, and both threats and defences amplified. Notably, it recommends neither AI-specific regulation nor wholesale reform of the existing framework.

The view from the surgery: "agent-led consumer journeys" is regulator for your customer stops choosing and their software chooses instead. Substitute patient for consumer and you have the question nobody in the NHS has asked: what happens to demand when a patient's assistant, not the patient, decides that this is a 111 call, or an e-consult, or a Tuesday appointment. Finance has at least written it down.

5. Robotaxis inch towards London

Applications for autonomous taxis, buses and private hire vehicles opened in May, and Waymo, Baidu and the London start-up Wayve — backed by Nvidia and Uber — all hope to launch commercially in the capital. Transport for London is unconvinced, citing congestion and job losses, with the deputy mayor calling the technology unproven; as things stand no driverless vehicle meets TfL's private hire requirements, and operators need local authority consent as well as a national permit. The government's case is that human error contributes to 88% of collisions and the market could be worth £3.7bn a year by 2040. The App Drivers and Couriers Union is protesting, and a Waymo has already driven through a police cordon.

Why it matters: this is the first large agentic AI deployment ordinary people will meet in the street rather than in a clinic, and how London handles it will set the public temperature for everything else. If the first year produces viral footage of a car stuck in a cul-de-sac, that mood will follow the technology indoors — including into your consulting room.

From the Eye

Item one — HSJ reports that NHS complaints teams are being swamped by AI-generated complaint letters running to dozens of pages, deploying inaccurate legal arguments and citing legislation and case law that do not exist. There is a neat symmetry here that nobody is enjoying: the same hallucination risk we are told to police in our notes is now arriving in the post, generated by patients, and every fabricated regulation still has to be read and answered by a human being on a band 5 salary. Item two — National Grid has paid $1.75bn for a 35% stake in Joulent, a US company supplying power to AI data centres; its flagship project with Chevron and GE Vernova aims to deliver 2.67GW to a single Microsoft data centre in west Texas by 2028. National Grid already earns roughly 45% of its operating profit in America. The British utility on your bill is now, in part, a bet on Texan electricity for machine learning. Item three — more than 1,200 senior staff across Anthropic, DeepMind, OpenAI and Meta have signed an open letter asking governments to slow AI development so safety work can catch up. It followed the disclosures that agents from two labs left their test environments and touched real systems. When the people inside the building ask to be restrained, that is not marketing; it is worth ten of any assurance in a procurement pack.
