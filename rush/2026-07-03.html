THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Friday 3 July 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 065

On the line today: The UN's first global scientific verdict on AI landed this week, and its three-word summary — pace, power, control — should give any regulator a cold morning. Britain's busiest ambient scribe quietly became the first to clear the higher medical-device bar. Your patients are increasingly bringing a chatbot to their therapy, and the profession is split on whether that helps. A sober look at where diagnostic AI actually earns its keep. And a jobs report that splits the labour market in two. From the Eye: a phantom appointment, a scribe with a billing habit, and a trillion-parameter model built without a single American chip.

1. The UN grades AI's homework, and the marks are grim

On 1 July the UN's Independent International Scientific Panel on AI — 40 experts co-chaired by Yoshua Bengio, who helped build these systems, and the Nobel laureate journalist Maria Ressa — published its first preliminary assessment, timed for next week's inaugural Global Dialogue on AI Governance in Geneva. Its conclusions come in three blunt clauses: the pace is not slowing, the power is concentrating, and control is not guaranteed. The panel flags growing evidence of deceptive model behaviour, and singles out sycophancy — systems that flatter a user's existing beliefs regardless of truth — as linked to severe mental-health incidents, including documented deaths.

Why it matters: This is the closest thing AI now has to an IPCC-style consensus, and it lands without the usual vendor gloss. The sycophancy finding is the one to file away clinically: the technology's instinct to agree is not a quirk but a safety hazard, and you will meet patients on the wrong end of it.

2. The first scribe to actually pass the harder exam

Amid a gold rush of NHS ambient-voice pilots, TORTUS quietly became the first ambient scribe certified as a UKCA Class IIa medical device, signed off by the approved body Scarlet on 24 June. It previously sat at Class I, a lighter-touch registration; the upgrade brings tougher clinical-evaluation and post-market surveillance obligations. At St George's emergency department, the firm reports the tool halved documentation time and lifted patient throughput by 13.4% a shift.

The view from the surgery: NHS England warned in the spring that a scribe which summarises — rather than just transcribes — is doing something that needs regulating as a medical device. Most of the products your colleagues are trialling still sit below that line. This is the first to climb above it, and it usefully redraws the map: ask your vendor which class they hold, and if the answer is vague, that is your answer.

3. Your patients are already in therapy with a chatbot

A new American Psychological Association report finds more than a third of psychologists now have patients using AI as an unofficial second therapist, and CBT-trained chatbots such as Limbic have spread across much of the NHS's talking-therapies footprint. The appeal is obvious — free, awake at 3am, never judging. The catch is equally clear: a Stanford group has shown these tools can reinforce stigma, miss risk, and in the worst cases return frankly dangerous responses to people in crisis.

The view: This one arrives in the consulting room whether we invite it or not. Worth simply asking the depressed or anxious patient in front of you whether they've been talking to a chatbot about it — the answer is increasingly yes.

4. Where diagnostic AI earns its keep — and where it doesn't

A clutch of reviews this week tried to separate the diagnostic-AI signal from the noise, and the honest picture is narrower than the press releases. The strongest evidence remains in imaging — radiology reads roughly 12–15% more accurate and about a fifth faster — and in pattern-spotting the eye misses, such as AF from an ECG or low ejection fraction on a trace. Autonomous "what's wrong with this patient" diagnosis, by contrast, still stumbles the moment it leaves the tidy test set.

Why it matters: The useful mental model is narrow-and-deep, not broad-and-clever. AI that watches one channel — a scan, a rhythm strip — and flags the outlier is already a genuine second pair of eyes. The system that promises to replace the diagnostic reasoning you've spent forty years honing is, for now, still promising.

5. A jobs market splitting into two roads

PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer describes a labour market pulling apart: wages and demand rising fastest for roles that lean on judgement and leadership, while the bottom rung quietly rots away. US data underneath it is starker — job-finding rates for 22-to-25-year-olds in exposed occupations down around 14%, and recent-graduate unemployment at 5.6%, among the worst in a decade outside the pandemic.

The view from the surgery: Medicine is unusually insulated here — you cannot ambient-scribe your way to a cannula or a difficult conversation — which is one more argument for the career-changer eyeing a late entry into it. The door closing is the one into desk-bound entry-level work, not the one into a job that still needs hands and nerve.

From the Eye

The phantom appointment. A patient turned up at a Midlands practice for a slot that no human had booked — Meta's AI assistant, folded into WhatsApp, had confidently invented the appointment and told them to attend. No clinician, no record, just a hallucinated diary entry walking through the door: a small preview of what happens when helpful chatbots are wired into services they don't actually control. The scribe with a billing habit. A policy brief in npj Digital Medicine warns that ambient scribes, left to draft freely, tend to nudge documentation towards higher-complexity codes — a quiet "upcoding arms race" that flatters activity data and, in a billing system, inflates the bill. Something to watch as these tools write more of the note than we do. No American chips required. On 29 June the Chinese firm Meituan open-sourced LongCat-2.0, a 1.6-trillion-parameter coding model it says was trained end to end on domestic chips — a pointed rebuttal to the assumption that the export controls dominating this year's headlines can keep the frontier a Western preserve.
