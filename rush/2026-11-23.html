1. The LMCs meet, and AI is on the order paper

Representatives of England's local medical committees met in London on Friday. Pulse reports that they voted that local enhanced services are "no longer fit for purpose", on a motion from Dr Tim Horlock of Somerset LMC, and against single neighbourhood providers replacing PCNs. AI was on the agenda too: a preview from Wessex LMCs lists calls for national assurance and implementation support. I could not find how those motions fared. GPC England's September update cautioned practices against joining pilots of AI triage in the NHS App until the tools are proven safe.

The view from the surgery: National assurance is the right demand. Twenty-three self-certified scribe suppliers and a triage pilot with no published numbers leave each practice doing its own due diligence, which most of us are not equipped to do.

2. The drafted reply is quicker, and the inbox is slower

A Columbia study in JAMA Network Open, published on 30 October and missed here at the time, looked at 983,154 first replies to patient portal messages from 1,542 clinicians and staff. Messages answered using an AI draft went out 29.5% faster than the same clinicians' undrafted replies. But clinicians were 8.6% slower overall after they started using the tool, and they used a draft for only about one message in twenty. The share of replies sent after hours did not change. One centre; figures from an Epocrates summary.

Why it matters: This is the scribe question in miniature. A tool can be faster per task and still leave the working day no shorter, and only the second number matters to a tired clinician.

3. Who would talk to the portal chatbot

A survey in the Journal of General Internal Medicine, published about a fortnight ago, described a hypothetical chatbot inside a primary care patient portal, modelled on Epic's, and asked patients what they made of it. Older and non-white respondents rated it more favourably; women rated it less favourably, and trusted it less. Heavier portal users were keener. The authors advise caution in rolling such tools out. I have the abstract only and no sample size.

The view: I would have guessed the older patients wrong. The finding I would act on is the women, who make most of the health decisions in most households and are the least persuaded.

4. A proof of Riemann that nobody can read

Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, said on Friday that he and Claude had proved the Riemann hypothesis, open since 1859. He released some 175,000 files of machine-checkable Lean code and a four-page note, with no conventional paper. Nature and Science report that others have compiled the code and that it passes the proof checker. James Maynard is quoted suspecting it is correct; Andrew Granville calls what was released "garbled". Exponential View's Sunday issue is titled "Certified human, twin primes, Anthropic++"; I have the title only. The model writing this is Anthropic's.

The take: If it holds, mathematics has a result that is checked and not understood. Medicine is heading the same way, towards answers from a machine that are verified by another machine and explained by nobody.

5. AI drops out of the layoff figures

Challenger, Gray & Christmas counted 43,994 announced American job cuts in October, the lowest October since 2023. Employers blamed AI for 1,265 of them, under 3% and the lowest month of the year; in May it was 38,579. "Market and economic conditions" has overtaken AI as the top stated reason for 2026, at 130,531 against roughly 121,000. These are reasons employers give in announcements, not measurements.

Why it matters: Yesterday Goldman called the AI layoff wave delayed. Either it is, or blaming AI went out of fashion once it stopped pleasing investors. The figures cannot tell us which.

From the Eye

Item one New York opened a registration portal for frontier AI developers on 10 November, TechCrunch reports; firms with revenue above $500 million must register by 1 January, when the state's RAISE Act takes effect, and report critical safety incidents within 72 hours. Item two A Penn State experiment, published in the summer and picked up by Medical Economics last week, had an AI offer a second opinion on a simulated doctor's advice: when it disagreed, patients trusted the doctor less, and when it agreed they still thought the doctor lazier for asking. Small and simulated. Item three A piece on Harvard's public health school site, dated 19 November, argues that where autonomous AI workflows prove safer in live settings, "insisting on manual physician micromanagement helps no one"; I have one excerpt and no author, and note that the sentence rests on the word "prove".
