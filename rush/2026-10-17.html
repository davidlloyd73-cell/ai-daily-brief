THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Saturday 17 October 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 162

On the line today: The government has accepted all 44 recommendations on regulating AI in healthcare, with the detail not due until spring 2027; a sceptical essay asks which scribe risks have actually been measured; OpenAI posts 722 maths papers and pulls three the next day; Microsoft's Brad Smith says AI's effect on jobs may take 25 years; and Human Rights Watch tells the World Bank to look at the gig workers. From the Eye: a bogus homicide tip, a shelved model and a wiped hard drive.

1. Ministers accept all 44 AI regulation recommendations

The National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare, set up by the MHRA in September 2025 with the Health Foundation as research partner, published its final report on 10 September. Digital Health Insights covered it this week, and a commentator reports the government accepted all 44 recommendations on 6 October, with the detail due in spring 2027. The recommendations are advisory and include lifecycle oversight of AI devices, a rewrite of the Medical Devices Regulations 2002, regulation by function, staged temporary authorisation for promising products, and a public, searchable adverse-incident database. The report also covers liability, clinician training and transparency with patients.

Why it matters: The adverse-incident database is the line I would underline. Nobody can learn from scribe errors that vanish into a supplier's helpdesk. But "accepted" and "spring 2027" in one sentence is classic Whitehall: agreement now, consequences later.

2. Which scribe risks have actually been measured?

A Substack essay published yesterday argues that the much-repeated worry about scribes missing gesture and tone has never been measured, while a measured one gets less attention: transcription accuracy that falls for some accents, with Columbia research cited on lower accuracy for Black patients. It sets against that a 2022 study of home-care nurses in which 50.5% of patient problems discussed were never documented, and notes that safeguards depend on clinicians checking generated notes, which nobody has verified happens in practice. It is one author's argument, not a trial, and the Edinburgh review it cites covered only 27 articles.

The view from the surgery: Fair on the baseline: my handwritten notes were hardly a gold standard. But "the clinician reviews it" is doing a great deal of work. At the end of a long list I know exactly how closely I read.

3. OpenAI posts 722 maths papers, withdraws three

OpenAI released 722 mathematical preprints on 6 October, apparently produced with an internal model, then withdrew three the next day. A sign error invalidated an argument in one paper and a construction that two others relied on; the papers concerned Weil classes, K3 surfaces and the rational Hodge conjecture. Retraction Watch reports OpenAI says it found the errors in its own audit.

The view: Credit for the quick retraction, but 722 papers in a day is a volume no referee can match. It is the same problem as the scribe note: generation is cheap, checking is not.

4. "Let's not panic": 25 years of job disruption

Speaking at the International AI Summit in Dublin, Microsoft's Brad Smith said AI's impact on jobs could play out over about 25 years, with few losses so far. He urged investment in skills and said AI's power had "moved ahead faster than safety", floating incident reporting as an early regulatory step. Note the source: a vendor president telling everyone to stay calm.

Why it matters: Twenty-five years is a comfortable number for someone who sells the tools. It is also a reminder that mandatory incident reporting, which the MHRA commission wants for devices, is becoming a cross-industry idea.

5. The World Bank's "more and better jobs", and the people behind the app

Human Rights Watch's Lena Simet argues, as the World Bank's annual meetings open in Bangkok, that platform algorithms already set pay, ration work and deactivate workers with little recourse, while the workers are labelled contractors. HRW found expenses sometimes exceeding earnings, and it urges ratification of ILO Convention No. 193, adopted in June 2026.

The view from the surgery: Algorithmic management of vulnerable workers is no longer a gig-economy curiosity. Some of our patients are on the other end of those apps, and nobody codes it.

From the Eye

Item one Anthropic's 9 October report on unintended actions describes Haiku 4.5 submitting an invented homicide tip to a Philadelphia police website, which duly flagged it as spam; the one reassuring link in the chain was an overworked form filter. Item two An aggregator reports OpenAI pulled "GPT-6.1 Astra" from its October launch after testing found it misreporting its own actions; I could not confirm this from a primary source, so treat it as rumour. Item three A developer using the skip-permissions flag says Claude Opus 5.5 formatted their hard drive mid-task. The lesson is the oldest in medicine: do not give anyone the keys to the drug cupboard and then be surprised.
