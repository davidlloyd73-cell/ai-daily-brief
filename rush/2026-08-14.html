THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Friday 14 August 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 105

On the line today: a record number of AI devices have qualified for Medicare's new-technology top-up this year, and health economists are pointing out the obvious problem with paying a hospital every time it runs an algorithm; the FDA's consultation on the software it has decided not to regulate closed yesterday, which is a sentence worth reading twice; OpenAI's foundation is putting $100m into doubling hepatitis C cure rates in four American states; Demis Hassabis has taken his proposal for an industry safety watchdog directly to the US Treasury; and a data centre landlord is contemplating a $100bn flotation. From the Eye: a referral programme cancelled within 24 hours of publication, a Chinese AI firm about to delete eight months of its users' work, and computers that float.

1. Paid per algorithm

A record number of AI-based devices qualified this year for Medicare's new technology add-on payments — the temporary two-to-three-year top-up that helps hospitals absorb the cost of expensive new kit. STAT's Katie Palmer reported yesterday, in the fourth part of her Paying for AI series, that these payments have become a central commercial strategy for AI startups: hard to persuade a health system that an algorithm pays for itself, much easier if the federal government covers the first few years. Health economists quoted in the piece warn the per-use structure creates an obvious incentive towards volume: some AI-assisted scans now attract reimbursement above $1,000. The payments are meant to bridge a gap, then expire.

The view from the surgery: we have watched this film before with a different cast. Any time you attach a fee to an act rather than an outcome, the act becomes more frequent — that is not cynicism, it is the whole basis of QOF, item-of-service payments and every enhanced service any of us has ever signed. The awkwardness with diagnostic AI is that the extra activity is invisible: nobody sees an unnecessary algorithm run, they only see the incidental finding it generated and the CT that followed. Britain has no NTAP equivalent, which is usually presented as a barrier to adoption. On this evidence it might also be a rather effective accident preventer.

2. The consultation about the software nobody regulates

The FDA's request for information on non-device software closed yesterday. It asked stakeholders for real-world evidence on how software functions excluded from the medical device definition under the 21st Century Cures Act — clinical decision support, data transfer and display — actually affect patient safety, alert fatigue, user training and clinical decision-making. The context is January's revised CDS guidance, which relaxed the criteria considerably and let a large class of generative tools offering diagnostic suggestions reach American clinics without device review. The American Hospital Association filed a response on the closing day.

Why it matters: the regulator loosened the definition first and is asking what happened second, which is an unusual order of operations. It rhymes with the MHRA's July clarification that ambient voice tools which transcribe, summarise and draft — with a clinician reviewing the output — are not medical devices. Both decisions rest entirely on the assumption that the clinician meaningfully reviews. That assumption is the single load-bearing beam in the whole regulatory structure on both sides of the Atlantic, and nobody has yet measured how strong it is at 6pm on a Friday.

3. A hundred million dollars for hepatitis C

The OpenAI Foundation announced on Wednesday that it is committing $100m to the Common Health Coalition's Breakthroughs to Follow-Through initiative, headed by Anna Makanju. The first target is deliberately unglamorous: at least doubling hepatitis C cure rates over two years across eight initial states and localities, with the opening awards going to collaborations in Alabama, Illinois, Louisiana and Massachusetts. Participating jurisdictions choose their own tools. HIV prevention and curable cancers are next. The stated problem is not discovery but delivery — a curable disease with a poor cure rate.

The view: choosing hep C is a genuinely good instinct. We have had direct-acting antivirals for a decade and the barrier has never been the pharmacology, it has been finding people, testing them, getting them to treatment and keeping them there — exactly the tedious case-finding and follow-up work that a well-built system can do at scale. It is also, of course, a frontier lab funding an evaluation of its own products in public health. Worth watching whether anyone independent gets to mark the homework.

4. Hassabis takes the watchdog to Treasury

The Wall Street Journal reported yesterday that Demis Hassabis has pitched his proposal for an independent AI safety standards body directly to Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and White House science and technology director Michael Kratsios, as well as to executives at rival labs. The model is a FINRA for AI — industry-funded, federally overseen, starting with voluntary pre-release reviews that could harden into conditions of market access. Sam Altman has been pushing a different shape: an IAEA-style international body certifying countries and companies, with access to frontier models as the lever.

The view from the surgery: the medical royal colleges are the closest analogue we have — industry-funded, self-governing, setting standards that eventually acquired teeth. They took a century and several scandals to get there. The interesting question is not whether the labs will accept oversight, but who ends up writing the clinical safety standard for a model deployed in a consulting room: a regulator, a college, or the company's own review board with a federal logo on the letterhead.

5. The landlords are worth more than the tenants

Vantage Data Centers is weighing an IPO that would value it at around $100bn, Reuters reported, in preliminary talks with advisers about raising roughly $10bn; a sale is also on the table, possibly as soon as next year. Backed by Silver Lake and DigitalBridge, Vantage has raised about $11bn since late 2023 and is building part of the Wisconsin campus tied to the OpenAI–Oracle Stargate programme. The same week, legal AI firm Legora sought funding above $10bn barely four months after a $5.6bn valuation, and the coding startup Cognition opened talks at $40bn-plus.

Why it matters: a warehouse full of other people's computers is being priced as though it were the intelligence itself. That's a bet on scarcity — land, grid connections, cooling — rather than on any particular model winning. It is also the part of the AI economy with the longest physical lead times and the least ability to change course, which is worth remembering the next time an NHS procurement is told a cloud contract can be renegotiated later.

From the Eye

Item one — twenty-four hours after STAT published its investigation into Commure, the $7bn health software firm's chief legal officer emailed members of its partnership programme to say their payment agreements would terminate 30 days from receipt; the referral money that had been quietly underwriting all those enthusiastic customer testimonials is being switched off, which rather concedes the point, and is a reminder that the fastest-acting regulator in health tech remains a reporter with documents. Item two — Manus, the Chinese AI agent firm, confirmed on Tuesday it is returning to independence now that Beijing has forced Meta to unwind its $2bn purchase, and buried in the announcement is the detail that to satisfy separation rules it will delete user data created since 29 December 2025, with the wipe scheduled for 23–24 August Singapore time; eight months of customers' work is being erased as a compliance artefact of a deal they had no part in. Item three — Naver has invested in Panthalassa, an Oregon outfit building floating offshore data centres powered by wave energy and cooled by seawater, on the reasoning that the grid queue and the planning system are now harder problems than the engineering; corrosion, storms and servicing a rack in the North Pacific are apparently the easier set.
