THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Thursday 24 September 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 141

On the line today: the lab bosses ask the Security Council to regulate them, hours before Trump meets Xi with an AI hotline on the table; America's first striking hospital doctors settle, with AI among the issues; Heidi raises $340m to turn the scribe into an agent; a scribe-maker's AI "beats" GPs on synthetic cases; and Anthropic and OpenEvidence give clinical AI away free in about 100 poorer countries. From the Eye: a health app at Fashion Week, a lawsuit over the letter N, and the first hospital to pass an AI inspection.

1. The builders ask to be governed

At yesterday's Security Council briefing, Dario Amodei said AI "managed poorly" could be a risk to humanity as a whole. He proposed narrow global bans (starting with AI-assisted bioweapons), a way for states to verify each other's promises, and shared testing standards with incident notification. Sam Altman backed him and said the big decisions "cannot be made by labs in San Francisco alone". Bengio called the dangers "real and imminent". Today Xi meets Trump in Washington, with a US proposal for a bilateral AI incident hotline waiting for a decision at summit level. Trump had just told the General Assembly he rejects any "globalist scheme" to control AI.

Why it matters: a hotline is the most modest idea on the table and the most useful. STAT's AI Prognosis column asked the right question: how much of the doom talk is safety, and how much is pre-IPO positioning?

2. First contract, with AI in the room

About 150 Allina Health doctors at two Minnesota hospitals announced a tentative first contract last night. Last week's four-day walkout is thought to be the first strike by private-sector hospital doctors in US history. After three years of talks, AI was a main issue next to pay and sick leave: doctors said algorithms were creeping into diagnosis and billing. The union is claiming wins on professional autonomy and a grievance process. It gave up on paid sick days.

The view from the surgery: who controls the algorithm is now a bargaining issue, not just a question for the governance committee. The BMA should get scribe and triage AI into the salaried model contract before someone else decides for us.

3. Heidi wants to do more than write the note

Heidi has raised $100m in a Series C led by Blackbird, plus $240m in growth financing from General Catalyst, at a $900m valuation. The money is for "clinician-supervised agents" that fill in order sets, draft referrals and pull a patient's history together before the consultation. Heidi is already sole supplier for NHS England Midlands, covering 15 trusts, every ICB in the region and 1,239 GP practices.

The view: a scribe records what you said. An agent that drafts the referral makes a small clinical decision for you. That calls for a different MHRA classification, a different safety case and a clear answer on liability. Ask for all three before the next update switches it on.

4. Beating GPs on paper

An arXiv preprint pits Doctorina, a clinical AI system, against eight physicians and four frontier models on 150 synthetic primary-care consultations in Polish. Doctorina's top diagnosis matched the reference 82% of the time, against 57% for the doctors, and it also scored higher on workup and treatment. Separately, a Nature Medicine commentary calls current medical AI benchmarks "misleading and insufficient" and proposes a task-based test built on thousands of clinicopathological conferences.

The view from the surgery: a vignette has no history to take, no body language and no worried relative, and no GP gets the case written up in advance. Until these systems are tested on a real Monday list and the outcomes followed up, the 25-point gap measures the benchmark, not the doctoring.

5. Free, if you're poor enough

Anthropic and OpenEvidence are giving doctors in about 100 low- and middle-income countries, including Uganda, Sudan, Haiti and Mongolia, free access to OpenEvidence's clinical question tool, which runs on Claude. OpenEvidence says it will adapt the tool to local disease patterns and to the diagnostics each country actually has. Daniela Amodei said the market alone "would not cause it to happen".

Why it matters: it could help a single-handed doctor in rural Uganda, as long as it isn't giving advice that assumes a CT scanner down the corridor. That depends on the local adaptation, and nobody has published how it will be evaluated.

From the Eye

Catwalk medicine: FreeHealth.ai, which calls itself "the Operating System for Human Health", marked a "healthcare milestone" with a Times Square Fashion Week event. HIStalk points out there was no product launch, no customers and no clinical results. Trademark N: Nitra is suing Notable over its stylised "N" logo, saying customers might confuse the two healthcare AI brands. No patient harm reported yet. The first AI inspection: Hackensack Meridian Health, the first organisation through the Joint Commission's new AI certification, says inspectors checked its AI inventory, risk assessments and monitoring. It advises other hospitals to ask clinicians how comfortable they are with AI, and to write contracts that stop vendors reusing patient data. CQC could borrow both.
