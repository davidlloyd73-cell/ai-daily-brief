THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Monday 21 September 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 138

On the line today: ten professional regulators and the PSA sign a joint statement of intent on how they will police clinicians' use of AI; a Californian class action says the four big labs' agreement to slow down is a cartel; a three-country randomised trial finds GPT-4o lifts doctors' vignette scores most where doctors have least; NHS England hands Netcompany £215m to rebuild screening around the NHS App; and a zero-click flaw in the coding agents leaves Copilot unpatched and Gemini CLI quietly retired. In From the Eye: Suleyman on the China bogeyman, AI reading spirometry in Australian general practice, and the 3 per cent of respiratory apps that do anything.

1. The regulators find each other

On Wednesday the ten statutory health and social care regulators, the GMC and NMC among them, together with the Professional Standards Authority, published a joint statement of intent on AI. It commits them to a shared set of principles for how they will regulate registrants' use of the technology, on the basis that the sector needs "a system-wide approach" and that professional regulation has "an important contribution" to make in keeping public confidence. It is short, and it is a promise to write something rather than the thing itself.

The view from the surgery: this is the piece the MHRA left on the table when it decided scribes are not devices. If the product is unregulated, the regulator regulates the user, and the first fitness-to-practise cases about AI notes are already in the pipeline. Read it as a statement of where the liability will sit, and it sits with you.

2. Slowing down, allegedly in concert

A class action filed on Friday in the Northern District of California accuses Anthropic, OpenAI, Google and SpaceXAI of an antitrust conspiracy. The evidence, such as it is, is public: Dario Amodei's 12 September essay calling for industry-wide deceleration and the approving replies from Altman, Musk and Hassabis, which the complaint says capped months of quieter coordination. The plaintiffs say each company may slow itself, but agreeing to do so together substitutes "collective restraint for individual accountability" and shortchanges paying subscribers. Lead counsel Nick Rowley also says AI "could kill us all", which is an unusual argument for making it go faster.

Why it matters: the same week the labs floated a FINRA-style self-regulator, a court is being asked whether safety coordination is price-fixing. If the answer is yes, the only lawful place for restraint is a statute, which is precisely what Washington has declined to write.

3. The bigger the gap, the bigger the lift

npj Digital Medicine has published a randomised trial of 249 physicians across Indonesia, Kenya and the Netherlands, each given four primary-care vignettes with or without access to GPT-4o. The model's help improved scores everywhere, but unevenly: 7.2 percentage points in the Netherlands, 10.7 in Indonesia and 18 in Kenya. It is English-language vignettes, not patients, so the usual caveat about scores and outcomes stands.

The view: the gradient is the finding. Assistance is worth most where baseline training and supervision are thinnest, which in the NHS means the ARRS pharmacist, the PA and the out-of-hours locum at 3am, not the senior partner who gets the demo. Deploy accordingly.

4. Screening, rebuilt round the App

Public Technology reports that NHS England's contract for "adult screening and personalisation digital delivery services" went live on 1 September, awarded to the Danish firm Netcompany and valued at £215m. The supplier is to build new digital channels for reaching screening participants, with a stated focus on under-screened groups, all "enabled by the NHS App", and to support trials of AI tools reading breast and other screening images.

Why it matters: the App is now the front door for triage, screening invitations and, per Friday's pilots, the GP consultation. The under-screened groups this money targets are the ones least likely to have it installed. Somebody should ask what happens to the letter.

5. Zero clicks, four agents, two fixes

Researchers at AIR disclosed Plugin4Shell on Thursday, a zero-click remote code execution flaw in the four leading AI coding agents: Claude Code, OpenAI's Codex, GitHub Copilot and Google's Gemini CLI. A malicious plugin update can slip past the SHA pinning that is supposed to lock an installed extension to its reviewed version, and it runs with everything the developer can reach. Anthropic and OpenAI have patched; Microsoft has not, and Google has retired Gemini CLI without a fix.

The view from the surgery: every trust digital team now has someone building on these tools, often on a laptop that also opens the PAS. "Retired without a fix" is not a patch note you want to find for the thing your integration was written in.

From the Eye

The bogeyman — Microsoft's Mustafa Suleyman told CNN's Fareed Zakaria on Sunday that "I don't think we should use China as the bogeyman" for not building guardrails, arguing for shared safety standards while keeping US development fast, a rebuke of the national-security framing Washington has used to resist regulation, from the man who runs Microsoft's AI. Blowing into the machine — an Australian study at the European Respiratory Society congress ran AI interpretation over spirometry from more than 1,000 primary-care patients and reached 84 per cent sensitivity and 86 per cent specificity against clinician diagnosis, which is respectable, if you have a practice nurse who can produce an acceptable trace and a pathway that does something with the answer. Three in a hundred — a systematic review presented alongside it found that of more than 100 respiratory apps, 3 per cent meaningfully supported patient self-monitoring; the other 97 support the app store.
