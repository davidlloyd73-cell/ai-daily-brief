THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Sunday 23 August 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 114

On the line today: Washington has published what the industry told it about clinical AI, and the answer is mostly "help us work out what's any good"; a peer-reviewed evaluation of chatbots inside an NHS Talking Therapies service finds the useful bit isn't the therapy; a company with $60m raised and a $3.8bn valuation says it can predict how biology changes; AT&T has halved its AI bill by sending the easy questions to cheaper models; and ChatGPT has been let into people's text messages. From the Eye: what British publishers are quietly signing away, where Brazil is buying its compute, and the startup that wants to clone you at work.

1. Washington's answer on clinical AI is a shopping list

The US Department of Health and Human Services has published its summary of responses to the sweeping request for information it opened in December on accelerating the adoption of AI in clinical care. Three themes dominated. The sector wants HHS agencies to coordinate with one another rather than issue overlapping guidance — one respondent's version was that the right hand doesn't talk to the left. It wants practical help with implementation and governance. And above all it wants evaluation and benchmarking tools, so that buyers can tell which products actually work. Alongside the summary, the Administration for Community Living opened a competition for AI tools supporting carers of older and disabled people, and ARPA-H confirmed it is building AI agents intended to manage cardiovascular disease care autonomously.

The view from the surgery: the striking admission is that after two years of enthusiastic deployment nobody can reliably distinguish a good tool from a bad one, and industry is now asking government to build the yardstick it declined to build for itself. Note also what this exercise is not. The framing throughout is adoption and cost, not safety. ARPA-H's autonomous cardiovascular agent is the one to watch — titrating antihypertensives and statins is exactly the sort of protocolised, numerical work an agent might do well, and exactly the sort where a quiet drift in performance takes months to show up in anyone's outcome data.

2. The chatbot that works is the one before the therapy

A service evaluation published in Counselling and Psychotherapy Research examined two automated chatbots piloted within a single NHS Talking Therapies provider — one designed to prepare patients for therapy, the other offering solution-focused coaching about a specific problem while they waited. The interesting result is not symptom improvement. It is retention: the tools kept people engaged with the service during the wait for a first appointment, in a pathway where dropping out before session one is the largest single source of lost treatment. Separately, services using Limbic's self-referral chatbot have reported a 15% rise in referrals against 6% in matched controls.

Why it matters: this is the honest version of AI in mental health, and it is more useful than the hyped one. Nobody is claiming the software does therapy. It does the thing a waiting list does badly, which is keep hold of the patient. The uncomfortable corollary is that the intervention only has value because the wait exists — we are automating the management of a queue rather than shortening it, and someone should be measuring whether that makes the queue easier to tolerate politically.

3. Sixty million raised, valued at $3.8bn, on a promise about biology

Astromech has come out of stealth with $20m at a $3.8bn valuation, bringing total capital raised to $60m. It was co-founded by the entrepreneur Ben Lamm and the Harvard geneticist George Church, and it is not training on language or images. The pitch is to combine genomic, evolutionary and functional biological data into models that predict how biological systems change over time — where organisms become vulnerable, how traits emerge, how diseases and populations might shift. The claimed applications run from drug discovery and ageing research to disease resistance and pandemic preparedness. The company is candid that it remains research-heavy and that substantial scientific validation lies ahead.

The view: sixty-three times the money raised, for a model that has not yet been shown to predict anything. That is the market pricing George Church's name and the possibility that predictive biology becomes a category. It may well. But note the direction of travel — after a decade of AI that describes biology, capital is now backing AI that claims to forecast it, and forecasting is where confident wrongness does the most damage.

4. AT&T works out it was buying the wrong model

AT&T has cut the cost of coding and other advanced AI tasks by as much as 56% by routing employee queries to cheaper models whenever the job doesn't need a premium frontier one. Performance quality fell by 2%. Open-weight models — Meta's Llama and Google's Gemma among them — now handle around 40% of employee AI requests, with a target of 60–70% within a few years; developers can still reach for Anthropic or OpenAI when the work demands it. Goldman Sachs' Jim Covello argues this helps rather than threatens the hyperscalers, because cheaper inference makes far more usage affordable and fills the capacity they are spending billions to build.

The view from the surgery: 56% for 2% is a trade any practice manager would take without blinking. The read-across is direct. Most of what a clinical AI does is not hard — summarising a discharge letter, coding a consultation, drafting a routine referral. If the NHS is paying frontier-model prices for tasks that a small open model does at 98% of the quality, that is a very large amount of money buying very little extra, and nobody in procurement is currently asked the question.

5. ChatGPT moves into the messages

OpenAI has begun rolling out a Messages integration for ChatGPT on macOS, letting the assistant read, search, summarise, draft and send messages once the user grants permission. OpenAI says the data is processed locally and that it does not build a separate index of conversations. The company is not the only one doing this — the pattern across the industry is assistants moving out of their own window and into email, calendars, documents and browsers. The obvious hazard is prompt injection: an instruction hidden inside an incoming message that the assistant reads as a command rather than as content.

Why it matters: the granularity of consent is the whole problem. Granting an assistant access to your messages grants it access to every message, including the ones sent to you by people who never agreed to any of this. For anyone whose phone carries clinical content — and that is most of us, whatever the policy says — this is worth thinking about before the same integration arrives in NHS mail, which it will.

From the Eye

Item one: in June the Competition and Markets Authority ruled that UK publishers should be able to opt out of having their content used to train Google's generative AI without their conventional search rankings suffering. Since then Google has been signing publishers to licensing deals that hand over broad rights — including training — in exchange for cash, bundled with agreements not to sue and not to discuss the terms. Press Gazette calls it a prisoner's dilemma, which is generous; it is a demonstration that a regulator can win a right and the market can extinguish it by private contract within weeks, and that nothing in the ruling required anyone to keep the right they were given. Item two: Brazil has announced roughly $444m of AI investment involving both American and Chinese partners, including some $250m for a supercomputing project with Huawei and the Chinese AI firm iFlytek. Emerging economies are quietly discovering they have leverage precisely because they will take compute from either bloc, and the assumption that sovereign AI capacity means choosing a side is looking parochial. Item three: Twin1 has launched with $20m to build "digital twins" of individual knowledge workers — systems that answer questions and carry out tasks based on one named employee's knowledge and judgement, plugged into Slack, aimed first at legal and professional services. Nobody has yet explained who owns the twin when the employee resigns.
