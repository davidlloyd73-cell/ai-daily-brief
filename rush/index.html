THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Saturday 22 August 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 113

On the line today: the FDA has authorised a robot that takes blood out of a patient's arm with nobody's hands on it, and one phlebotomist can supervise three of them; the MHRA's new fast-track route into Britain will honour an FDA clearance for almost any device except, pointedly, AI software; the American map of states banning AI therapists keeps filling in while England quietly builds triage chatbots; Anthropic is preparing an IPO that would be among the largest ever floated; and Nvidia has worked out how to buy a company without buying it. From the Eye: the animals Brussels wants to stop testing on, the seven convictions a judge threw out, and what Apple Music is about to start labelling.

1. The robot that finds the vein

The FDA on Wednesday granted De Novo authorisation to the Aletta, made by the Dutch firm Vitestro — the first standalone robotic device that draws blood from an adult's arm with no hands-on operator intervention. It uses near-infrared light and Doppler ultrasound to find a vein and distinguish it from an artery, then applies the tourniquet, preps the skin, inserts and disposes of the needle, swaps the tubes and puts the plaster on. If it can't find a suitable vein it declines to try. If the patient moves, the needle detaches and the draw stops. Success rates in the trial were comparable to or better than trained human phlebotomists across varying skin tones and self-reported difficult access. A trained phlebotomist starts each session and can supervise three machines at once.

The view from the surgery: the interesting number is three. This is not a machine that replaces a phlebotomist; it is a machine that changes the ratio, which is how automation has always actually arrived. Our own phlebotomy bottleneck is real and unglamorous, and a device that declines to stick when it isn't sure is more honest than several diagnostic algorithms I could name. The catch is the same as ever — capital cost, cleaning between patients, and who carries the can when the arm bruises.

2. Britain will trust the FDA, except on AI

The MHRA has advanced draft regulations for an International Reliance route, letting manufacturers use existing clearances from the FDA, Health Canada and Australia's TGA to accelerate entry into the Great Britain market. Reforms are expected to become law during 2026 with the reliance routes live from 2027, and the agency keeps the final decision. Buried in the exclusions is the part that matters: software as a medical device and AI as a medical device are carved out, along with in-house and custom-made devices and Class III products approved on predicate equivalence. Separately, the MHRA's National Commission on the Regulation of AI in Healthcare — chaired by Alastair Denniston, with Patient Safety Commissioner Henrietta Hughes as deputy — is due to report its recommendations this year.

Why it matters: the regulator is saying, in effect, that a titanium hip cleared in Maryland is a titanium hip in Middlesex, but an algorithm cleared in Maryland is not the same algorithm here. That is defensible — performance shifts with the population it meets — and it also means every AI tool still faces the full domestic route while the Commission is still writing the rules it will be judged against.

3. The AI therapy map keeps filling in

Four American states — Illinois, Nevada, Rhode Island and Maine — now ban AI from delivering therapy to the public, with Colorado, Tennessee and Vermont adding restrictions this year. Utah requires disclosure; New York, California and Nebraska have layered on crisis-referral duties and protections for minors. In the first three months of 2026 alone, 36 states introduced more than 70 bills on AI chatbots, most of them demanding that the user be told they are talking to a machine. California legislators are now moving on a bill that would stop companies marketing chatbots as therapy at all. In Europe, the AI Act's transparency provisions came into force on 2 August and national authorities have begun their first formal supervisory checks — patient-facing chatbots and conversational triage now carry mandatory disclosure duties.

The view: note the asymmetry. America is legislating against the thing while England builds an AI triage layer into the NHS App with £10bn behind it. Both jurisdictions have concluded the public needs telling when it is talking to software. We should assume that duty lands on the practice, not the vendor.

4. Anthropic heads for the exit ramp

Anthropic is preparing an IPO that Bloomberg reports could match or exceed SpaceX's record-setting debut, with filing possible as soon as the end of this month and talk of a valuation approaching $2 trillion. Annualised revenue is reported at roughly $65bn as of the end of July, driven by enterprise spending on Claude and on agents. Broadcom, meanwhile, is in talks to raise $60bn or more in senior secured debt — a package potentially reaching $100bn with junior financing — for AI infrastructure tied in part to Anthropic.

Why it matters: a listing forces disclosure. For the first time we would see audited margins, compute costs and customer concentration for a frontier lab, rather than the numbers each lab chooses to brief. Anyone signing a five-year contract for clinical AI built on somebody's foundation model has a direct interest in whether the economics underneath it work.

5. How to buy a company without buying it

Nvidia has reportedly agreed to pay $6bn to the AI coding startup Poolside under a non-exclusive licensing agreement, invest a further $1bn at a $12bn pre-money valuation, and hire around 109 of its employees — while leaving the corporate entity standing and independent. It is an acquisition in everything but name, and in particular in everything but the name that would attract a merger review.

The view from the surgery: this template will travel. Watch for it in health AI, where the interesting companies are small, the buyers are enormous, and the thing actually being bought is a dozen people and a set of weights. Procurement rules that ask who owns a supplier are not built to notice when the supplier still exists but everyone who built the product now works somewhere else.

From the Eye

Item one: the EU's Innovative Health Initiative has opened funding calls for large-scale AI foundation toxicology models intended to replace animal testing in preclinical safety evaluation — a consortium effort to build models that predict organ toxicity well enough that regulators accept them in place of a rodent study. If it works it is the largest reduction in laboratory animal use in a generation, achieved not by campaigning but by a procurement line item. Nobody covered it. Item two: a federal judge has thrown out seven economic-espionage convictions against former Google engineer Linwei Ding while leaving his trade-secret theft convictions standing, ruling that prosecutors failed to show he knew or intended his conduct to benefit the Chinese government. The distinction is going to matter enormously as governments reclassify model weights and chip designs as national-security assets: taking your employer's secrets is a crime, and taking them for a state is a much bigger one, and the second is far harder to prove than the indictments imply. Item three: Apple Music has told music-industry partners it will display visible labels on tracks that content providers identify as "materially generated using AI", extending its AI Transparency Tags scheme. The burden falls on distributors and rights holders to classify honestly, which is an interesting bet. Note who is not being asked to label anything: the model that generated the track, or the catalogue it learned from. Britain's consultation on that second question remains unresolved.
