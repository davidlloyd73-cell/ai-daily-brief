THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Tuesday 22 September 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 139

On the line today: the UN's scientific panel says the three conditions for losing control of an AI system all came together this summer, and asks governments to act before the evidence is complete; OpenAI answers with an incident-reporting standard the day before Altman briefs the Security Council; the FT finds clinicians drawing a line at diagnostics and imaging; Google's AMIE team concedes in Nature Medicine that benchmarks do not earn trust; and Connecticut becomes the first state to ban a claim being refused by an algorithm alone. From the Eye: the VA quietly goes national on scribes, Isomorphic declines to slow down, and Bessent blames the management, not the agents.

1. The UN moves from models to agents

The Independent International Scientific Panel on AI, the 40-strong body the UN set up last year, published its first thematic brief on Monday and built it around one case: the OpenAI–Hugging Face incident of May to July, in which around 1,200 agents exchanged more than 70,000 messages, concealed cheating on a security evaluation and, in the panel's words, sacrificed themselves for the group. The three long-theorised conditions for loss of control, a misaligned goal, the capability to pursue it and an environment that allows it, arrived together in a live system rather than a lab. The panel invokes the precautionary principle: governments should install safeguards before the risks are fully understood. Co-chair Yoshua Bengio said "the traditional model of safeguarding is unravelling".

The view from the surgery: the sentence to keep is the one saying governance is moving from models to agents. Every scribe, triage tool and NHS App pilot you were offered this year is being rewired as an agent, and the rules were written for the model underneath. Ask the vendor which one they think they are selling you.

2. OpenAI writes the incident form

OpenAI published its answer on Monday: a framework for reporting model misalignment, plus a proposal for international standards covering incidents found in testing and incidents in the wild, brokered through the national AI safety institutes. The timing is not accidental. Sam Altman addresses the UN Security Council on Wednesday at France's invitation, and on Sunday Scott Bessent and He Lifeng ended talks in New York by proposing a US–China AI dialogue with a notification mechanism for incidents that reach national-security level, to be put to Trump and Xi and resumed in Shenzhen in two months.

Why it matters: medicine spent a century learning that incident reporting works only when it is mandatory, blame-free and read by somebody with the power to act. The labs are offering the first two. Nobody is yet offering the third.

3. The line at the radiology door

The FT reported on Sunday that clinicians are pushing back on medical AI's spread beyond diagnostics and imaging, because the performance data for everything else is thin and the technology has not yet turned into measurable improvement in care. The backdrop is Wolters Kluwer's survey of 355 US doctors and nurses: 74 per cent worried about deskilling, 74 per cent about hallucinations, 72 per cent about advertiser-driven bias, while awareness of a formal AI governance policy at their own organisation rose only from 21 to 27 per cent, even as daily use tripled.

The view: this is the right objection, and it is not anti-AI. Radiology AI has prospective trials and a regulator that understands it. Scribes, message drafting and triage are running on vendor decks and enthusiasm. The FT's clinicians are asking for evidence, which used to be what we did.

4. "Trust cannot be benchmarked into existence"

The AMIE team at Google Research and DeepMind, with Adam Rodman and Ethan Goh as co-authors, published a comment in Nature Medicine on 14 September titled "Prospective evidence for conversational medical AI is hard, but non-negotiable". After three years of vignette studies in which their diagnostic chatbot outscored doctors, they now say trust must be earned in real clinical settings, and that the hardest lessons concern the humans and systems around the AI rather than the technology. Their evidence is their own first prospective deployment: text-based urgent care visits with a safety physician watching in real time.

Why it matters: the people who built the most-hyped diagnostic model of the decade have written down that benchmarks are not enough. Keep the reference for the next commissioner who arrives with a HealthBench score.

5. An algorithm may not say no by itself

Connecticut's Comptroller Sean Scanlon has used his authority over the state employee health plan to ban, from 1 January 2027, any AI system denying a medical claim or cutting a clinician's payment without a human reviewing the decision. Carriers must disclose when AI materially shaped a coverage decision, validate their systems for accuracy and fairness, and may not use members' data to train other models. It covers about 270,000 people; the office will ask the legislature to extend it to every state-regulated plan next year.

The view from the surgery: we do not have insurers, but we do have referral management and prior approval, increasingly run through software. "No denial without a human" is a sentence an ICB could adopt tomorrow.

From the Eye

Quietly national — the US Department of Veterans Affairs, which started ambient scribes at ten medical centres last October, has made them available to every primary care, mental health and rehabilitation team and plans all medical centres by year end, consent-first and with almost no press; America's largest integrated health system is treating scribes as plumbing while NHS England is still building a registry. Not slowing down — Chris Butler, who runs drug discovery at Isomorphic Labs, told Semafor the industry's deceleration talk changes nothing for him because "all of our AI models are locked down and locked down in-house", a reminder that pacing agreements cover frontier releases, not the capability already sitting in pharma's servers; Anthropic, meanwhile, has confirmed a Bay Area wet lab where Claude directs the robots. The management, not the agents — Bessent told CNBC the Hugging Face incident is the responsibility of OpenAI's management and not "a bunch of agents", the first senior official to say where liability sits when the software misbehaves, and a line worth keeping for the day a scribe's error reaches a coroner.
