THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Thursday 26 November 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 196

On the line today: Page fetching is still blocked, so this rests on search summaries and I flag the thin spots. No fresh NHS lead that I could confirm, so we start in Ohio, where a mother is suing a children's hospital over an AI scribe that recorded her children's appointments; five free chatbots are tested on cauda equina against the NHS pathway; American parents are cool on therapy chatbots for their children; the FCA tells tech firms that an AI agent which moves money is a regulated business; and Jensen Huang's return reopens Washington's argument over open-weight models. From the Eye: what Reddit says about scribes, Amazon's $7.5 billion purchase of no models at all, and the premium on being human.

1. The scribe, the children and the vendor

A mother filed a proposed class action on 20 November in the federal court for the Southern District of Ohio, on behalf of herself and her two children, against Dayton Children's Hospital. The complaint alleges that the hospital used an AI documentation tool to record paediatric consultations and send them to the vendor without meaningful notice or her informed consent. Bloomberg Law names the vendor as Abridge and reports the further allegation that the recordings were used to train its models. I found no response from the hospital or from Abridge, and these are allegations only.

The view from the surgery: Our own guidance already says to tell the patient the scribe is listening. The harder question, which this case asks, is what the supplier keeps afterwards and what it does with it, and few of us could answer that for the tool on our own desk.

2. Five chatbots and the cauda equina question

A blinded study in Frontiers in Digital Health put five free chatbots (ChatGPT, Claude, Grok, Perplexity and Microsoft Copilot) through symptom enquiries scored against the national GIRFT cauda equina pathway. All five advised emergency escalation for every enquiry consistent with the syndrome. The cost was over-referral: eight of 25 responses to scenarios without the syndrome, 32%, still urged same-day or emergency assessment with no red flag present, most of them in the grey-zone cases. No platform was clearly better than another. I have the abstract, not the paper, and one write-up counts the 25 differently.

Why it matters: Missing none is the right way round for a condition where delay means a catheter for life and a seven-figure claim. But a third of the worried well sent to A&E for an MRI is a real cost, and it lands on us and on the on-call spinal team.

3. Parents are not sold on the therapy chatbot

A national survey from Harvard and Boston Children's Hospital, run with RAND and published in JAMA Pediatrics, finds American parents reluctant to let an AI chatbot support their child's mental health. Their chief concerns were diagnostic accuracy, whether the chatbot shows empathy, and whether a clinician had recommended it. One report gives the sample as 572 parents and says about a quarter were comfortable; I could not confirm either figure at source. The Harvard write-up sets this beside an earlier finding that 19% of 12-to-21-year-olds already use a chatbot for mental health advice, often without telling their parents.

The view: The parents are answering a question their children settled some time ago. Note what would change their minds, though: a professional's recommendation, which means that sooner or later they will ask us.

4. The FCA reads the small print on agents

Meta launched its personal AI agent, Muse, in Britain on Tuesday, and three regulators replied the same day. The Financial Conduct Authority, naming no company, said that calling yourself a technology provider is no exemption: an agent that makes payments, brokers credit, promotes financial products or gives advice may need authorisation, at launch or as its abilities grow. It will write no AI-specific rules. The Competition and Markets Authority said consumer law applies to such agents, and the Information Commissioner said it will monitor the rollout. Secondary reports say the British version cannot yet reach a user's bank account.

The take: This is the sensible British habit of regulating what a thing does, whatever it is called. I should like to see the same clarity about an agent that books the appointment, describes the symptoms and chases the result.

5. Huang returns, and so does the open-weight argument

Reuters reported on Tuesday that Jensen Huang's reinstatement at Nvidia has pushed open-weight models, those anyone may download and run, to the front of Washington's AI debate. Huang has urged the government to avoid "premature restrictions" on them. Anthropic declined to sign his statement, and Dario Amodei has argued that capable open models carry security risks, biological ones included. The White House says it supports open-weight models, while it considers making American companies disclose their use of Chinese-made models.

Why it matters: Open models sell more chips and closed ones sell subscriptions, so nobody in this argument is disinterested. The Chinese medical model I described yesterday is exactly the kind of thing being argued over.

From the Eye

Item one A study in PLOS Digital Health analysed clinicians' own Reddit posts about AI scribes and concluded that the tools move the work, towards setting up, checking and pasting notes into the record by hand, more than they remove it. Self-selected posters, and I have a summary only. Item two Amazon is buying the Israeli startup Decart for a reported $7.5 billion, a figure from the business paper Calcalist which Amazon has not confirmed. It gets the optimisation software and the engineers, to make its own chips run faster, and none of Decart's AI models. Item three Exponential View's Sunday issue was headed "Certified human" and argues, on the summary I have, that human-made work is becoming a scarce premium tier. General practice may wish to note the pricing.
