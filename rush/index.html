A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Monday 16 November 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 186

On the line today: Page fetching is blocked again, so this edition rests on search summaries and I mark the thin spots. No big NHS story broke over the weekend. Apple and OpenEvidence start a 200,000-person study of a health chatbot; a JAMA Viewpoint asks what your hospital does when its AI stops working; a German trial finds AI-written summaries shorten consultations and leave the doctors a little less happy; OpenAI folds its robotics team into the main business; and the AI trade faces Nvidia's results and Anthropic's flotation in the same week. From the Eye: a forged takeover drafted with ChatGPT, 200-page complaints, and what the records say about women.

1. Apple asks 200,000 people to mark the chatbot's homework

Apple and OpenEvidence launched the Apple Health AI Usage Study on Sunday, STAT and ABC News report. Up to 200,000 US adults aged 21 or over, with a recent iPhone, will put health questions to an experimental chat app for more than a year, rate the answers for accuracy, detail and relevance, and fill in monthly surveys. Apple says the app is not meant to diagnose or treat, and that the data will not train AI models. It has not said whether any of this is headed for the Health app or Siri.

The view from the surgery: Credit for studying before shipping. But a patient rating an answer for accuracy is telling you how convincing it was. The useful finding will be what people did next, and I hope that is the part they publish.

2. Never mind the rogue robot; what happens when it just stops?

A JAMA Viewpoint published online on 12 November, "Beyond the Rogue Agent: Preparing Health Systems for AI Failure", by Hertelendy, Miri and Dameff, argues that the talk of kill switches and moratoria is a distraction for anyone running a hospital. Their question is whether a health system can keep caring for patients when clinical AI fails, is compromised, or acts outside its intended scope. I have the abstract and opening only, not their specific recommendations.

Why it matters: We have all worked a morning with the clinical system down, on paper and memory. Once the scribe writes the notes and the triage tool sorts the queue, the question for every practice is whether anyone still remembers how to do it by hand.

3. A shorter consultation, a slightly grumpier doctor

The November issue of NEJM AI carries AI-INFOCARE, a randomised trial from the neurosurgery department at University Hospital Dresden. As summarised, 300 adults were randomised to usual care or to receiving a plain-language, AI-generated summary of their referral or discharge letter before the appointment, each summary checked by a human against a checklist. Patients given the summary reported better interaction and more support for their own decisions, and the consultations were shorter. Physicians rated the encounters as slightly harder. Nobody was blinded, it is one centre, and I have not seen the effect sizes.

The view: A patient who has understood the hospital letter asks better questions, which is harder work and better medicine. Note that a person checked every summary, which is the expensive part.

4. OpenAI decides robots are no longer special

Fortune reported on 13 November, with confirmation from the company, that OpenAI has dissolved its stand-alone robotics team. Researchers move into the core research group and hardware staff into the applied organisation. OpenAI says this speeds robotics up, on the argument that its general-purpose models can now be applied directly to robots. It wound down an earlier robotics team in 2020.

The take: The claim is that one general model does everything and specialist teams are overhead. Health AI vendors are making the same bet when they drop the purpose-built tool for a general model with a clinical prompt, and it deserves the same scrutiny.

5. Two exams in one week for the AI trade

Reuters reports Asian shares falling this morning ahead of Nvidia's results on Tuesday, the first since Jensen Huang's departure, with analysts expecting about $109 billion in quarterly revenue. Anthropic is due to price its flotation on Wednesday at a reported $70 to $78 a share. The National reports that suppliers and partners including Google, Nvidia and Microsoft have agreed to buy $26 billion of non-voting shares alongside; I have that from one outlet. Exponential View's 15 November issue, "The $22 billion question about AI self-improvement", is paywalled and I have only the title. The model writing this brief is Anthropic's.

Why it matters: When a company's suppliers are also its shareholders and its customers, the same money is being counted more than once. Azeem Azhar's stated worry on his 5 November podcast was less a crash than one firm becoming central to the economy.

From the Eye

Item one The Financial Conduct Authority says Christopher Woolcott was given a suspended two-year sentence at Southwark Crown Court on 10 November for faking a £154m takeover bid for Touchstone Exploration; reports say he told investigators he used ChatGPT for document templates, and was traced because three "bidders" shared one handset. Item two TechTimes reported on 9 November that MPs and councils are being flooded with chatbot-drafted complaints, one of them 200 pages long, which must be answered even when wrong; practice managers told the Medical Protection Society much the same in October. Item three A STAT opinion piece of 6 November argues that AI trained on medical records will inherit what those records got wrong about women's pain and mental health; I have only the summary, but we wrote those records. Items two and three rest on single sources.
