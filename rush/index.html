THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Saturday 31 October 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 175

On the line today: STAT reports Washington scrambling over state-run health AI sandboxes; the MHRA's ruling on AI scribes is worth rereading before your next vendor call; STAT's investigation into Epic's grip on patient data; Google's cyber-focused Gemini 4 Carbon goes to defenders only; and the courtroom count of AI-invented case law keeps climbing. From the Eye: a model with two names, a medical-board workgroup with a frontier lab in it, and a two-year suspension.

1. Washington argues with its own states over health AI

STAT reported yesterday that federal health officials are working behind the scenes to head off a clash between the FDA and CMS on one side and US states on the other, as states look to test AI in clinical settings through regulatory sandboxes. The piece is behind STAT's paywall and I could read only the summary, so the detail is thin. The underlying tension is plain enough: states want to let clinical AI out of the gate faster, and federal rules were not written with sandboxes in mind. It follows STAT's earlier report that a Federation of State Medical Boards workgroup is drafting guidance on medical AI.

The view from the surgery: We have our own version of this in the MHRA's AI Airlock. The lesson from across the Atlantic is that a sandbox without a clear exit route into routine use just moves the argument to a different committee.

2. The scribe ruling worth knowing by heart

The MHRA's guidance on ambient voice technology, published on 29 July and reported by the BMJ on 10 August, says products that only transcribe, summarise, draft letters or suggest codes for a clinician to review are not medical devices. Anything that supports diagnosis, treatment or automated clinical action is. NHS England, which had treated all such products as devices, has withdrawn its earlier position. The guidance applies to Great Britain only and, per one law-firm commentator, is interpretive rather than a change in the law. It is not new today, but with the Midlands deal for Heidi covering around 1,239 practices now reported, it is the rule most of you will be working under.

Why it matters: No device status means no device-style regulatory scrutiny, so the verification burden lands on the clinician who signs the note. Critics argue that filtering audio is itself a clinical judgement. I would check what your indemnifier says in writing, not what the vendor says on a slide.

3. Epic and the question of who owns the data

STAT's investigation, published on 28 October, reports that Epic selectively restricts access to patient data in ways that disadvantage rival software developers, according to hospital executives and app developers it interviewed. Epic says it offers more data connections than federal law requires. Behind that sits a reported FTC investigation (from anonymous sources, per Reuters in August), a Texas Attorney General suit filed in December 2025, and Particle Health's private action, which survived a motion to dismiss. None of these has produced a finding.

The view: This is a US story, but the principle travels. Any AI tool is only as good as the data it can reach, and whoever controls the record controls which tools get a look in. Worth remembering when a supplier tells you its product "integrates".

4. Google's cyber model goes to defenders first

Google announced Gemini 4 Carbon on 28 October, with a rollout first to a selected group of cyber defenders and later to other trusted partners; there is no date for the public API or consumers. Secondary coverage reports self-reported scores of 88.2% on a vulnerability-identification benchmark, 54.8% on exploitation and 71.0% on fixing vulnerable code. I could not reach Google's own announcement, so treat those numbers as claims. The gated release is itself the story: a lab is saying a model is too useful to attackers to hand out freely.

Why it matters: The same capability that finds holes in hospital software finds them in the GP system your practice runs on. Yesterday's brief noted £24m for public-sector AI cyber defence. This is the other side of that ledger.

5. Fake law, real sanctions

Trackers of AI-fabricated material in court filings disagree on the numbers, with one counting 1,598 proceedings worldwide by May and another over 1,227 cases by June, which tells you how loosely anyone is counting. What is consistent is the direction: courts increasingly treat unchecked AI citations as a failure of verification, not an honest accident, and sanctions are growing. This is not new today, but it is a good model of what a medico-legal complaint about an unchecked AI-drafted record could look like.

The view from the surgery: Swap "citation" for "clinical note" and you have the GMC's likely question: did you read it before you signed it?

From the Eye

Item one Google's new model has been reported by different outlets as both "Gemini 4 Carbon" and "Gemini 4 Argon", which is an impressive amount of confusion for a product whose pitch is precision. Item two The Federation of State Medical Boards' workgroup on medical AI, per STAT, has 22 members including executives from the AMA, the American Hospital Association, Elevance Health and Anthropic, so the regulated and the regulator-in-waiting are sharing a table. Item three One roundup reports a Mississippi federal court suspending two lead attorneys for two years in June over AI-invented citations; I could not confirm it against the court record, but it is a long time to be out of practice for trusting a chatbot.
