THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Wednesday 2 September 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 122

On the line today: UCL researchers put a number on how little of the NHS's digital estate has been safety-assured, and the number is 17 per cent; the FDA clears a surgical "cockpit" that runs on Apple's headset; the Pentagon switches on ChatGPT and Grok for three million people; Brussels fires its first formal demands at the frontier labs four weeks into having the power; and America's health-plan scorekeeper hands measure development to an AI platform. From the Eye: the states legislating that a chatbot may not call itself a nurse, the firms that have stopped buying software because they now write it, and Europe's £330m answer to the compute problem.

1. Seventeen per cent

Researchers at UCL, writing in BMJ Innovations, argue that the NHS 10 Year Health Plan "risks propagating patient harm at an unprecedented scale" — not because the technology is bad, but because the safety architecture underneath it barely exists. Clinical risk assessment of digital health technology has been a statutory expectation since the Health and Social Care Act 2012. Their finding: only around 17 per cent of digital deployments in the English NHS have documented evidence of safety assurance against those standards. They propose CQC enforcement, National Quality Board standards, a professionalised digital-safety workforce, and digital safety taught at undergraduate level.

The view from the surgery: we are being handed £10bn of technology and a governance system that five out of six deployments apparently ignore. Every practice manager reading this knows the drill — the tool arrives, someone signs something, and the DCB0160 assessment is a document nobody has seen. The paper's real service is converting a shrug into a number you can put in front of a board.

2. The surgeon puts the headset on

The FDA has cleared Stryker's "surgical cockpit", which runs on Apple's Vision Pro — the first serious sign that the headset Apple could not sell to consumers has found its market in an operating theatre. The idea is to put imaging, implant planning and device controls in the surgeon's field of view rather than on a trolley across the room, with the sterile field left intact.

Why it matters: theatre displays have been a solved problem badly for thirty years, and this is a plausible fix rather than a demo. Two things to watch: whether surgeons tolerate three hours in a headset, and whether "cleared" here means the imaging is validated or merely that it is displayed somewhere new. Regulatory clearance of a screen is not clearance of what appears on it.

3. Three million new users, one careful login

The Pentagon flipped the switch on Monday, adding OpenAI's ChatGPT Mil and xAI's Grok for Government to GenAI.mil, its enterprise portal, alongside the Google Gemini that launched it. More than three million military and civilian personnel now have access; 1.7 million have already onboarded. Access is gated by Common Access Card, restricted to government kit on the unclassified network, and accredited to Impact Level 5 for controlled unclassified information.

The view: this is the deployment model the NHS keeps failing to build — one accredited front door, several vendors behind it, no data leaving the estate. Set aside what you think of the customer; a health service that gave every clinician a single logged, accredited AI entry point would have solved most of its shadow-AI problem in a fortnight. Instead we have 27 scribe products and a self-certified register.

4. Brussels starts asking questions

Four weeks after the EU AI Act's general-purpose obligations became enforceable on 2 August, the AI Office has sent formal requests for information to providers of general-purpose models, covering model security, independent external evaluation and how models are monitored once on the market. These are Article 55 powers being exercised for the first time: the Office can demand documentation, commission its own evaluations, require mitigations, and fine up to 3 per cent of global turnover or force a model off the market.

The view from the surgery: the interesting question is not whether the labs answer, but what "independent external evaluation" turns out to mean in practice — because whatever Brussels settles on will become the template every health regulator borrows, the MHRA included. The EU is doing the expensive definitional work; we will import the result.

5. The scorekeeper hires the machine

NCQA, which sets the HEDIS measures against which American health plans covering 236 million people are judged, has signed a multi-year deal with Komodo Health to build future measures using Komodo's 330-million-patient claims map and its AI analytics platform. Measure development that took months of field testing with individual payers now runs across Medicare, Medicaid and commercial populations in one pass.

Why it matters: faster and broader is real. But quality measurement works because it is slow, contested and visible, and the risk is measures optimised against the peculiarities of one commercial dataset. QOF's indicators were argued about in public for years; that friction was a feature. NCQA says it remains solely responsible for the measures, which is the right answer and also the one that will be tested.

From the Eye

The unlicensed nurse — a quiet wave of US state legislation now makes it unlawful for an AI to claim a clinical title. Delaware's HB 191 bars any non-human entity from being licensed as a nurse, physician or PA, or from using the protected titles; Tennessee bans marketing a chatbot as capable of acting as a licensed mental health professional; Idaho and Nebraska add disclosure and crisis-response duties from 2027. Nobody in the UK has thought to legislate this, presumably because nobody imagined needing to. The software that never got bought — McKinsey's State of AI in 2026 finds 32 per cent of organisations have skipped purchasing at least one software product or feature because they could build it internally with agentic coding tools. Applied to NHS procurement that is either liberation or an unmaintained estate of one-off apps, and probably both. The Finnish answer — EuroHPC signed a €387.8m contract on 31 August for LUMI-AI in Kajaani, on AMD silicon. Europe's response to compute dependence is a supercomputer in a former paper mill town, cooled by Finnish winter and heating the district in return, which is at least a plan.
