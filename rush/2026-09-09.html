A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Wednesday 9 September 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 129

On the line today: The Health Bill gets two days on the Commons floor and the amendments that matter are about who holds the data, who owns the plumbing and whether Palantir is retendered before February; a security firm uses AI to find and weaponise a WeChat bug in two days; the FDA appoints its first deputy commissioner for technology and AI; Google tells 450 million Europeans it has made Search worse on purpose; and Mistral raises €3 billion for sovereign AI the week Parliament debated exactly that. From the Eye: Australia lets you switch the algorithm off, 220 million records behind a default password, and a UK consultation on being managed by software.

1. The Health Bill, and the amendments worth watching

The bill abolishing NHS England had two days at report stage, Monday and Tuesday, with third reading to follow — a courtesy usually reserved for contested legislation. Karin Smyth opened with a government clause on visiting rights, but the action is in the backbench amendments to Clause 51, the single patient record. NC32 would hard-wire privacy by design into the SPR and the Federated Data Platform. NC33 would make the NHS own the data-connector software rather than lease it. NC34 would force a competitive retender of the FDP before the February 2027 break. NC109 would require AI inventories, a named senior responsible individual and CQC assessment of AI governance. NC2 to NC5 would risk-assess non-UK suppliers handling patient data. The Health Foundation's briefing asked the question government has not answered: will GPs remain liable for data they no longer control? Division results were not published as this went to press.

The view from the surgery: most will fall, as backbench amendments do. But they are the checklist for the Lords and for your ICB. The SPR arrives via the NHS App from 2028, maternity and frailty first. Ask now who the data controller is on day one.

2. Two days from bug to worm

Security firm Calif has disclosed WeWorm, a proof-of-concept that hijacked WeChat on iOS and Android through an unanswered call — the exploit fires while the phone is still ringing — then used the victim's contacts to call the next target. Working with AI tools, the team found a memory-corruption bug in WeChat's call stack and wrote a working remote-code-execution exploit in about two days, then spent a week turning it into a worm. Tencent was told in July and has patched. Experts told the New York Times a live version could have reached hundreds of millions of accounts within hours.

Why it matters: the number is not 1.4 billion users, it is two days. A small lab plus models did what once took a large team months. Every trust running clinical apps on unpatched phones is now on the wrong side of that arithmetic, and yesterday's national cyber statistics are counting the old threats.

3. The FDA gets an AI deputy commissioner

On Tuesday HHS filled four senior FDA posts at once: Michael Davis for drugs, Karim Mikhail for biologics, Bret Koplow for tobacco, and Jared Seehafer as the agency's first Deputy Commissioner for Technology and Artificial Intelligence. Seehafer co-founded Enzyme, a compliance-software firm for life sciences, came most recently from a San Francisco venture fund, spent the past year as a senior adviser to the agency and had a hand in its white paper on regulating generative-AI devices. The same day STAT reported the FDA had lost another senior digital health official.

The view: a regulator that has spent a year pushing staff to use AI to speed reviews now has a named person accountable for it, drawn from the industry it regulates. The MHRA has a commission, a sandbox and a call for evidence. What it lacks is a name on the door.

4. Google makes Search worse, and says so

Google rolled out redesigned results across the EU this week to comply with the Digital Markets Act after a €460 million self-preferencing fine in July. Rival specialised search engines now sit at the top of the page, hotel and flight carousels lose live prices, and Nick Fox, the executive responsible, told Reuters it was "the largest reduction in quality of service" in the product's 29-year history. Google says earlier DMA remedies already cut free direct-booking traffic to European businesses by 30 per cent. The Commission had given it 60 days, with penalties of up to 5 per cent of worldwide turnover on the table.

The view from the surgery: whatever you think of Google, this is a company running two products, one for users and one for regulators, and telling users which is which. It is the argument the AI labs will make against every European rule for a decade.

5. Sovereign AI finds €3 billion

Mistral has raised €3 billion in a Series D at a valuation above €21 billion, led by Samsung with the EU-backed Scaleup Europe Fund, PSG Equity and ASML alongside — among the largest private technology rounds ever completed in Europe. The pitch is open-weight models and deployments where the customer keeps the data, the infrastructure and the intellectual property; annual recurring revenue is reported to have passed a billion dollars.

Why it matters: the week MPs tabled amendments to prefer UK suppliers for NHS data systems, the only credible European frontier lab took Korean money to stay in the race. Sovereignty is a procurement preference until someone builds the thing. Nobody in Britain has.

From the Eye

Switch it off — Anthony Albanese has confirmed draft Digital Duty of Care legislation giving Australians over 16 a durable choice between an algorithmic feed and a follow-only one, with penalties above A$100 million; the same package would force AI chatbots and games to limit children's exposure to eating-disorder content and misogyny. Critics note that opting out of the ranking still leaves you with whatever you already follow. Default password — a publicly exposed Advance Passenger Information database linked to Vietnam held roughly 220 million passenger and crew records from 2017 to 2026, passport numbers included, reachable through a cloud path protected by default credentials; the breach that gets you rarely needs a zero-day. Managed by software — the government's Make Work Pay consultation on workplace monitoring technologies, covering keystroke tracking, algorithmic management and solely automated decisions about workers, closes on 30 September; practices using AI to score call-handler performance might read it before it reads them.
