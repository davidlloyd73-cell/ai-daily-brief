THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Saturday 4 July 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 066

On the line today: Lord Carter is back for a third bite at pathology, and the missing £450m turns out to be mostly a digital problem. A former US health-system chief says medicine must go autonomous to survive. The team behind last year's most fought-over obesity biotech is founding drug companies run on AI scientists. OpenAI has offered Washington a 5% stake in itself — one way to end an argument with your regulator. And California has bought Claude for the whole state at half price. From the Eye: Anthropic goes chip shopping in Seoul, Google's electricity bill catches Denmark, and Big Tech reinvents itself as a consultancy.

1. Carter rides again: pathology's £450m is stuck in the post

Twenty years after his first review rewired NHS pathology, Lord Carter of Coles has published the Pathology Transformation Review 2026, commissioned by the Institute of Biomedical Science. The headline: finishing the consolidation job could free around £450m a year — a cumulative £1.4bn by 2029/30 — for reinvestment, not cuts. The grimmer detail: two-thirds of trusts reported at least one non-accredited test type last year, cellular pathology vacancies remain stubborn, and only 23% of pathology networks rate as digitally "maturing" or better.

The view from the surgery: Every consultation you do leans on a lab, and the AI everyone promises — algorithms reading slides, flagging the abnormal FBC — cannot land on networks that are three-quarters digitally immature. The £450m story is really a plumbing story, and plumbing never gets the press release.

2. "Autonomous or bust", says the man who ran Geisinger

In a STAT First Opinion this week, Glenn Steele — who ran Pennsylvania's Geisinger system for fifteen years — argues American health care will become autonomous not because the technology is irresistible but because health systems cannot otherwise survive their own cost base. It is at least refreshingly free of the usual "augment, never replace" soothing.

The view: Note what's doing the work in that argument: solvency, not safety or outcomes. When the case for autonomous medicine is an accountant's case, the question to keep asking is the old one — when the autonomous system misses the pulmonary embolus, whose name is on the claim?

3. The obesity-drug winners are now founding AI biotechs

STAT reports that Edison Scientific — whose "AI scientist" runs literature synthesis and hypothesis generation as an agent rather than a chatbot — has been tapped by Population Health Partners, the team behind Metsera, the obesity biotech at the centre of last year's extraordinary pharma bidding war. The plan is not to sell AI tools to drug companies but to found new biotechs with the AI doing a share of the science from day one.

Why it matters: A week after Anthropic pitched Claude at the lab bench, the money that called the obesity wave early is making the same bet. The test is unforgiving: not whether AI writes beautiful preclinical rationales, but whether any of these molecules survive contact with a phase II trial.

4. OpenAI's offer Washington can't refuse — 5% of the company

The Financial Times reports OpenAI has proposed handing the US government an equity stake of roughly 5% — around $42.6bn — under a "Public Wealth Fund" concept modelled on the Alaska Permanent Fund. The timing is not subtle: it follows June's export-control chaos that darkened Anthropic's frontier models for nineteen days and left GPT-5.6 in a government-vetted preview, and it lands as the White House finalises voluntary release standards for frontier models, possibly next week — a saga The AI Daily Brief has tracked all fortnight.

The view: When a company offers its regulator shares, the word is not "partnership". A state that profits from a model's success will find it remarkably hard to restrict that model — which is presumably the point.

5. California buys the surgery a chatbot — statewide, half price

Governor Newsom announced on Monday that every California state agency and local government can access Claude at a 50% discount through the state's new shared-services portal, with free workforce training thrown in — the largest state-level AI deployment in US history. The subtext is spiky: Anthropic fell out with the Pentagon this year over surveillance and autonomous-weapons safeguards, the Department of Defense signed with OpenAI instead, and Sacramento has pointedly gone the other way.

Why it matters: Procurement is becoming the real AI policy — states and systems now pick a lab the way they once picked an EPR, values row included. The NHS's Copilot deal for 505,000 staff was our version; the question in both is what happens to the price once you're thoroughly locked in.

From the Eye

Anthropic goes chip shopping. The Information reports Anthropic is in early talks with Samsung about a custom AI accelerator on its 2nm process, having quietly hired silicon engineers — even the model-makers no longer fancy betting their future on Nvidia's order book. Google versus Denmark. Buried in Google's own environmental report: electricity use up 37% in a year, its largest jump ever, with data centres drawing over 42 million megawatt-hours — roughly Denmark's annual consumption, disclosed with the serenity of a firm reporting stationery costs. The consultancy pivot. Microsoft launched its "Frontier Company" this week — $2.5bn and 6,000 forward-deployed engineers embedded in customers — two days after Amazon committed $1bn to the same idea; when software giants start selling people by the hour, someone has concluded the models alone don't finish the job.
