THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Sunday 26 July 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 087

On the line today: Utah's six-month experiment in letting an AI renew repeat prescriptions produces data that is neither the triumph nor the disaster either camp wanted; the NHS starts rolling out a £30 blood test that could spare 18,000 women a year an uncomfortable womb-cancer scan; OpenAI plugs ChatGPT straight into patients' medical records with "ChatGPT Health"; a Delhi court hands OpenAI a fair-dealing win that quietly reshapes the copyright fight; and Stanford's "canaries" data says AI is hollowing out the entry-level job. From the Eye: the indemnity body drowning in AI-written complaint letters, the French regulator that built its own shopping bots to catch the market carving up, and the first hard evidence that leaning on AI makes doctors worse at the thing the AI does.

1. Utah's AI has been renewing prescriptions for six months — here's what happened

Utah's first-in-the-nation pilot, in which Doctronic's AI autonomously handles repeat prescriptions for around 190 chronic-condition drugs at a few dollars a script, is now six months old, and the data are more interesting than either the boosters or the doom-mongers predicted. In roughly 72% of cases the AI declined to act alone and routed the renewal to a human doctor for sign-off; the company also claims its system matched physician treatment plans in 99.2% of 500 urgent-care cases it reviewed. No catastrophe, no revolution — mostly a machine that knows when to ask for help.

The view from the surgery: repeat prescribing is the quiet grind of general practice — thousands of low-risk, high-volume renewals that eat clinical time and occasionally hide a real problem. A tool that safely clears the genuinely routine and escalates the rest is exactly the shape of automation that could work here. But note where the 72% went: to a human. The system's headline safety feature is that it defers, which is reassuring right up until the volume makes deferring impractical and someone tunes the threshold down. The number to watch isn't the accuracy, it's how long that 72% survives contact with a budget.

2. The NHS starts spreading a £30 blood test that spares women a scan

Several NHS organisations are beginning to adopt PinPoint, a machine-learning blood test that reads around 30 markers to score a woman's risk of gynaecological cancer, following an evaluation of 16,481 patients referred with suspected cancer across 170 Yorkshire practices. The test correctly flagged 99.1% of cancers as elevated or high risk and returned a negative predictive value of 99.8% in the lowest-risk group — enough, its developers say, to spare roughly 18,000 women a year the transvaginal ultrasound many find uncomfortable or painful.

Why it matters: this is the kind of AI story that actually earns the label — a cheap, validated test that removes an unpleasant investigation from a frightened patient's pathway without lowering the safety net. The negative predictive value is the whole game, and 99.8% is a serious number on a serious cohort. The caveat is the one that always applies: a risk score is only as good as the pathway it sits in, and "elevated risk" still has to lead somewhere. Deployed as a rule-out to reassure and de-escalate, it's a genuine win. Deployed as a gate to ration scans, it's a different tool wearing the same coat.

3. ChatGPT now reads your medical records

On 23 July OpenAI launched "Health in ChatGPT" for US users, letting anyone over 18 connect Apple Health data and — via infrastructure spanning the big Epic and Oracle record systems — their actual electronic health record, so the chatbot answers questions with the patient's own results in front of it. OpenAI says connected records aren't used for training or advertising. Arriving the same week as the Utah data, the pairing isn't a coincidence: the frontier is now the patient, unmediated.

The view: the clinical logic is seductive — most people cannot read their own blood results, and a patient tool that explains them in plain English fills a real gap the NHS never staffed. But "half of AI health answers are wrong even when they sound convincing," as this spring's BMJ Open analysis put it, and a chatbot with your cholesterol in hand sounds far more authoritative than one guessing. The patients who arrive having "asked ChatGPT about my bloods" are about to arrive having shown it the actual bloods. That's a better-informed conversation and a more confidently-wrong one, often in the same visit.

4. A Delhi court hands OpenAI a copyright win worth watching

On 24 July the Delhi High Court refused news agency ANI an interim injunction against OpenAI, ruling that training a large language model on copyrighted news content is, prima facie, "fair dealing" under India's Copyright Act, and that ChatGPT's outputs weren't substantially similar to ANI's originals. The judge pointedly noted that Parliament had not restricted the research exception to non-commercial use — so a company can claim it. The main suit rolls on, and the court flagged its findings as interim only.

Why it matters: this is the copyright fight that sits underneath every AI tool we'll ever use, decided in the world's most populous country and leaning firmly towards the model-builders. It joins a run of US rulings pointing the same way, and the direction of travel matters for medicine too: the training data behind clinical models is scraped from journals, guidelines and textbooks whose authors never consented, and the law is quietly deciding they don't have to. Whatever you think of that, it's being settled now, largely out of the medical profession's sight.

5. Stanford's "canaries" say AI is erasing the entry-level job

Erik Brynjolfsson's Canaries dashboard — built with ADP payroll data across 730-plus occupations — now shows workers aged 22–25 in the most AI-exposed jobs seeing employment shrink at 3.8% a year, while the same age group in the least-exposed roles grows at 2%. The mechanism isn't mass redundancy; it's a hiring freeze at the bottom, young people simply unable to find the openings that used to exist. Azeem Azhar's Exponential View tracks it as the clearest labour signal yet.

The view from the surgery: medicine feels insulated — you cannot ChatGPT your way to a foundation year — but the entry rung is exactly where our training pipeline lives, and it's precisely the "easy" work (routine letters, first-draft notes, straightforward reads) that AI is eating first. If the junior doctor never does the simple case because the scribe and the triage bot did, we should ask, as radiology already is, where the next generation's pattern recognition comes from. The canary here isn't the coder. It's the trainee.

From the Eye

The seven-page complaint. The Medical Protection Society reports a surge in AI-generated complaint letters landing on doctors and clinics — seven pages or longer, dense with confidently-wrong legal argument and the tell-tale tics of a chatbot "coaching" an aggrieved patient. Each one still has to be read, understood and answered by a human, so the net effect is a machine generating work for clinicians at the speed of a prompt. MPS has separately warned of a "widening gulf" leaving clinicians holding the liability when an AI they used gets it wrong. The regulator that built its own bots. France's competition authority, suspicious that AI shopping assistants were quietly steering buyers, did something regulators rarely do — it built its own agents, fired 550 shopping questions at them, logged exactly which sites they cited, and published a 3,700-page dossier concluding that OpenAI, Google and Anthropic already hold 84% of the agent market. Enforcement by experiment; more of this, please. The deskilling proof. Buried in a March review and resurfacing this month: a multicentre colonoscopy trial found adenoma detection dropped from 28.4% to 22.4% when endoscopists went back to working without AI after getting used to it — the first real-world evidence that the tool doesn't just assist the skill, it erodes it. Seventy per cent of nurses and 77% of doctors in a July US survey said they already fear exactly this.
