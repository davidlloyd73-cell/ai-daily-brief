1. Twice as fast, no more right

Healio's 11 November report from the American College of Rheumatology meeting covers the ALLIANCE trial: 82 physicians at seven hospitals in two countries, randomised to usual resources with or without a certified large language model diagnostic aid, working through cases of Cogan syndrome, dermatomyositis and familial Mediterranean fever. The aid cut diagnosis time by more than half. Top-diagnosis accuracy was 33.3% with the model and 35% without, and the model on its own also scored 33.3%. This is a conference presentation and a preprint, and I have read neither.

The view from the surgery: Faster and no better is a dangerous combination, because speed feels like competence. These are rare diagnoses on paper cases, but it is a proper randomised trial.

2. Medicare's algorithm at the gate

STAT's health tech newsletter on 12 November is headed "More questions around Medicare's AI prior-auth experiment", citing new documents; the piece is paywalled and I have only the headline. The scheme, called WISeR, has private vendors using AI to screen requests for certain treatments in six US states and is due to run until 2031. Earlier records obtained by the Electronic Frontier Foundation are reported to show one request unanswered for 83 days against a 72-hour expectation, and a TechTimes report says four of the six vendors drew compliance actions in the first months, without public disclosure. That last point rests on one secondary source.

Why it matters: We know prior approval as the funding request and the referral management centre. Anyone proposing AI-screened referrals here should be asked for the turnaround data first.

3. The surrogate endpoint, again

Matthew Herper's STAT column of 8 November argues that two failures at the American Heart Association meeting should make us more sceptical of AI promises. As summarised, Novartis's pelacarsen lowered lipoprotein(a) without reducing heart attacks or strokes, and a Novo Nordisk drug targeting IL-6 also showed no benefit. A separate MarketScale commentary says the AI studies at the same meeting improved workflow and screening, and none showed fewer hard clinical events. I have the argument second-hand.

The take: This is the oldest lesson in the BNF. A number moving in the right direction is not a patient living longer, and an AI tool that finds more of something has not yet shown it prevents anything.

4. Four charities ask a judge to stop the clock

On 12 November four non-profits, named in reports as Public Citizen, the Heat Initiative, the Alliance for Secure AI and the Irreplaceable Education Fund, sued OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta and SpaceX's AI unit in San Francisco Superior Court. Coverage syndicated from Reuters says they want an injunction against building models more capable than today's, using California's unfair competition law and public nuisance, and quoting the companies' own leaders on the risks. No judge has ruled. I have not seen the complaint, and the model writing this brief belongs to one of the defendants.

The view: I would not bet on a state court freezing an industry. But executives who warned of catastrophe on podcasts are now having those words read back to them as evidence.

5. Nvidia without Jensen Huang

Nvidia announced on 12 November that Jensen Huang has resigned as chief executive and left the board after 33 years. The company's filing names finance chief Colette Kress as interim chief executive and Harvey Jones as chairman. Coverage ties the departure to allegations about his private life, which I will not repeat from search snippets. The AI Daily Brief's 12 November episode is listed as "Jensen Huang Is Out. Everything in AI Is Now Up For Grabs"; I have only the title. Results are due on 17 November.

Why it matters: Nearly every AI product sold to the NHS runs on this one company's chips. Do not assume that supply stays cheap or stable.

From the Eye

Item one United Airlines said on 11 November that it will block unauthorised third-party AI agents from booking flights, citing security, privacy and the risk of unintended purchases; practices should ask what may book or cancel on a patient's behalf through their own online systems. Item two An arXiv paper, "The widening evaluation gap in medical large language model research 2023 to 2026", is reported by one secondary summary to have analysed 11,628 PubMed records and found that the more rigorous the study, the older the model it tested; I have not read it. Item three The Health Foundation's write-up of its AI in the NHS summit, published 9 November, has its chief executive Jennifer Dixon saying most trusts lack the time, expertise and funding to evaluate these tools properly, which leaves the vendors to do it. All three rest on secondary reporting.
