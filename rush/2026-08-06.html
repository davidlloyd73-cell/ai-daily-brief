THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Thursday 6 August 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 097

On the line today: a thousand NHS clinicians are polled and nine in ten are already using AI, two thirds of them without a word of employer guidance; a Michigan team trains a brain-imaging model on five million uncurated scans and beats GPT-5 by twenty points on triage while still missing one critical finding in seven; American regulators hold an unannounced "clinical AI demo day" for ten companies and no one else; the argument over who pays for AI's electricity reaches actual tariffs; and a hundred economists decline to panic about white-collar work. From the Eye: nineteen retractions, £1.65m of quantum, and a Supreme Court justice changing sides.

1. Nine in ten — and two thirds of them improvising

Censuswide polled 1,000 UK healthcare professionals in late June for Heidi, the ambient scribe company, and the results ran on Tuesday. Ninety per cent said they use AI in clinical work; 94% have increased or held steady over the past year; and 65% are using it ahead of any formal guidance from their employer. On the same day Digital Health carried a piece by Dr Yat Wah Li asking whether last week's MHRA ambient voice guidance — which placed transcription and summarisation outside medical device regulation — has quietly shifted the weight of responsibility onto the clinician rather than the vendor.

The view from the surgery: treat the 90% with the caution any vendor-funded number deserves. The 65% is the figure that should worry a partnership. The regulator has said the tool is not a device precisely because a human checks its output, which makes the checking the safety mechanism and the checker the liable party. Defensible — but only if the checking is real. At 4.40pm in a ten-minute appointment, often it isn't.

2. Five million scans, no labels

Nature Medicine published NeuroVFM on Monday, from a University of Michigan group. Instead of curated datasets paired with radiology reports, they took 5.24 million consecutive clinical CT and MRI volumes straight out of Michigan Medicine's archive — the uncurated real thing, artefacts and all — and used self-supervised training to teach the model what neuroanatomy looks like. In a prospective week-long run across the whole health system in January, it triaged critical findings at 92.6% accuracy against GPT-5's 71.2%. Sensitivity was 86.5%: it missed 21 of 155 critical findings. The authors are explicit that this is decision support, not autonomous screening.

Why it matters: the interesting claim isn't the twenty-point margin over a general model — it's "health system learning". The argument is that the messy operational data a large provider already generates beats anything curated. If that holds, the NHS is sitting on an asset it has spent twenty years failing to value and, on current form, will hand to somebody else for nothing.

3. Demo day at White Oak

STAT reported yesterday that FDA and CMS officials hosted a "clinical AI demo day" on 8 July at the FDA's White Oak headquarters, with leaders from ten companies: Anthropic, Microsoft AI, Amazon One Medical, K Health, Hippocratic AI, Curai, Counsel Health, Doctronic, Ellipsis Health and Welldoc. The meeting was not publicly announced; STAT obtained the agenda. The purpose was to give regulators first-hand experience of AI doctor technology as it currently exists, while officials work out both how to regulate it and how to pay for it.

The view: nothing improper — regulators should see the products. But the guest list was entirely sellers. No medical society, no patient body, no state board, and the whole thing only surfaced because a reporter got the paperwork. When the payment rules land next year, someone will note who was in the room.

4. Who pays for the electricity

The fight over data-centre power has moved from op-eds to tariffs. Ohio and Virginia have brought in large-load tariffs aimed squarely at data centres; seven companies — Amazon, Google, Meta, Microsoft, OpenAI, Oracle and xAI — signed a White House-brokered pledge in March to fund the grid upgrades their sites require. Modelling from NC State and Carnegie Mellon puts demand-weighted wholesale prices 6–29% higher nationally by 2030, and up to 57% in the worst-hit regions. US data-centre demand has gone from 23GW in 2023 to 42GW today.

Why it matters: the risk regulators keep naming is stranded cost — substations built for projects that never arrive, with the bill socialised across everyone else's standing charge. Britain has the same exposure and a thinner grid.

5. A hundred economists decline to panic

Indeed's Hiring Lab published its first quarterly Labor Market Outlook Survey yesterday, polling more than 100 prominent labour and macroeconomists. A small majority, 52%, expect AI to be at least a mild drag on employment over the coming year; 35% expect a net gain; 13% expect no effect. A larger majority, 57%, expect downward pressure on the wages of college-educated workers. Unemployment is forecast to drift from 4.2% to about 4.4% by year end, with job postings down 1.4% through June 2027. The consensus word is reshuffle, not collapse.

The view from the surgery: set against the graduate-hiring gloom of the past fortnight, a useful corrective — a cooling market with AI in it is not the same thing as a market being eaten by AI. But note what the panel actually agrees on. Not fewer graduate jobs. Cheaper ones.

From the Eye

Item one — the British Educational Research Association has confirmed that Wiley has retracted or will shortly retract 19 papers, 17 of them from a single guest-edited special section now cancelled, after nine months of pressure from Stephen Vainker, who first flagged the "AI slop"; Bera blames manipulated peer review by unaffiliated parties, Vainker says dozens more must follow. Meanwhile Organization Science reports submissions up 42% since ChatGPT launched, the fastest-growing category being manuscripts scoring 70% or more AI content. Somebody will build next year's systematic reviews out of this. Item two — the NIHR quietly put £1.65m into 17 early-stage quantum health projects on Tuesday through the i4i FAST route, alongside the Q-BIOMED and QuSIT hubs, covering earlier cancer detection, home monitoring of long-term conditions and better decision tools. Ninety-seven thousand pounds a project is seed money rather than a programme, but it is the first time quantum has been funded against NHS clinical questions rather than physics departments. Item three — Anthropic has appointed Mariano-Florentino Cuéllar as its first chief global affairs officer: former Justice of the California Supreme Court, former president of the Carnegie Endowment, on leave from Stanford Law. He had been a trustee of the company's Long-Term Benefit Trust, the body meant to hold it to account, and resigned from it to take the job. No rule has been broken. It is simply worth noticing how thin the membrane is between the people overseeing these companies and the people employed by them.
