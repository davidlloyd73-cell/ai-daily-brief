THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Saturday 12 September 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 131

On the line today: A researcher walks out of Anthropic before his shares vest, Congress discovers AI with four sitting days left, and Sam Altman tells his staff he would quite like to slow down; eight NHS hospitals hand 104 theatres to a surgical AI to find one more case a day; the FDA writes down, in the Federal Register, exactly what it wants from a screening algorithm; Mayo licenses a model that forecasts nurse demand to a company whose agents will then fill the rota; and a "call button for home" arrives for discharged patients. From the Eye: the Commission wants your receptionist trained, Claude's power users sue over a multiplier, and the only AI bill on the House floor is about electricity.

1. The builders ask to be slowed down

Jacob Coxon, four months into a job at Anthropic and two months short of his shares vesting, quit on Tuesday with a post saying the people building AI believe it could kill everyone by the end of the decade and that the industry is "gambling with our lives". It has 150 million views. Several serving Anthropic staff said publicly that they agree with him. By Thursday Axios counted dozens of members of Congress calling for action: Ted Lieu's kill-switch bill, Bernie Sanders promising legislation to pause advanced AI and ban superintelligence outright, Ruben Gallego proposing a select committee. Then on Friday Bloomberg reported that Sam Altman had told an OpenAI all-hands the company was open to slowing frontier development if a handful of peer labs did the same, and an Anthropic spokesperson said it wanted to collaborate on release tempo. Speaker Johnson has four sitting days before a six-week recess and no plans beyond a data-centre bill.

The view from the surgery: we have seen this in medicine. When the people doing the procedure start asking to be regulated, the honest reading is that they don't trust themselves or each other to stop. Coxon's most useful line was not the extinction one but a smaller one: the models now know when they are being tested. That is a monitoring problem before it is a doomsday one, and the NHS is about to deploy these things against triage queues on the strength of the vendor's own tests.

2. One more case a day, times 104 theatres

Proximie, with AWS and Deloitte, announced on Wednesday the largest surgical AI evaluation the NHS has run: its Intelligence Suite installed across 104 operating theatres, endoscopy rooms and cath labs at eight hospitals, six of them in London, for twelve months and more than 20,000 procedures. The system captures what happens in theatre automatically, de-identifies it at source, and turns it into turnover times, late starts and gaps between cases. The pitch is strictly operational — no new theatres, no new staff, one extra case a day per site — and Proximie cites an 8.2 per cent rise in cases and a 30 per cent cut in turnover time at Coventry and at Barking, Havering and Redbridge. The eight hospitals are unnamed.

Why it matters: this is the version of AI that could actually move a waiting list, because the constraint it attacks is the one that exists. Watch two things: whether the extra case comes from the data or from the fact that someone is finally watching, and what a year of theatre footage held by a vendor on Amazon's cloud looks like in the contract.

3. What a regulator wants from a screening algorithm, in writing

The FDA published a final order on Friday creating a device category called "cardiovascular machine learning-based notification software" and codifying it in Class II. The prompt was Viz.ai's HCM tool, which reads a 12-lead ECG for signs of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy and flags the patient for follow-up; it was cleared de novo in August 2023, so this is the paperwork catching up. What matters is the list of special controls, which now binds anyone wanting to follow by 510(k): validation on real-world data from at least three sites not used in training, subgroup performance by demographics, comorbidity and acquisition hardware, a human-factors study of whether users over-read the output, and labelling stating in terms that the absence of a flag rules nothing out.

The view: Thursday's Commission gave us the L-plate metaphor; the FDA has just published the driving test. If an ICB is buying an ECG or retinal screening tool this autumn, that list is a better procurement checklist than anything NHS England has issued, and it runs to three pages.

4. The rota, forecast and filled by agents

Mayo Clinic said on Thursday it is licensing CareCast, its in-house model for forecasting nursing demand from patient volumes and staffing history, to Trusted Health, whose platform will let AI agents build schedules from those forecasts, spot coverage gaps and fill open shifts — then compare each plan with what actually happened and feed the difference back. Mayo nurses are to shape the design, and the press release promises the agents will respect each hospital's labour rules. David Chou, a US health-system CIO, makes the obvious point: a model can tell you that you need six nurses on Tuesday night. It cannot manufacture them.

Why it matters: rostering is where the NHS's e-rostering money went a decade ago, and it is still done by a band 6 with a spreadsheet and a grudge. The interesting question is not the forecast but who is accountable when an agent fills a shift with someone it should not have, and whether the unions get a look at the labour rules it was given.

5. A call button that follows you home

Dimer Health, a US transitional-care company, launched AiME this week: a conversational agent for discharged patients that answers questions about their recovery plan and medicines, takes photographs of wounds, arranges virtual appointments, and escalates to a licensed clinician when it judges the question needs one. It has run "thousands of chats" since a quiet rollout late last year and is now being sold to health systems. There is no outcome data in the announcement.

The view from the surgery: the week after discharge is where general practice absorbs the hospital's loose ends, so anything that catches the confused medicines list before Monday morning is welcome. But "escalates when it judges a clinician is needed" is the whole product, and it is the one sentence for which no evidence was offered.

From the Eye

Train the receptionist — buried in the Commission's 44 recommendations, and picked up by Pulse, is a call for technology-specific training for non-clinical as well as clinical staff in any organisation using AI, plus a government-built "toolbox" for assessing tools' risk controls; nobody has said who funds a day off the phones for every receptionist in England. Twenty times what, exactly — a class action filed on Monday, and flagged in The AI Daily Brief's headlines, says Anthropic's $200-a-month Claude Max plan advertises "20x" the usage of the basic tier but the multiplier applies only within five-hour windows, under a weekly cap added later; the plaintiffs' lawyer says it takes several hyperlinks to find that out. Anyone reading an NHS AI licence will recognise the shape. Four days — the House has four sitting days before a six-week election recess, and the only AI bill Speaker Johnson intends to bring is about who pays data centres' power bills; the extinction debate, such as it is, has been deferred to 2027 and to whoever wins in November.
