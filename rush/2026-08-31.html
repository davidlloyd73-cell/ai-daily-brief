THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Monday 31 August 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 120

On the line today: cardiologists in Munich show that a routine mammogram can flag hypertension, ischaemic heart disease and prior stroke; the American consolidator Radiology Partners buys Everlight, the firm reading NHS scans overnight; Bill Gates declares nobody is planning for AI's upheaval and half the commentariat points out they've talked of little else; Anthropic warns that ordinary malware is quietly hijacking signed-in Claude sessions; and Google launches vertical AI suites for lawyers and bankers while medicine waits its turn. From the Eye: a $30 trillion number nobody laughed at, Apple's quiet local-AI pivot on the eve of a new CEO, and €8.7m for scanning the worried well.

1. The mammogram keeps moonlighting

At the ESC Congress in Munich — this year themed, inevitably, around AI — researchers from Sheba Medical Center and Tel Aviv University showed a deep-learning model that reads cardiovascular disease off routine breast screening images. Tested across 97,364 mammograms from 29,921 women, average age 54, it discriminated hypertension with an AUROC of 0.79, ischaemic heart disease at 0.78 and prior stroke at 0.86, with performance holding up across age and cancer status. The pitch: midlife women already attend for breast screening, cardiovascular risk in women is under-assessed, and the film is already sitting there.

The view from the surgery: on Thursday this brief covered the economics of AI reading mammograms for cancer; now the same film moonlights as a cardiovascular screen. But an AUROC of 0.79 at population scale is a machine for generating amber flags, and each one lands on a GP's desk as "the breast unit thinks I have heart disease". Opportunistic screening of people who consented to something else needs a worked-out pathway before it needs a bigger model.

2. The night shift changes owners

Radiology Partners, the largest radiology practice in the United States, agreed on 25 August to buy Everlight Radiology — the teleradiology firm whose 800-plus radiologists in more than 40 countries report over 2.5 million exams a year for hospitals across the UK, Ireland, Australia and New Zealand, including a large slice of NHS out-of-hours CT. The price was undisclosed; reports put it somewhere between $715m and $1bn, a spread of $285m that nobody involved feels obliged to narrow. Everlight will be merged with vRad, Radiology Partners' US teleradiology arm, with management staying put.

Why it matters: the 2am head scan is national infrastructure, and it has just become a line item in an American consolidator's global platform. The buyer's stated logic is scale and AI-enabled workflow, which is plausible — and worth watching, since the same parent's Mosaic unit spent mid-August petitioning the FDA to clarify when imaging AI counts as a medical device. Consolidate the reads, shape the rules: it is a coherent strategy, just not one the NHS is party to.

3. Gates discovers the alarm everyone else is holding

Bill Gates published a 6,000-word essay on 26 August warning that AI's disruption of work is arriving faster than anyone is planning for, proposing a robot tax and "Human Reserved" jobs, and telling Semafor he is "in a state of shock that I'm sort of the first one saying, 'This is crazy.'" The media tour ran all week. So did the rebuttals: The AI Daily Brief noted his essay landed the same day as 130-odd pages of OpenAI and METR reporting on the Hugging Face agent breakout — the industry publicly dissecting exactly the kind of failure Gates says nobody discusses — and economists queued up to reject the premise that nobody is paying attention.

The view: strip the main-character syndrome and there's a useful distinction underneath. Plans built on imagined futures — the 18-months-until-no-white-collar-jobs school — have aged badly; responses built on observed failures, like monitoring gaps and unpublished evidence, age well. The NHS version of this argument was settled on Friday, when the statistics watchdog ruled on the 29 per cent.

4. The stolen session

Anthropic warned late this week that ordinary infostealer malware — Vidar, LummaC2, StealC and RedLine on Windows, Atomic Stealer on Macs — is being used to lift signed-in Claude session cookies from infected computers, replay them, and quietly drain accounts' usage without ever needing a password. The company is signing affected users out, wiping saved payment methods and refunding unauthorised charges. The tell: usage limits that refill and then drain while you weren't using the thing.

The view from the surgery: no password was phished and no vendor was breached — the machine itself was compromised, and the session did the rest. The computer on your consulting-room desk now holds signed-in sessions for tools that can read what the account can read. Practice IT policies were written for passwords; the attacks have moved on to cookies, and "log out occasionally" has become clinical governance advice.

5. Lawyers get their AI suite; medicine waits

Google launched Gemini Enterprise for Legal and for Financial Services on 25 August: bundled connectors to Thomson Reuters and the productivity suites, pre-built skills for contract review, research and regulation-scanning, all customisable to a firm's own playbooks and sitting inside Google's existing data-protection framework, so compliance officers need not vet a new vendor.

Why it matters: note what the professions with money get — governed, vertical, playbook-aware AI inside an existing compliance wrapper — and compare it with medicine's retail model of individual clinicians choosing individual scribes. A Gemini Enterprise for Medicine is an obvious next release, and the interesting question is who writes the "firm playbook" when it arrives here: the vendor, the ICB, or the profession. On current form, the vendor.

From the Eye

The number — the Wall Street Journal reports Anthropic will tell pre-IPO investors it sees over $30 trillion in potential revenue, roughly the size of the entire US economy; when Uber floated in 2019 its much-mocked TAM was $6 trillion, and all 191 tech companies in the S&P 1500 together booked $2.4 trillion last year. As one commentator put it, either you believe this eats the economy or you don't — but the street no longer flinches. The handover — Tim Cook's last day as Apple CEO is today; John Ternus takes over tomorrow, inheriting, among other things, a curious late-August product launch: Mac minis rebuilt around local AI inference, marketed in language closer to developer relations than Apple gloss, after a hobbyist agent framework sold out the previous model worldwide. The company that missed the chatbot wars may be quietly arming the run-your-own-model era. The scan spa — per Nelson Advisors' weekly, Swiss firm Ahead Health has raised €8.7m to expand preventive whole-body MRI screening centres. Every incidentaloma those machines find becomes somebody's urgent referral, and that somebody is us; private screening's business model remains reassurance sold retail, follow-up billed to the NHS.
