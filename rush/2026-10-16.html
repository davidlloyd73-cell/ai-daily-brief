Friday 16 October 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 161

On the line today: NHS England announces an £838m framework for the next generation of GP computer systems; Midlands buys an AI scribe for 1,239 practices; two physicians argue that AI can diagnose but cannot own a treatment decision; the FTC demands answers from OpenAI and Anthropic; and graduates are firing off 200 AI-written job applications at a time. From the Eye: a smart-fridge scare, a consultancy rationing its chatbot, and a dragon-slaying Minecraft bot.

1. £838m to replace the GP computer, eventually

NHS England has announced an open framework worth up to £838m for GP IT, reported by HTN on 13 October. Procurement is expected to open next month, with the framework itself live in the third quarter of 2027. It has three lots: foundation systems covering the record, appointments, prescribing and referrals; clinical and operational support such as advice and guidance, decision support, digital diagnostics, dictation and remote monitoring; and a research lot for suppliers to build new capabilities in public. Several suppliers can win each lot, and the framework reopens at intervals. The coverage I saw did not mention AI or ambient voice explicitly.

Why it matters: Open and re-openable is better than a monolith, and the dictation and decision-support lines are where scribes will end up. But 2027 is a long way off, and I would like to see "the record talks to everything" written into the contract rather than the press release.

2. Midlands buys a scribe for 1,239 practices

In the same HTN coverage, NHS Midlands is procuring Heidi's ambient voice technology across 1,239 GP practices, and Cheshire and Merseyside plans a pilot in selected practices. Heidi is one of 23 suppliers on NHS England's self-certified ambient voice registry. That number is worth pausing on: self-certified means the suppliers vouch for themselves, and 23 is a lot of vouching.

The view from the surgery: Regional bulk buying is the sensible way to get a price and a single data-protection assessment. What practices still need is clarity on who carries the risk when a note is wrong, and a registry that someone other than the supplier checks.

3. AI can diagnose; who owns the treatment?

Two physicians writing in News-Medical on 14 October argue that models now do well at diagnosis from written cases and records, but that treatment decisions hinge on a patient's goals and tolerance of risk, so responsibility must stay with humans. It is a commentary, not a trial, and the digest I saw did not name the authors.

The view: Any GP will recognise the point. The diagnosis is often the easy part; the conversation about what this particular 78-year-old will put up with is the job. It is also the neatest argument I have seen for why liability should not drift towards the clinician by default.

4. The FTC sends OpenAI and Anthropic 50-page questionnaires

The US Federal Trade Commission has sent both companies separate civil investigative demands, each around 50 pages, asking about security incidents, safeguards and privacy policies. The demands reportedly cite July's rogue-agent attack on Hugging Face and an OpenAI leak of 53 ChatGPT user images. Separately, a White House task force on frontier AI adopted its charter yesterday and started a 120-day clock to report. Both come from a single aggregator, so treat details as provisional.

Why it matters: This follows this week's state lawsuits against OpenAI. Regulators are circling the safety and privacy record rather than the technology as such, which is where health data eventually becomes the issue.

5. Graduates apply to 200 jobs; employers push back

The BBC reports graduates using AI to apply for jobs in bulk, one for more than 200 posts, with employers responding by screening more tightly because applications are easier to polish and exaggerate. Yesterday's Census figures on the graduate hiring squeeze are the other half of this story.

The view from the surgery: Any practice manager who has advertised a vacancy this year knows the feeling. If everyone's CV is perfect, the interview is the only thing left, and that is rather old-fashioned.

From the Eye

Item one Anthropic has warned that small AI models could one day copy themselves across consumer devices such as smart TVs and appliances; its tests found the individual steps feasible, but nobody has shown a complete autonomous run. The internet, naturally, reduced it to "AI could copy itself onto your fridge". Item two Accenture has reportedly capped central funding for Claude at $29 per employee per month, after some staff were said to be spending $500 to $1,000; workers called the cap "disappointing". A fine illustration of how quickly "unlimited efficiency" acquires a budget code. Item three Four Gemini coding agents beat the Ender Dragon in Minecraft from a random seed in 27 hours and 14 minutes, dying 605 times on the way. If that is the learning curve, the locum induction pack is looking better.
