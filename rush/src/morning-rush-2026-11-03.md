## 1. The NHS becomes its own device manufacturer

EMJ reports that NHS England's AI Assisted Discharge Summary tool, which uses large language models on the Federated Data Platform to draft summaries from the record, is now live at Chelsea and Westminster. It is described as a Class I device, and NHS England is quoted as saying it is the first AI medical device it has manufactured itself. Clinicians review, edit and approve every draft. The "first" claim rests on one source and I found no MHRA or NHS England page confirming it, nor a precise go-live date.

**The view from the surgery:** Doctors.net.uk has reported senior doctors warning that AI discharge summaries could drop clinically relevant detail. We are the ones who receive these letters and act on them, so ask whether a missing sentence is detectable downstream.

---

## 2. A first patient-facing LLM device in the US

Trade analysis says the FDA authorised 86 AI or machine-learning devices in the second quarter of 2026, 84 of them through the 510(k) route, and that the public list passed 1,600 by September. The same coverage reports UpDoc's July 510(k) as the first FDA clearance for software using patient-facing large language model technology. These are secondary summaries; the FDA list lags and is not exhaustive.

**Why it matters:** A 510(k) shows substantial equivalence to something already marketed, not proof of benefit in a population like ours. Volume is a measure of paperwork, and the interesting question is how many of those 1,600 anyone has audited in routine use.

---

## 3. OpenAI's biology model and the trusted-access door

One news roundup says OpenAI released GPT-6 Rosalind on 30 October for biology and drug discovery, with refusals relaxed on dual-use biological questions for vetted users under a trusted-access programme. I have only aggregator coverage and have not seen OpenAI's own announcement, so treat the details as provisional.

**The view:** Credentialed access is a sensible compromise, but credentials are only as good as the vetting, which nobody outside can inspect. For clinicians the practical point is that the gap between "research tool" and "something a patient has already used" is closing.

---

## 4. A voluntary accord at the White House

A roundup reports that President Trump signed a voluntary AI accord with the leaders of OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, xAI and Nvidia. Yesterday's brief carried a report of possible pre-release model testing via the Commerce Department; this may be the same thread or a separate one, and I could not find the text of either.

**The take:** Voluntary means the companies mark their own homework. Compare it with the EU's slipped timetable and you see two different bets on how much to bind frontier developers, with the UK still watching from the touchline.

---

## 5. Google's cyber-minded model

Sources conflict on naming, but several describe a new Gemini 4 variant tuned for finding and exploiting software vulnerabilities, alongside DeepMind's Genie 4 world model, released on 27 October to trusted testers only. Which Gemini 4 variant is generally available differs between trackers, so I would not quote a model name.

**The view from the surgery:** Our exposure is old, unpatched clinical systems and a supplier chain, not frontier models. If vulnerability discovery gets cheaper, the supplier patch cycle matters more than any scribe feature. Ask yours how fast they patch.
