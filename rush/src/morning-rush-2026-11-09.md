## 1. Britain edges towards regulating AI devices across their whole life

Secondary reports say the Government accepted all 44 recommendations of the AI in healthcare regulation commission on 6 October, with a lifecycle approach to software and AI devices at the centre. The MHRA is reported to have committed to draft guidance on managing changes to adaptive AI devices by December, and its AI Airlock sandbox is said to be choosing a new cohort this month. Draft replacement device regulations were published in May with no commencement date I could find. I have not seen the GOV.UK pages myself, and one source gives different dates for the commission, so treat the timeline as provisional.

**The view from the surgery:** A scribe or triage tool that updates itself weekly is a different animal from a stethoscope. Until the guidance lands, ask your supplier in writing what changed in the last quarter and who decided it was safe.

---

## 2. The AMA splits the blame for clinical AI errors

The American Medical Association's framework, reported from around 29 October, assigns liability by who can control the risk. Developers must show and maintain safety and effectiveness; health systems answer for how tools are chosen, governed and monitored; physicians answer for the judgment they can meaningfully exercise. It also says doctors should be able to decline unproven tools without penalty. This is a policy proposal, not law, and I have read only trade coverage, not the document.

**Why it matters:** The UK position is murkier. Indemnifiers say they will support clinicians using AI provided the clinician checks the output, which leaves the practice carrying the can for a tool it cannot inspect. Ask your indemnifier to confirm cover for your exact setup, locum sessions included.

## 3. Microsoft's diagnostic orchestrator, read slowly

Microsoft's MAI-DxO was published in Science on 22 October. Coverage reports roughly 83 per cent on 304 hard NEJM cases against about 36 per cent for generalists with reference materials and 22 per cent without. The comparison was against individual generalists, not specialist teams, on documented cases rather than live patients, and part of the gain appears to come from the underlying model. A prospective study with Beth Israel Deaconess is promised. Figures vary between outlets.

**The take:** Puzzle cases are the part of medicine least like Monday morning. Wake me when it copes with a patient who has six problems, no notes and a lift that is out of order.

## 4. OpenAI closes a smaller round at a lofty price

Bloomberg is reported to have said OpenAI has closed an initial $11bn at a valuation of about $1.25tn, with Coatue and T. Rowe Price among the investors. The target was reportedly cut from at least $30bn to somewhere between $15bn and $20bn as AI stocks sold off. Whether the valuation is pre- or post-money differs by outlet, and I am relying on aggregators.

**Why it matters:** This is the same wobble I flagged last week from a single source, now with a number. The NHS is signing multi-year scribe and platform contracts with suppliers whose own funding cycles are visibly less certain than they were. Ask what happens to your data and your service if the supplier is bought.

---

## 5. The labs agree one line in robots.txt

OpenAI, Google, Microsoft and Anthropic have announced support for a shared robots.txt token that lets sites opt out of AI training, reported from 30 October. It does not block crawling or remove material from models already built. Anthropic says it has begun honouring it; OpenAI, Google and Microsoft give later dates, and the technical draft is at the IETF. I checked only secondary coverage.

**The view:** A voluntary opt-out is a courtesy, not a right. Publishers and anyone holding patient-facing content should note that the default remains training unless you say otherwise.
