## 1. Paid to say no

In March the Electronic Frontier Foundation sued Medicare for the paperwork behind WISeR, its AI prior-authorisation pilot running in six states since January. The thousand pages it has now published, and STAT worked through yesterday, are grim reading. Vendors are paid on averted spend, so a denial earns and an approval doesn't; low quality scores trim that payment by 5 to 10 per cent. Two firms refused more than 20,000 requests in the first three months, one of them declining more than it approved. The promised 72-hour turnaround was missed routinely, one request sat for 83 days, and clinicians in Ohio wrote in about patients crying at the bedside waiting for kyphoplasty. Best of all, one vendor told CMS a month before launch that its software wasn't finished or tested, and that it would simply auto-approve everything until it was. A planning document lists what might be added later: air ambulance, MRI, cancer treatment.

**The view from the surgery:** none of this is the algorithm's fault. It is a payment design that would corrupt a human reviewer just as quickly, with AI supplying the volume. Our own prior-approval machinery, from ICB individual funding requests to advice-and-guidance rejections, is heading the same way with the same vendors circling. Ask who gets paid when the answer is no.

## 2. The humans reading your chat

404 Media has got hold of the internal materials for "Project Lily", under which OpenAI routes real ChatGPT conversations to hundreds of contractors, recruited through Crossing Hurdles and paid via Mercor at $50 an hour or more, to summarise the user's intent and score candidate replies from one to seven. The brief is to reward less sycophancy, fewer emojis and less "AI-speak". Each conversation arrives with a "user memories summary" of the person's past interactions, sometimes including location. OpenAI says a privacy filter strips names, addresses and phone numbers first, and concedes in the same breath that it cannot catch everything.

**Why it matters:** every practice has patients who paste their blood results and their fears into ChatGPT at 2am, and more than a few clinicians who paste in a discharge letter to get it summarised. The consumer product is not a clinical tool and never claimed to be; this is the reminder that a stranger in another time zone may be grading the answer. If your staff use it, use the enterprise version or don't.

## 3. Wales writes the scribe into the decade

Bids close today on Digital Health and Care Wales's £10m open framework for ambient voice technology, covering health boards, GMS practices, HEIW and Public Health Wales. It runs from December to 2029, with an option that would take it to 2034. The specification is the interesting part: not just transcription and summarisation but suggested clinical coding, automated template population, approval workflows and GP system integration, scored on AI performance, safety, regulatory compliance and social value as well as price. Award is expected by 30 November. It lands while HSSIB's investigation into AVT harm in hospitals, opened in August, is still in its early stages.

**The view:** a three-to-eight-year national framework for a category whose safety investigation hasn't reported is a bold sequencing choice, though arguably the honest one, since the alternative is the same tools bought practice by practice on nobody's terms. The clause worth reading when the award comes is the one about suggested coding: that is where a scribe stops being a stenographer and starts populating your QOF.

## 4. Half a trillion, then the essay

The Information has totted up Anthropic's compute agreements since last October: $517bn over the coming decade, 14.8 gigawatts across Google, Amazon, Microsoft, SpaceX and others, against the $180bn it had told investors it would spend through 2029. The eleven months of signing ended in August; the "pace the frontier" essay came on Saturday. Azeem Azhar's Exponential View this week calls the week of 6 September the moment the curve turned: of 250 IT executives he addressed in Las Vegas, some 95 per cent said they had serious results from AI, up from a quarter a year ago, and every one planned to spend more. He then walks past Adam Smith's grave in Edinburgh and quotes him on what happens when people of the same trade meet together.

**Why it matters:** a slowdown proposed by two firms that have already bought the power stations is also a moat, and Azhar is right to say so out loud. For the NHS the practical reading is simpler: the capacity is contracted, so the price of frontier models keeps falling, and the vendors selling you "AI-native" anything are pricing on next year's costs, not this year's.

## 5. Who audits the auditors

Governor Newsom signed two bills on 9 September that create the first American framework for checking AI safety claims from the outside. SB 813 tells the state's Government Operations Agency to set qualification criteria by 2028 for independent verification organisations; AB 1405 creates a registry of AI auditors with independence and transparency standards, and from 2029 bars unregistered firms from grading models. The same day, OpenAI put Paul Christiano, the alignment researcher who left it in 2021 and now advises the US government's AI standards centre, on its foundation board and safety committee, with a recusal from all OpenAI matters in his day job.

**The view:** the NHS has spent two years asking who signs off a clinical AI safety case and answering "the supplier, mostly". California has at least written down that the assessor must be neither the vendor nor a mate of the vendor, and must be registered. MHRA's new powers from the Commission should say the same in fewer than three years.

### From the Eye

**ChatGPT on the rates** — the US General Services Administration has signed a 27-month deal that makes ChatGPT Enterprise's $15-a-seat platform fee $0 for federal, state, local and tribal government from 1 October, with usage at half price and no minimum spend; that opens it to roughly 23 million public employees, which is one way to make sure the next Project Lily has plenty to read. **Superintelligence, no press release** — Shanghai AI Laboratory dropped a 744-billion-parameter open-weight agentic model called Atria Dawn Preview on Hugging Face on 11 September with no blog post or pricing, followed three days later by a 143-author paper titled "The Dawn of Agentic Superintelligence"; the authors evaluated it on 16 benchmarks and report that developers rated a third of their AI-assisted tasks impossible without it, a claim that is hard to falsify and easy to put in a title. **Thirty-eight gigawatts** — Bloomberg reports Microsoft plans to take its AI-serving capacity from about 2GW to nearly 13GW by 2032, within a fleet growing from 12GW to 38GW; Hinkley Point C, when it finally opens, will manage 3.2.
