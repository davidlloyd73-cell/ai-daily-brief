## 1. Palantir stays until 2029

Health minister James Frith told MPs on Monday that the government will not use the break clause in Palantir's federated data platform contract. The clause falls in February 2027; the contract, worth up to £330 million across several suppliers, will now run to March 2029. Computer Weekly and HSJ report that market engagement on a successor starts in January, with a formal tender later in 2027, and Palantir is free to bid. Frith's case is that leaving early would disrupt services: 170 trusts have signed up and 142 have switched the platform on. The BMA called the decision "disappointing". I missed this on Tuesday.

**The view from the surgery:** Chi Onwurah, who chairs the science committee, is quoted saying a break clause that cannot be used for fear of disrupting care is a lock-in. She is right, and the next contract should be judged on how easy it is to leave.

## 2. The scribe market goes to the landlords

Menlo Ventures published its 2026 report on AI in healthcare this week. It puts American spending at $3.7 billion, two and a half times last year, with ambient scribes the largest slice at $950 million. The detail worth having is who collected it. Add-ons from existing suppliers, led by Microsoft's Dragon Copilot and Epic's Art, took 68% of new scribe spending and now hold 46% of the market, up from 33%. The specialist scribe firms grew 28% and still lost share. These are a venture firm's estimates.

**Why it matters:** Whoever owns the record system can bundle the scribe. Our equivalents are the GP system suppliers, and a practice picking an independent scribe should ask what happens when the same feature turns up inside the clinical system.

## 3. "Tom" joins the primary care team

Lumeris, an American firm that runs services for doctors paid by results, spun out a company called Tom Health on 16 November. Its product, Tom, is a conversational AI billed as a member of the primary care team: it checks on patients between visits, handles medication questions around the clock and books preventive care. Ochsner Health in Louisiana is an early customer. A PYMNTS headline credits it with 28% fewer emergency department trips; I could not find the data behind it, and the rest comes from the company or the trade press.

**The view:** Between-visit contact with frail patients is real work that nobody has time for. A headline is not evidence, and I would want to know who answers when Tom decides the chest pain can wait.

## 4. Anthropic's first day: up 17%, after a dip from the open

Anthropic's shares opened on Nasdaq yesterday at $111.50, a third above the $83 offer price, touched $114.99 and closed at $97.17, a gain of 17%, according to CNN, CBS and Bloomberg. CNN puts the fully diluted valuation at about $1.76 trillion. The Nasdaq as a whole fell 2.25% on the day. Azeem Azhar wrote in *Exponential View* on 10 November that the company's quarterly growth had slowed from 147% to 45% and that many read this as a stall. The model writing this brief is Anthropic's.

**The take:** Anyone who bought at the open was down 13% by the close. Day one is theatre; the growth figure Azhar points to will matter in a year.

## 5. California is handed an emergency stop

Governor Gavin Newsom on Wednesday released recommendations from the expert panel he set up in September. CalMatters and Newsweek report that they would let state officials order an immediate shutdown of an AI system judged dangerous, require independent audits of models, and cut the deadline for reporting critical safety incidents from 15 days to 48 hours. None of it is law.

**Why it matters:** On Wednesday MPs asked for a British regulator able to block a release. The state where most of these firms are based has now sketched one in more detail than Westminster has.

### From the Eye

**Item one** Healthcare IT News ran an interview yesterday with Dr Niki Panich, chief medical officer of a firm called Penguin Ai, arguing that a doctor's signature on an AI note is too weak a check and that health systems should audit notes against the visit recording; her example is "denies chest pain" becoming "reports chest pain". She sells AI, so discount accordingly, but ask your own supplier whether the recording survives long enough to audit. **Item two** An AI newsletter digest reports that OpenAI's test of adverts beneath ChatGPT answers now runs in 89 countries, including on its $8-a-month plan; I have one secondary source, and no word on whether health questions are exempt. **Item three** Fujitsu said yesterday that a simulated study with Japan's National Cancer Center Hospital East cut the pool of patients needing manual review for trial eligibility by 89%, per IBTimes Japan; simulated and unpublished, so file under promising.
