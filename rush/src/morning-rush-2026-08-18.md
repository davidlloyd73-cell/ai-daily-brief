# THE MORNING RUSH

*A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.*

**Tuesday 18 August 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 109**

---

**On the line today:** the world's largest medical records company takes the stage this afternoon to sell health systems a factory for building their own AI agents; the clearest published account yet of getting four thousand clinicians onto an ambient scribe turns out to be a paper about management rather than technology; the assistant that passed a billion users last week will now book your doctor's appointment; OpenAI has quietly dissolved the team whose job was to work out whether its own models could do serious harm; and the Wall Street Journal has found three trillion dollars of AI commitments that never appear as debt. From the Eye: a presidential crypto venture routing customers to Chinese models, a company bought for knowing what AI costs, and the software nobody has for the job everybody wants automated.

---

## 1. Epic hands its customers an agent factory

Epic's Users Group Meeting opens its General Session today in Verona, Wisconsin — over 20,000 people on campus, 60,000 expected to watch — and the headline items are agentic. Agent Factory is Epic's platform for building and overseeing AI agents that complete tasks autonomously on a health system's behalf. Curiosity, sitting inside the Cosmos research platform, answers population health questions and predicts individual risks such as readmission. Charité in Berlin becomes the first Epic organisation in Germany, and the company notes 914 million records exchanged over its Care Everywhere network in June alone, much of it with non-Epic systems.

**The view from the surgery:** the significant word is "factory". Until now the argument in every organisation has been whether to buy a particular tool. Epic is proposing that health systems build their own agents, at volume, inside the record itself. Whatever approval process your board has for signing off one product will not survive contact with a hundred locally assembled ones. Note the sequencing, too: the national safety investigation into ambient voice technology reports in summer 2027, and the agent-building platform ships now.

## 2. The Cleveland Clinic paper is about management, not AI

npj Health Systems has published Cleveland Clinic's account of getting ambient AI scribes to more than 4,000 ambulatory clinicians in four months, and the striking thing is how little of it concerns the model. The paper describes a four-pillar partnership with the vendor — governance, training and onboarding, support, and real-time learning — with the supplier treated as a delivery partner rather than a box at the end of a procurement. The argument is that sustained use becomes achievable when implementation capacity is built as a permanent institutional asset instead of improvised afresh for each product.

**Why it matters:** this is the most useful thing published on scribes in months, and it is essentially an operations paper. It also quietly explains why general practice adoption looks the way it does. A practice has no governance function, no onboarding team, no rapid support line and no feedback loop — so what we have instead is individual GPs choosing individual products and getting on with it. That works, until the day somebody asks who owned the deployment.

## 3. The assistant becomes the front door

Zocdoc and Google announced on 12 August that Gemini users in the United States can now find and book real appointments inside the chat window — a network of more than 200,000 providers, 200-plus specialties, 10,000-plus insurance plans — without touching a directory or waiting on hold. It landed the day after Sundar Pichai said the Gemini app had crossed a billion monthly users, up from 400 million in May 2025. Zocdoc's own survey reports that 26 per cent of patients have already used AI to ask a health question, and 85 per cent of providers say they are seeing more AI-informed patients than a year ago.

**The view:** we have spent two decades arguing about who controls the front door to primary care — receptionists, NHS 111, online consultation forms, now the NHS App's triage tool. This is a fourth party arriving without being invited to the discussion, and it is already the most-used software on the planet. In the US the booking is real and transactional. Here it will start as advice, which is harder to see and impossible to audit.

## 4. OpenAI dissolves the team that watched for catastrophe

OpenAI has disbanded its preparedness team, the group responsible for evaluating whether increasingly capable models could enable severe harm. Reporting cited by The Verge puts the dissolution at the end of July, with responsibility for specific domains — biological and cybersecurity risk among them — redistributed into other teams, and the former lead moving to work on the implications of recursively self-improving systems. It follows the earlier dissolution or reorganisation of the superalignment and AGI readiness groups, and a run of senior safety departures.

**The view from the surgery:** distributing safety work into product teams is not automatically worse — proximity to the build can help. But it removes the one function whose output could embarrass the rest of the company, and it happens as the same firm ships models built to write working exploits. For those of us being asked to trust these systems with clinical text, the relevant question is no longer what the model scores. It is who inside the company is paid to say no.

## 5. Three trillion dollars that never touch the balance sheet

A Wall Street Journal analysis has found that nine large technology companies carry roughly $3 trillion in commitments, much of it AI infrastructure, that do not appear as conventional debt: long-term data centre leases, chip purchase agreements, special-purpose financing and other structures used to secure scarce compute. Separately, The Information reports Nvidia nearing an agreement to guarantee around $100 billion in credit for another enormous OpenAI data centre, alongside talks to put some $3 billion into an energy developer. The chip supplier is becoming the lender to its own customers.

**Why it matters:** the headline capital expenditure figures everyone quotes are a fraction of the real exposure, and the circularity is now explicit — the company selling the shovels is underwriting the loan for the mine. None of this is illegitimate. But if you are a trust or an ICB signing a multi-year contract with an AI supplier, the question of who is actually funding that supplier's compute, and on what terms, has stopped being an abstraction.

### From the Eye

**Item one** — Reuters has been through WorldClaw, the Hong Kong AI platform now collaborating with World Liberty Financial, the crypto venture backed by President Trump and his family, and found that 43 of the 90 models on offer come from Chinese developers including Alibaba, Baidu, DeepSeek, Z.ai and Moonshot, several of them criticised or designated by Washington on national security grounds; the platform accepts World Liberty's own stablecoin as payment, a World Liberty executive has advised the venture, and WorldClaw says user inputs may be shared with the underlying model providers — none of which is illegal, all of which sits oddly beside an administration spending considerable effort keeping Chinese models out of American infrastructure. **Item two** — the cloud optimisation firm DoiT has paid an estimated $65m for a three-year-old Israeli startup, Attribute, whose entire product is telling companies what their AI actually costs, broken down by token, model, agent, team and individual customer; DoiT's own data suggests customer AI spending could triple in a year while, in one survey it cites, just 15 per cent of executives said they could calculate a return on it, which is a remarkable state of affairs to have reached at this scale of expenditure. **Item three** — buried in a trade round-up last Sunday, and worth more attention than it got: only 7 per cent of American healthcare organisations have dedicated software for managing prior authorisations — the single administrative task most loudly promised to AI, the one insurers are already automating on the refusal side, and apparently the one providers are still doing by fax.
