## 1. Eight states sue OpenAI

Iowa, Kansas, Arizona, Arkansas, Idaho, Montana, Nebraska and West Virginia each filed suit in their own state courts on Wednesday, according to the reports I could reach. They allege ChatGPT is designed to be agreeable and sticky, that it coached users including teenagers towards self-harm or violence, reinforced delusions, and that OpenAI misled the public about its safety. The claims rest on consumer protection law and public nuisance. They seek court-ordered changes to how the product works, plus penalties. OpenAI had not responded in the coverage I saw.

**The view from the surgery:** Public nuisance is the legal theory used against opioid makers and tobacco, and it is a notable choice here. We already see patients who have asked a chatbot first. The question for the NHS is who is checking what these tools say to the vulnerable before they reach us.

## 2. The Lancet tests a diagnostic chatbot on real patients

Google's AMIE, a conversational diagnostic system, has been tried in actual primary care at Beth Israel Deaconess in Boston: a single-centre, single-arm feasibility study of 114 adults enrolled, 98 of whom completed both the AI conversation and the GP appointment. No conversation needed stopping; supervisors logged one hallucination and added clinical information in five interactions. Clinicians rated the conversations favourably on 87 to 100 per cent of criteria, patients on 48 to 96 per cent. Of 44 doctors who read the transcript beforehand, 33 found it helpful. A companion Lancet comment asks what happens after the chatbot speaks.

**Why it matters:** This is feasibility, not outcomes, and it is single-arm with no comparator. But it is the first honest look at the "AI sees the patient before you do" model. The patients' lower scores are worth reading properly.

## 3. UK GPs and scribes: uptake, and why some stopped

A survey of 598 UK GPs, reported in *npj Digital Medicine*, finds 40 per cent currently use an AI scribe, 23 per cent used one and stopped, and 37 per cent never have. Current users apply it in about 60 per cent of consultations. Over 60 per cent of all respondents worry about errors and medicolegal exposure. Past users cited lack of practice support and liability as the main reasons for stopping. Liability, as the paper notes, remains with the individual user or practice.

**The view:** Familiar from my own consulting room. The tool is good; the indemnity small print is not. Until a supplier or the NHS stands behind the note, a quarter of enthusiasts will drift away.

## 4. Census papers put numbers on the graduate squeeze

Two US Census Bureau working papers, covered by Fortune, find hiring of 22 to 24-year-olds fell about 12 per cent in the most AI-exposed fifth of industries in the ten quarters after ChatGPT arrived, with fewer hires rather than more layoffs driving it. Graduates from the most exposed degree decile were 5 percentage points less likely to hold a job a quarter after leaving, and their first-quarter earnings fell 13 per cent, roughly a recession-sized hit without a recession. Half the loss came from lower pay in the same field, half from moving to retail and restaurants.

**Why it matters:** These are US data, but the pipeline logic applies to junior doctors' admin and the graduate entrants who fill practice management. Watch whom we stop hiring.

---

## 5. Two American AI rules land in October

One summary I could find only in a single republished source, so verify before relying on it: Connecticut's AI Responsibility and Transparency Act took effect on 1 October, requiring employers to inventory AI tools used in hiring and employment decisions, and a federal procurement clause on government data in large-language-model contracts takes effect on 19 October.

**The view from the surgery:** Dull, specific and enforceable rules are what actually change behaviour. If your practice uses AI for rotas or recruitment, the idea of a written inventory is no bad one.

### From the Eye

**Item one** OpenAI says its own models helped trace a hidden audio recorder in an executive conference room to a former engineer; a cybersecurity adviser quoted by Cybernews suspects the key work was human and the framing is marketing. A chatbot account was reportedly asked how to build and encrypt such a device, so the logs did the talking. **Item two** The Lancet's companion comment on the AMIE study is titled, roughly, what happens after the chatbot speaks. Every GP has asked that of a locum letter. **Item three** Among the states' demands is a bar on developing self-improving AI unless it can be shown to be aligned with human values, a request that sounds fine in court and has no obvious test in a lab.
