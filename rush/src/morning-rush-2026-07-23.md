**On the line today:** The FDA quietly clears the first patient-facing clinical LLM — a software device that talks a diabetic through their own insulin dose — and the interesting question is whether the machine is the interface or the decision-maker. ---

## 1. The FDA clears the first LLM that talks to the patient

The US regulator has cleared UpDoc V1.0, described as the first software-as-a-medical-device built on a *patient-facing* large language model. Within a tightly drawn indication — insulin titration for adults with type 2 diabetes — patients talk or type to the system and receive dose guidance, cleared via a 510(k) using an old drug-dose-calculator as its predicate and backed by a Stanford titration trial. STAT put the sharp question well: is the LLM merely the interface, or is it the one making the decision? The distinction is the whole ballgame.

**The view from the surgery:** this is the line we've all watched approaching. A dose calculator with a friendly voice is one thing; a conversational model a patient trusts to adjust a drug that can kill them if it's wrong is quite another, predicate or no predicate. The narrow indication is doing enormous regulatory work here — cleared for insulin today, but the pressure will be to extend the pattern to warfarin, to titrated antihypertensives, to anything with a dosing ladder. Watch where it goes, not where it started.

---

## 2. The FDA's list is now mostly radiology — and it's hunting foundation models

The same agency's 10 July update added 211 AI-enabled devices cleared since late 2024, of which roughly 81% are radiology tools — image-processing software (product code QIH) alone accounting for 58 of them. More telling than the count: the FDA says it will begin *tagging* devices that incorporate foundation models, an admission that the old mental model of a narrow, single-task algorithm no longer fits what's being submitted.

**Why it matters:** the centre of gravity in approved medical AI remains stubbornly in imaging, where the input is a picture and the output is a measurement — the tractable corner. But the tagging plan is the real signal. A regulator that needs a special label for foundation-model devices is a regulator that has noticed the same tool can now do many jobs and change between updates. That loops straight back to the post-market surveillance worry the MHRA's own commission flagged: certify once, and you've certified a moving target.

---

## 3. Chatbots are refilling prescriptions, and no one's seen the patient

Physicians in the US are warning that AI chatbots have begun renewing prescriptions in several states without a checkup — with particular alarm about drugs like anticoagulants where the margin for error is unforgiving. It sits on top of a broader unease: ECRI named the misuse of general chatbots the top health-technology hazard of 2026, and a *BMJ Open* audit of five chatbots found nearly half of 250 health answers problematic and one in five potentially harmful.

**The view:** the danger here isn't the flashy misdiagnosis, it's the quiet convenience. A repeat prescription is exactly the low-friction task that gets automated because it's boring — and boring is where the blood thinners live. We built recall systems and medication reviews precisely because "just renew it" is how people drift into harm. An LLM that renews without the review isn't innovation; it's a safety step removed and dressed up as a feature.

---

## 4. France says three firms own the agent economy

The Autorité de la concurrence has published a weighty opinion — more than 3,700 pages with annexes — finding that OpenAI, Google and Anthropic together hold over 84% of the AI-agent market, and warning that the shift from chatbots to autonomous agents could concentrate the digital economy around a handful of vertically integrated firms unless regulators act on data access, interoperability and default placement. It's a competition-law shot across the bows before the agent market has even properly formed.

**Why it matters:** agents are the layer that will eventually book the appointment, chase the referral and reorder the repeat — and if three firms own the plumbing, the NHS and every vendor selling into it inherits that dependency wholesale. The French are early, but they're asking the right question: not "is the model safe" but "who controls the thing that acts on your behalf". Lock-in decided now, quietly, at the agent layer, will be far harder to unpick later than any single model contract.

---

## 5. The boardroom goes cold on AI job cuts

A quieter signal from Azeem Azhar's *Exponential View*: the share of CEOs expecting significant AI-driven headcount cuts has fallen from 46% in January 2025 to just 20% by May 2026, even as those same executives say they intend to invest *more* in AI. The two facts sit oddly together only if you assumed AI's first act was mass replacement.

**The view from the surgery:** the deflation of the job-apocalypse narrative matters to us because it reframes what AI is actually for in the near term — augmentation, not substitution. The scribe doesn't sack the receptionist; it hands the GP back twenty minutes. That's the honest, unglamorous version, and it's the one the survey data now supports. Anyone still selling AI as a way to run the same service with half the staff is selling last year's fantasy — and the people signing the cheques have, belatedly, noticed.

### From the Eye

**Follow the money on the insulin bot:** buried in UpDoc's $18m seed round is a backer worth a raised eyebrow — Eli Lilly, one of the world's largest insulin manufacturers, alongside the American Diabetes Association and Mayo Clinic. A device that guides insulin dosing, part-funded by a company that sells insulin, is exactly the sort of alignment nobody puts on the press release. **The bot that invented anatomy:** among the ECRI test failures doing the rounds — a chatbot advising an electrosurgical return electrode be placed over the shoulder blade, and, per the *BMJ Open* audit, models that "invented body parts" outright. Filed under: reasons the machine should not be left alone with a patient. **The regulator that fought fire with fire:** to write its 84%-concentration opinion, France's competition authority didn't just survey the market — it built its own shopping agents and put 550 purchasing questions to them, logging exactly which websites they visited and cited. Regulators running empirical agent audits rather than taking the vendors' word for it is a small, welcome sign of the watchdogs catching up.
