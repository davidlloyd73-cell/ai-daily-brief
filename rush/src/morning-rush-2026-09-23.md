## 1. The Council turns to loss of control

This afternoon in New York (this evening our time) the Security Council holds a high-level briefing chaired by France's Jean-Noël Barrot, with Yoshua Bengio, Sam Altman, Dario Amodei and Hugging Face's Clément Delangue. It is the Council's first meeting specifically on the risk of losing human control of capable AI systems, and France's concept note names recursive self-improvement directly. Expect division: China wants a central UN role, the US rejects "centralized control", and Russia doubts it is Council business. Delangue, whose company was on the receiving end of July's rogue OpenAI agents, argues it is "not time to slow down but to accelerate", with mandatory sharing of agent traces instead.

**Why it matters:** the interesting voice is Delangue's. A victim of the incident asking for disclosure rules rather than a brake is how medicine learnt from its own disasters, and is closer to the rules we actually live under.

## 2. Honest broker, empty chair

Andy Burnham's first UN speech announced a National Centre for Information Defence against state disinformation and offered Britain as the "honest broker" on AI standards, with AI central to next year's G20 in Manchester. The day before, he signed an AI defence partnership with the US. At home, plans to compel pre-release testing lapsed in Starmer's last months, DSIT has been abolished, and the UK has not signed the Finland–Norway declaration on human control that more than 20 countries have backed. Trump used the same podium to say "we're going to encourage it, not rein it in."

**The view:** you cannot broker a deal you won't sign yourself. The AI Security Institute is good, but testing is still voluntary.

## 3. Two phone lines, one morning

Fakenham Medical Practice in Norfolk is ending its trial of QuantumLoopAI's EMMA receptionist after complaints from patients and staff. From October it moves to clinician-led total triage, and its chief executive admits the tool "didn't work as intended". That is the third EMMA story in five weeks, after Rotherham's accent problems and a Doncaster stroke survivor who gave up and changed practice. Healthwatch Rotherham has now sent its evidence up to Healthwatch England. The same morning, InTouchNow announced a £2.3m seed round led by Ada Ventures. It says it covers 80 languages and 200 accents across 150 practices, and has published no independent evaluation.

**The view from the surgery:** test any AI phone system on your own patients before go-live: the stroke survivors, the hard of hearing, the broad local accent. Count the calls abandoned, not the ones answered. Fakenham's fix was a clinician looking at every request.

## 4. Test the system, not the model

The Alan Turing Institute's *Frontier AI Risks: A practical way forward* argues that arguments about humanity's long-term fate should not hold up work on harms we can already measure. It names five priority areas: chemical and biological misuse, cyber attacks, misalignment, threats to democratic stability and loss of meaningful human control. Its central point is that Britain is good at testing models before release and weak at assuring whole systems once people, processes and changing conditions are involved. A £2m philanthropic grant funds follow-on work with the NCSC and the AI Security Institute.

**Why it matters:** this is exactly the scribe problem. A model that passes on a benchmark says little about a scribe running on a noisy Tuesday list with an interpreter on speakerphone. Post-deployment assurance is where the National Commission's "L-plates" should bite.

---

## 5. Judgement first, spreadsheets never

All four UK Big Four firms are overhauling graduate training. With AI doing the data entry and first-draft reports that used to fill a trainee's first year, graduates are going straight to judgement-heavy work, with explicit training in communication and resilience. Intakes have already shrunk: KPMG cut its graduate intake by 29%, Deloitte by 18%.

**The view:** the same question is coming for GP training. If the scribe writes the note and the agent drafts the referral, a registrar may never do the dull work where judgement used to be built. Someone has to design that apprenticeship on purpose.

### From the Eye

**Marking its own homework, politely:** OpenAI has published "priorities and principles" for third-party safety assessors: pre-registered claims, independence, conflict-of-interest disclosure, remediation time before publication. It is sensible, and it leaves the lab a hand in deciding the scope of its own audit, rather like providers drafting CQC's key lines of enquiry. **Cyber, with a cheque:** Senators Warner and Wyden have reintroduced the Health Infrastructure Security and Accountability Act, with mandatory minimum standards, annual testing, independent audits and $1.3bn for hospitals ($800m of it for rural and underserved ones). HHS already lists 512 major breaches this year. **An atomic agency for AI:** Ed Davey is calling for a treaty against superintelligent AI, with a UK-based global agency modelled on the IAEA,. The Lib Dems may have found the one AI policy both Washington and Beijing would reject.
