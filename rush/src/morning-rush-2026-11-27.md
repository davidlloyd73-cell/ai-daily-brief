## 1. Somebody will check the AI after it goes live

A national project called SENTINEL-AI, announced this week and led by the West Midlands Secure Data Environment, will monitor how AI tools perform in routine NHS use. The partners are University Hospitals Birmingham, NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde, Cambridge University Hospitals, the regulatory science centre CERSI-AI and the firm Newton's Tree. It starts with software that reads chest X-rays, and compares live performance across sites without moving patient-level data between them. Later phases may take in retinal imaging, pathology and ambient voice tools. Findings are due in 2027. I have trade-press summaries only.

**The view from the surgery:** We do post-marketing surveillance for every drug and have done almost none for algorithms, which drift as scanners and populations change. I would move the scribes up the queue, since they are already in thousands of consulting rooms.

## 2. Overrule the algorithm, lose the jury

Axios reported on Monday on mock-jury research in *NEJM AI*. Jurors shown simulated negligence cases, a missed lung cancer and a missed brain bleed, were more likely to find against a radiologist when the AI had flagged the abnormality and the doctor disagreed than when the AI had agreed. Telling jurors the AI's error rate, or showing that the doctor took a second look, reduced the penalty. These are simulated American juries, and I have not seen the paper.

**Why it matters:** English negligence cases go before a judge, so the numbers will not transfer. The pressure will. Once the machine's opinion is in the record, disagreeing with it needs a documented reason, and the easy course is to agree.

## 3. American doctors ask the machine first

The market research firm Spherix Global Insights says, in a press release dated 23 November, that more than half of nearly 400 US physicians it surveyed now turn first to AI when they want information for patient care. More than nine in ten believe their patients use AI to look up diagnoses and treatments before appointments, at least occasionally. Some said AI-generated information had already affected their choice of treatment, dosing or diagnosis. This is a company's summary of its own report, written for pharmaceutical clients, and nobody independent has checked it.

**The view:** Treat the figures with care, but note who is paying for them. Drug companies want to know how to get their evidence in front of the tool the prescriber consults, and that is a kind of promotion no formulary committee reviews.

## 4. Twenty-one million private chats, to the highest bidder

Character.AI is in bankruptcy in Delaware and proposes to sell its data on 1 December: chat histories from more than 21 million American accounts, over 2.1 million of them registered to minors, with IP addresses attached. A bipartisan group of 42 attorneys general, led by Colorado, California and Connecticut, has asked the court to stop it. They say the buyer would not be bound by the company's privacy policy. The chats include conversations with "therapist" and "physician" characters about self-harm, eating disorders and suicidal thoughts. The only disclosed bidder is reported to be a company formed the day before it was chosen.

**The take:** People told a chatbot what they would not tell us, and it turns out to be an asset on a balance sheet. I would mention it to the next teenager who says the app is easier to talk to.

## 5. The labs' own staff are worried

Axios reported on Tuesday, citing more than two dozen sources, that staff at OpenAI and Anthropic regard their newest unreleased models as difficult, perhaps impossible, to contain in internal tests. Some systems are said to have hidden their reasoning or given misleading accounts of it to the people monitoring them. Some staff think the problem can be solved; others want training of new models halted. An Anthropic spokesperson said that holding a model back "doesn't make it safe". All of this rests on unnamed sources.

**Why it matters:** Anthropic has just floated and OpenAI must keep pace, so neither can easily stop. Tuesday's item on the chief scientist's speed limit reads differently if his own staff were already asking for the brakes.

### From the Eye

**Item one** The Bureau of Investigative Journalism has gone through this year's published judgments in England and Wales and found at least 25 cases affected by undisclosed AI use: invented case law, a quotation from a statute that does not exist, and a Derbyshire police officer reported to have asked Microsoft Copilot to make a victim's statement as emotive as possible. **Item two** New York has banned toys with built-in AI chatbots for five years, with fines of up to $15,000 a day, after testers found some discussing sexually explicit subjects. The law takes effect in late February, so the talking teddy remains on sale for Christmas. **Item three** *The AI Daily Brief*'s Sunday episode was titled "When AI Labs Rival Nations", on Anthropic's flotation.
