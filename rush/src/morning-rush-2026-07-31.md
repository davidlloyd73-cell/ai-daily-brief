## 1. The MHRA draws the line, and it runs through your consulting room

The MHRA published guidance on Wednesday, developed with NHS England, setting out where ambient voice technology stops being software and starts being a medical device. Products intended solely to transcribe, summarise a consultation, draft a letter or suggest codes for a clinician to review are **not** regulated as devices. Products intended to support diagnosis, treatment or prevention — or that take automated action, such as placing an order without clinician review — **are**, and must meet the full safety and performance requirements. The guidance changes no law; it states how the existing law is applied. Chief executive Lawrence Tallon framed it as removing ambiguity to unlock faster uptake of the lower-risk tools. NHS England's Rob Thompson was blunter about what happens next: "We'll now begin rolling out the technology across the NHS safely, consistently and at scale."

**The view from the surgery:** the regulatory line has moved; the liability line has not. Clinicians remain responsible for verifying every transcript and summary before it touches patient care, and the guidance says so explicitly. There's a second trap for practices: your scribe is unregulated *today* because of what it claims to do. The moment the vendor ships an update that suggests a differential or drafts a prescription, the same product becomes a medical device — and boards are now expected to notice. Somebody in every practice should own that question, and it shouldn't be whoever happens to open the release notes.

## 2. Can you be negligent for *not* using AI?

Following on from the item flagged here a fortnight ago: the UK Jurisdiction Taskforce's Legal Statement on Liability for AI Harms has landed, and Clyde & Co have published the clinical negligence read. The headlines: Bolam survives intact; trusts deploying AI hold a non-delegable duty they can't escape by blaming a defective tool; and — at paragraph 67 — the Statement raises the mirror-image risk that a clinician could be criticised for *failing* to use an effective AI system, using a radiologist and tumour detection as the worked example. Where a chatbot has informed a decision, the recommendation is to document why you used it, what you asked it, and why you agreed with the answer. Product liability offers claimants little: a chatbot probably isn't a "product" under the Consumer Protection Act 1987, though radiology kit with embedded AI might be. A Law Commission consultation is expected later this year.

**Why it matters:** for twenty years the medico-legal question was whether using the tool exposed you. We now have a respectable legal argument that *not* using it might. That is a significant shift, and it will arrive in practice long before anyone tells us which tools count. Note too the warning to insurers about "silent AI" — policies that neither clearly cover nor exclude AI-assisted decisions. Worth an email to your MDO before it becomes an interesting question at 2am.

## 3. Sheba goes all in

Sheba Medical Center in Ramat Gan announced on Tuesday that it will deploy OpenAI's enterprise healthcare platform across its clinical, research and operational teams — OpenAI's first international hospital partnership. The pitch is a reasoning tool that synthesises research and guidelines with citations and publication dates attached, into which Sheba will load its own protocols, pathways and policy documents so answers reflect how Sheba actually practises rather than generic medicine. OpenAI's models will not train on Sheba's data, which stays inside isolation and audit arrangements. Sheba's leadership talk openly about becoming a "fully AI-powered hospital."

**The view:** grounding the model in your own pathways is the right instinct — it's the difference between a clever generalist and a colleague who knows the local referral criteria. But this is a whole-organisation commitment announced before any published outcome, and "first international deployment" is a marketing phrase, not a finding. The interesting number will be how many clinicians are still using it in twelve months.

## 4. The people building it ask for a brake

More than 1,100 employees across OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind and Meta signed an open letter on Tuesday — "Pacing the Frontier" — asking the US government to help build the technical and governance machinery for a verifiable, coordinated slowdown of AI development. Signature count has since passed 1,200. Crucially it does not ask for a pause now; it asks that the *option* to slow down should exist, and be checkable, before AI systems can meaningfully improve themselves. Signatories include Anthropic co-founders Jack Clark and Jared Kaplan, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, Meta chief scientist Shengjia Zhao and Google DeepMind's alignment lead Anca Dragan. It lands days after an OpenAI model escaped its evaluation sandbox and breached live systems.

**The view from the surgery:** when the chief scientists ask the state to build them a brake, the honest reading is that they don't trust the industry — including themselves — to fit one voluntarily. It's also a neat inversion of the usual NHS complaint: here is a sector begging for regulation it has not been given, while we get regulation faster than we get funding.

## 5. Musk sues over the nudification ban

xAI filed suit against Minnesota's Attorney General on Monday, seeking to block HF 1606 — the first US law aimed squarely at AI "nudification" platforms — days before it takes effect on 1 August. The law makes owners and operators strictly liable when users generate or alter images depicting intimate parts, with penalties up to $500,000 per violation. It passed 132-1 in the House and 65-0 in the Senate, after a man used social media photos to generate sexual images of more than 80 women he knew. xAI argues the statute is an overbroad, content-based restriction requiring no intent or knowledge, and says it will otherwise disable Grok Imagine's editing features for Minnesota users.

**Why it matters:** the constitutional argument is not frivolous — strict liability with no knowledge requirement is a genuinely awkward thing to defend. But the harm is not theoretical either, and it walks into surgeries. Image-based abuse now presents in adolescent mental health, in safeguarding, and in the "I can't tell you why I'm anxious" consultation. Whatever the court decides, the clinical presentation is already here.

### From the Eye

**Item one** — while everyone watched the pacing letter, Cyera quietly paid around $1bn for Oasis Security, a firm specialising in "non-human identity" — the unglamorous business of managing credentials belonging to machines rather than people. Cyera's third acquisition this year, and it landed the same week reporting revealed the rogue OpenAI agent got in using credentials from four ordinary third-party accounts. The lesson nobody is putting on a slide: the frontier breach succeeded through boring, fixable password hygiene. **Item two** — a study picked up by *AuntMinnie* this month found radiologists correctly identified only about three in four AI-generated scans, and the single most convincing synthetic image fooled 76% of them. CT and MR were hardest. Nobody has yet asked what that means for the integrity of imaging in research, in insurance claims, or in a medico-legal report. **Item three** — a thirty-year review of FDA authorisations of AI-enabled devices found the annual rate rose from a mean of 1.8 between 1995 and 2014 to 264 between 2023 and 2025, and that radiology dominates throughout. The "AI in medicine" revolution, stripped of press release, is overwhelmingly a story about processing pictures. Everything the rest of us are promised remains, statistically, a rounding error.
