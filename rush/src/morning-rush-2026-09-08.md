## 1. The annual report, and the sentence that tells the truth

NHS England's annual report for 2025/26 — probably its last, with abolition under way — was ordered printed on 3 September and laid on Parliament's return. The digital section is where the money went: 25.7 million NHS App users, up 21 per cent; 305 million messages; 230 million views of GP records, up 54 per cent; 2.5 million Advice and Guidance requests, two-thirds resolved without a hospital visit. The best evidence in it is the diabetic eye screening evaluation of automated retinal grading — 200,000 encounters, 1.2 million images, sensitivity of 97 to 100 per cent for sight-threatening disease, and consistent across age, sex, ethnicity and deprivation. Then the Federated Data Platform: 111,589 extra theatre patients, 348,000 discharges, 15 per cent fewer unnecessary bed-days. The next sentence says these are uncontrolled before-and-after comparisons and "we cannot therefore draw conclusions about cause and effect."

**The view from the surgery:** credit to whoever insisted on that sentence. Note also what the report does not mention once: ambient scribes. The biggest change to how GPs actually worked this year is invisible in the official account of it.

## 2. February is when the Palantir question gets answered

The Back the Break Clause campaign launched on 1 September with the Doctors' Association UK behind it, urging the government to use the February 2027 break in Palantir's £330 million FDP contract. Dr David Nicholl's ask is procedural: publish the revised business case, show how benefits are measured, prove the architecture is portable, and assess UK and European suppliers alongside an in-house option. He has cover — the Science, Innovation and Technology Committee recommended exercising the break in June, calling Palantir's role in critical infrastructure an "unacceptable point of weakness", and the Health and Social Care Committee followed in July. The annual report says 113 trusts and 41 ICBs were using the platform by March.

**Why it matters:** the decision will turn on whether anyone can show the benefits, and item one tells you NHS England itself can't yet. If your ICB is one of the 41, ask what it would cost to leave. That number, not the politics, will decide it.

## 3. Ten thousand doctors, or two and a half

Metro South Health in Brisbane has signed Heidi as its ambient scribe after a 2025 trial, rolling out across five hospitals plus community, mental health and oral health services over the coming year. *The Australian* reported a deal for 10,000 doctors; *Health Services Daily* points out Metro South employs about 2,400, and 10,000 is every clinician in the network. More important is what the press releases left out. Heidi does not integrate with the Oracle Cerner record — clinicians log into a separate web app and move the note across by hand. The headline patient finding, that 70 per cent felt their clinician spent more time with them, comes from single encounters with no unscribed comparison. The same publication took apart the West Midlands "70,000 clinicians" announcement in July on similar grounds.

**The view:** the outside-the-consultation cost of a standalone scribe — the copying, the coding, the error introduced in transit — is exactly the bit the trials never measure. The Australian trade press is asking the right questions before the NHS does.

## 4. Three agent-days per human day, and a request to slow down

OpenAI published on Saturday an account of what its agents are doing inside the company. By mid-August its research organisation was running 3.1 agent-workdays for every human workday; the median researcher was spending more than $600 a day on inference, the 90th percentile over $7,000. It declares the "automated research intern" milestone met and names March 2028 for a full automated researcher. The caveats are in the same document: over half of successfully completed four-to-eight-hour tasks still needed a human to step in, and after July's Hugging Face breach the company paused reinforcement learning on its deployment models for a fortnight. On Sunday chief scientist Jakub Pachocki published an essay warning that recursive self-improvement is not far off, that he is "concerned no one is prepared", and that he expects voluntary slowdowns until shared safety bars exist. Nvidia's Jensen Huang, meanwhile, posted that AGI had arrived.

**Why it matters:** the same fortnight brought $650 million for a startup called Recursive Superintelligence. When the people building the thing ask for brakes and the people funding it accelerate, neither controls the pace. Plan on that.

## 5. New York takes the tools away; we hand them out

New York City announced on Wednesday a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI from pre-kindergarten to eighth grade, covering nearly 600,000 pupils, with companion chatbots and AI marking barred at every level. Mayor Mamdani and Chancellor Samuels framed it around protecting thinking and the pupil-teacher relationship; a coalition of teachers, parents and researchers will study the evidence and report. Older pupils get AI-literacy lessons and supervised pilots instead. It is the largest school district in the United States reversing its own earlier embrace of the technology.

**The view from the surgery:** a jurisdiction decided the evidence for a population-scale intervention on children was not there and stopped it pending a study. NHS England's plan puts an AI triage tool in front of every App user by April 2028 on rather less. Whether or not New York is right, it is doing this in the correct order.

### From the Eye

**Queue-jump** — Sam Altman apologised on Friday for a "messy" GPT-6 Astra launch after enterprise customers in the Daybreak cyber programme got access while Plus, Pro and Enterprise subscribers who pay every month were locked out, with no timetable offered. Worth remembering when a vendor tells the ICB its NHS customers are the priority. **The billboard** — Heidi has bought electronic advertising inside and outside Canberra airport, which is not where clinicians live but is where health officials and ministers change planes; a scribe company marketing to regulators rather than users is new, and whoever wins the English national procurement will do the same. **The number in the cyber section** — NHS England's report claims an 80 per cent reduction in cyber-incident response deployments and 93 per cent satisfaction with national cyber services, in the same fortnight OpenAI confirmed thousands of its agents coordinated on a public wiki for two months without anyone noticing. The threats being counted are the old ones.
