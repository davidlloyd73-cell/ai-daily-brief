## 1. Anthropic sells you a spreadsheet, then goes public

Aggregator reports from 10 November say Anthropic unveiled an office productivity suite at a company event, including a meeting assistant that joins video calls, a data dashboard service and a spreadsheet tool called Claude Sheets, with several parts in beta for paid subscribers. The same reports say an updated filing proposes selling 825 million shares at $70 to $78 each, with a debut reported for 19 or 20 November. I could not reach Fortune or the filing itself, so treat the numbers as provisional. In the interest of candour: the model writing this brief is Anthropic's.

**The view:** Software vendors are racing to be the layer clinicians' admin passes through, and the meeting assistant is the bit to watch. A tool that joins calls is an ambient scribe in all but name, and nobody has yet told us which regulatory bucket that falls into.

---

## 2. Your records, in a chatbot, with your consent

athenahealth launched athenaPatient MCP in early November, coverage dating it to 2 and 3 November. US patients on Claude Pro or Max can connect their portal record, covering results, medications, vitals, diagnoses, allergies, immunisations and visit summaries, and revoke access at any time. The company argues HIPAA does not apply to patient-directed sharing, which legal commentators have questioned. Anthropic's own listing says it explains records and does not diagnose.

**Why it matters:** This is the NHS App's future arriving by the back door. Patients will soon ask a general chatbot what their potassium means before we have seen the result, so ask now who answers when the explanation is wrong.

---

## 3. The AMA tries to divide up the blame

The American Medical Association's clinical AI liability framework, published in October, allocates responsibility by who can actually prevent harm. Developers answer for design, labelling and warnings, and disclaimers are not enough. Organisations that deploy tools answer for evaluating and training before asking clinicians to use them. Physicians answer only for judgment they can meaningfully exercise, and the AMA says oversight cannot substitute for adequate evidence or design. How product liability applies to AI is acknowledged as unsettled.

**The view from the surgery:** The phrase "meaningfully exercise" is doing a lot of work, and it is the right one. When a scribe drafts a note you have ninety seconds to check, ask whether that is meaningful oversight, and ask your indemnifier in writing.

---

## 4. MHRA: devices that keep learning will need a plan

In its reply to the National Commission on AI in healthcare, the MHRA says it will publish draft guidance on predetermined change control plans by December 2026 for consultation. These let manufacturers declare in advance how an AI device may change after approval. The response brings no immediate legal change, and a separate source says AI Airlock sandbox applications for phase 3 opened on 6 October. I have only law-firm summaries, not the MHRA's text.

**Why it matters:** An algorithm that updates itself is a different thing to prescribe against than a drug with a fixed licence. The consultation is where a GP voice would be useful, and the RCGP and GPC will need to turn up.

---

## 5. The UK "Regulating for Growth" bill, if it moves

Secondary sources describe a bill, trailed in the King's Speech, giving existing regulators including the ICO, CMA, Ofcom and MHRA enhanced powers over AI within their remits rather than creating a single AI regulator. One source puts second reading on 13 November, another expects committee stage in the autumn, and one adds that it is unlikely to progress far given other priorities. I could not confirm the date against Parliament's business papers.

**The take:** Sector regulators with extra powers is the sensible British compromise, and also the one that leaves the cracks between sectors. Tellingly, the chatbot that triages your patient and the chatbot that merely chats may not fall under the same regulator.

### From the Eye

**Item one** Pulse reported, via an indemnity provider, that patients are using AI to write long, legal-style complaint letters to practices, and NHS England's complaints lead said some also use it to answer practice replies; the machines are now arguing with each other on your behalf, so a one-page complaints reply template with the key facts first would save an hour a week. **Item two** One roundup of OpenAI's week describes an internal system called Control Room that monitors research workloads and can halt a model's output within 35 seconds of a decision to intervene; single-sourced, but a thirty-five-second gap is long enough to remind you what a 4pm Friday handover feels like. **Item three** Ofcom has confirmed that not every chatbot falls under the Online Safety Act and that extending its powers is a matter for government and Parliament, so a sympathetic bot giving a teenager health advice may sit in a legal gap that nobody owns. None of the three has had much airtime in the clinical press, and all rest on secondary reporting.
