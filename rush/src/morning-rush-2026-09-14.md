## 1. The essay meets the share price

Saturday's call to slow down survived contact with Monday's markets for about an hour. Asian AI stocks fell hard: SoftBank, sitting on an OpenAI stake that is also collateral for a $10bn margin loan, dropped as much as 13 per cent in Tokyo, with chipmakers and memory suppliers down across Hong Kong and Seoul and Nasdaq futures off more than a per cent. Over the weekend Satya Nadella said Microsoft "welcomes" the deliberate pacing needed to get alignment right and published a code of conduct for its models, and Sam Altman confirmed there will be no OpenAI listing this year, citing safety directly.

**The view:** the interesting number is not the share price but the one Azeem Azhar collected last week, asking 250 IT executives in Las Vegas how many had meaningful results from AI. A year ago a quarter of hands; this year he counted about 95 per cent, every one planning to spend more. Demand is not what wobbled today. What wobbled is the assumption that the people supplying it will keep going as fast as they can.

## 2. The first complaints about how we use it

HSJ reported this week that professional regulators have received the first complaints about the allegedly inappropriate use of AI by clinicians, and separately that the NHS has received its first clinical negligence claims arising from the use of AI in care. No details of either are public. It lands in the same fortnight as the National Commission's forty-four recommendations, and as Medical Protection's warning that patients are now sending practices twenty-five-page AI-drafted letters quoting legal arguments that may not exist.

**The view from the surgery:** we have spent two years asking whether the tools are safe. The question has now changed to whether *we* used them safely, which is a question about consent, documentation and whether you checked the summary before you signed it. If you run a scribe, the file note that matters is the one recording that you told the patient and that you reviewed the output. Regulators are not interested in the vendor's UKCA mark when your name is on the entry.

## 3. An AI that prescribes, with a regulator's blessing

ARPA-H has committed up to $62.7m over four years to ADVOCATE — Agentic AI-Enabled Cardiovascular Care Transformation — with $33.7m in year one. The aim is a partly autonomous digital member of the heart failure team that monitors patients between appointments, assesses symptom severity, prescribes drugs, orders lab tests and escalates to a clinician when it needs to. First awards went to Atman Health, UpDoc and Tempus AI alongside Stanford, Duke and Kaiser Permanente. Teams building patient-facing agents are expected to file an FDA authorisation package within two years.

**Why it matters:** heart failure titration is exactly the work that fits — protocolised, frequent, tedious, and done badly at scale because nobody has the clinic slots. It is also the first serious attempt to get a regulator to sign off an agent that acts rather than advises, which is the line everything else has carefully stayed behind. Two years is an absurd timetable. Watch it anyway, because if it works the NHS will be shown it in a procurement deck by 2029.

## 4. Beijing writes down what it is afraid of

China's state security minister, Chen Yixin, published an unusually detailed article in the official *China Cyberspace* magazine on Sunday warning that AI could directly threaten the country's political, institutional and ideological security. His list: hostile use of deepfakes, generated text and social bots to fabricate political rumours and incite division, and models increasingly capable of finding software flaws and writing the malware to exploit them. Xi told the party's National Security Commission last week to plan for worst-case scenarios.

**The view:** strip the vocabulary and it is the same threat model the Americans published a fortnight ago, with regime stability inserted where they had elections. That is a genuinely rare thing — the two rivals naming the same risks in the same month — and it is the only plausible route to anything internationally binding. It also tells you the deepfake problem is now a state-level concern everywhere, which is worth remembering next time a patient shows you a video of a doctor endorsing something.

## 5. Selling AI to hospitals that cannot afford it

STAT spent last week in Maine and Louisiana on the administration's claim that AI will rescue rural health care from nearly $1tn of Medicaid cuts — Mehmet Oz has offered "AI-based avatars" as the best way to help rural communities. Providers were unconvinced. Lori Dwyer, who runs Penobscot Community Health Care, said ambient scribes had cut documentation time and helped with burnout but had not reduced costs at all. Others cited weak broadband, handwritten and faxed records that no model can ingest, and an inability to tell the worthwhile products from what one consultant called the garbage.

**The view from the surgery:** the inequity here is the bit that travels. The systems that can afford to rebuild around AI will get the savings; the small ones will buy a scribe, feel better, and see no money. Substitute "single-handed practice in Harrow" for "critical access hospital in Maine" and the arithmetic is identical, which is why the national scribe framework matters more than any individual product on it.

### From the Eye

**Emma cannot understand you** — Healthwatch Rotherham reports that patients trying to book through the AI receptionist Emma, deployed across GP practices, found it did not cope with regional accents or speech impediments; one patient told them "I could never get it to understand me, I ended up just hanging up and not bothering." The vendor says over 90 per cent of patients report an improvement and that calls transfer to a human on request; South Yorkshire ICB is now writing guidance, having previously noted that AI reception is "an individual GP practice decision". **The mathematicians draw a line** — after OpenAI put 10,000 agents and 130 billion tokens through 88 hours to produce a 500-page proof of a Navier–Stokes result that no human can read, Terence Tao and twenty-four other Fields medallists signed a public declaration at mathandai.org warning that the way AI is being used in mathematics is misaligned with what mathematics is for; Tao's own verdict was that a famous problem is now technically solved with almost no value added to the field. **The whistleblower's odd ask** — Jacob Coxon, whose resignation post from Anthropic drew 160 million views, told *Meet the Press* that Congress should let the frontier labs regulate themselves in the interim, because he believes the executives genuinely want to be slowed and any bill drafted now will be outpaced by the next model.
