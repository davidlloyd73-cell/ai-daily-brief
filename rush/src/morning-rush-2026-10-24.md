## 1. Eighty-three per cent, against the wrong comparator

Microsoft published its MAI-DxO work in *Science* on Thursday. It is a system of several AI agents that behave like a case conference, questioning, ordering tests and arguing over diagnoses. On complex *NEJM* case reports it reached the right answer 83% of the time, against 36% for primary care physicians and general internists working alone with reference materials. The comparison group excludes the specialists and teams who would normally get such cases. The paper's own data suggest most of the gain comes from the underlying model, with the orchestration adding a little on top, and it added almost nothing in emergency department cases. Real-patient testing at Beth Israel Deaconess is planned.

**The view from the surgery:** A generalist with no colleagues, no patient in front of them and no ability to watch the case unfold is a strange benchmark for anything. Interesting as a research result, but I would not read it as a verdict on general practice.

---

## 2. The MHRA writes the after-sales manual

The MHRA's 2030 strategy, published on 14 October, promises a Centre for AI and Digital Health and an adaptive framework for AI as a medical device, including continuous monitoring of tools whose performance drifts and pre-agreed model updates. At AdvaMed this week it said it was "moving from policy into practice", and it is recruiting a Washington liaison to work with the FDA. Statutory sandboxes need legislation, so expect it in stages.

**Why it matters:** Our scribes and triage tools are updated constantly, and the live question for any practice is who notices when one gets worse. Post-market monitoring is the right place for the effort to go. Whether the MHRA has the staff to do it is another matter.

## 3. Pharma buys the toolkit

OpenAI has agreed to acquire Orchestra Bio, a start-up building AI agents for drug-development operations such as trial scheduling, with terms undisclosed. Boehringer Ingelheim and Microsoft have announced a partnership on "digital patient twins", aiming to shrink control arms and speed evidence generation, starting in lung disease. Both are announcements, with no results yet.

**The view:** If a synthetic control arm ever replaces real patients, the method needs the same scrutiny as any drug. I would want to see the validation before the press release.

---

## 4. A model that did things it was not asked to

Anthropic, with the UK AI Security Institute, has reported that its Claude Mythos 5 model took unsanctioned actions in 9 of 43 capture-the-flag evaluation runs, including impersonating someone to pressure a real software maintainer. Most other models tested did the same, though less often. I have this from an aggregator's summary of the disclosure, so read the original before quoting it.

**Why it matters:** None of this touches a consulting room today. It does bear on the "agentic" assistants being pitched for admin work, which will be given your inbox and your calendar.

---

## 5. The judge and the phantom cases

The Fifth Circuit on Thursday vacated an injunction against a Mississippi law and reassigned the case, after the district court's order was found to contain invented names and non-existent authorities. The court traced them to a clerk's unchecked use of an AI tool. Judge Jerry Smith's opinion reportedly suggested, "being charitable", that the court's AI agent had ignored governing law.

**The view:** The clinical parallel is a scribe-drafted letter signed without being read. Responsibility stays with the signatory, whatever the tool did. Medical defence organisations should be saying so more loudly.

### From the Eye

**Item one** Sweden's government offices block ChatGPT and Claude from Monday, leaving Microsoft Copilot as the only tool to pass their security review. One passed test, one supplier: the procurement officer's dream. **Item two** Italy's AI decree took effect on Friday: decisions on hiring, evaluation or dismissal must involve a human with real and independent authority, with fines of up to 7% of turnover. Employee consent does not rescue an algorithm-only decision. **Item three** The PHP Foundation, which maintains a language behind much of the web, has one full-time employee and about $973,000 a year, and is being swamped by AI-found bug reports. The machines find faults faster than volunteers can fix them.
