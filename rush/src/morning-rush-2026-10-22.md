## 1. Regulators told the rules were built for static products

The National Commission into the Regulation of AI in Healthcare, set up by the MHRA in September 2025, published its 119-page report on 10 September, and the commentary is only now landing. It makes 44 advisory recommendations: oversight across the whole product lifecycle, an update to the Medical Devices Regulations 2002, regulation by function, staged authorisation for promising tools, stronger post-market surveillance and a searchable public database of adverse incidents. Of 761 responses, 77% of patients and public said the framework was too loose to some degree, while 65% of industry said it was too restrictive. Government has yet to say what it will do.

**Why it matters:** A scribe that is updated monthly is not the same product you assured in March, and a public incident database is the part I would most like to see. It is advisory, so nothing changes in surgery until Whitehall answers.

---

## 2. The assistants get hands

Abridge has launched a chat-and-voice assistant that draws on the patient record, guidelines and hospital policy to answer questions and draft notes, referrals and handoffs, with cited sources. Epic has introduced Connectors, letting its AI assistants fetch information and complete tasks in other apps, with third-party versions listed from March 2027. The examples given include logging ambulance arrival times and turning a spoken meal request into a dietary order. Both are US announcements from the vendors themselves.

**The view from the surgery:** Scribes were the easy bit, because a human reads the output before it matters. An assistant that drafts the referral and then acts on it moves the risk from the note to the system, so the audit trail needs to be better than the demo.

---

## 3. Washington argues about who watches the machines

The American Hospital Association has urged the FDA to take a flexible, risk-based approach to generative AI devices, and to say that generative clinical decision support and administrative tools fall outside its oversight altogether. Meanwhile a former FDA digital health adviser says staff attrition and autonomous AI are testing the regulator's capacity, and the AMA has announced a peer-reviewed *JAMA AI*.

**The view:** Hospitals asking to be left alone is hardly news, but the combination of a thinner regulator and more autonomous tools is the one to watch. Compare item one: Britain is at least arguing about how to do it properly.

---

## 4. When the bug-hunters are machines

Two critical PHP vulnerabilities, which allow remote code execution without logging in, were patched this week. OpenAI says its models helped find and exploit them, and Wiz found both independently. Finland temporarily shut government web services, and Edinburgh council and the BBC took pages offline; I have not confirmed the exact link between each outage and these flaws. OpenAI calls the gap between AI-assisted discovery and disclosure a "Defender's Window".

**Why it matters:** Patch cycles measured in weeks were designed for human attackers. Ask your practice IT supplier how quickly web-facing components get updated, and in writing.

---

## 5. Everyone sells to everyone

CNBC reports that OpenAI's models and Codex will be offered through Google Cloud's Model Garden, further loosening the Microsoft tie. Separately, Anthropic has proposed federal legislation that would let the Commerce Department seek a court order to suspend models crossing set catastrophic-risk thresholds, and its chief executive has defended a four-month delay to its Mythos model. Both stories come from company statements and secondary coverage. I found nothing fresh from *Exponential View* or *The AI Daily Brief* that I could verify.

**The view:** A developer asking for a suspension power is either leading or lobbying, and the threshold definition is where we find out which. For the NHS, multi-cloud availability means procurement leverage, if anyone uses it.

### From the Eye

**Item one** Two Google DeepMind researchers, Sofia Liguori this week and Andreas Kirsch earlier in October, have resigned citing concern over Google's visibility into Pentagon use of its AI; Google says its public-sector contracts go through compliance review. **Item two** OpenEvidence and Doximity have settled their lawsuit, in which OpenEvidence had alleged Doximity staff posed as doctors to get into its system, a sentence which tells you where clinical AI is commercially. **Item three** A US physician argues from Jevons's paradox that AI will increase the volume of medical services rather than replace doctors, noting that there are more radiologists now than a decade ago despite the predictions. I recall similar promises about electronic records saving time.
