## 1. Five in six clinicians are using AI — and nobody told them how

A survey of 1,823 clinicians across 25 countries, run by the ambient-scribe firm Heidi and published this month as its *Pressure Points* report, found that 83% are using AI at work with no employer guidance, no formal policy and no recommended tool. Some 86% use it daily or several times a week, usage running highest among the *most* experienced practitioners — not the digital natives you might expect. Documentation remains the great millstone, named by 88% as their most time-consuming task, while 68% cite hallucinations and accuracy as their chief worry, ahead of privacy and over-reliance.

**The view from the surgery:** this is the real state of play, and it should worry the people who write the policies more than the people breaking them. When the governance vacuum is this complete, "adoption" is just thousands of clinicians quietly improvising with tools their trust has never assessed, indemnified or even acknowledged. The registry and the NHS guidance exist; the daily reality has simply overtaken them. The honest question isn't how to slow the horse — it's already bolted — but how to write rules that the 83% will actually recognise as describing what they already do.

## 2. Predicting who survives a brain bleed — without the scan

A newly reported machine-learning model predicts 30-day mortality after intracerebral haemorrhage using only routine clinical data gathered in the first 24 hours — bloods, observations, the ordinary bedside numbers — and matches the accuracy you'd expect from models leaning on advanced imaging. The claim isn't that scans don't matter clinically; it's that for *prognosis*, the humble dataset every unit already collects carries most of the signal.

**Why it matters:** for a stroke unit in a hospital without round-the-clock neuroimaging, that's a genuinely useful bit of kit — triage and family conversations grounded in something better than a gestalt. But prognostic models near the end of life carry a familiar hazard: a number that predicts poor survival can quietly become the reason care is withdrawn, and the prediction fulfils itself. A tool that informs the conversation is a good thing; a tool that ends it is not. The difference is entirely in how we choose to use it.

## 3. The RCGP builds an AI coach for the exam

The Royal College of General Practitioners has been piloting risr/'s *AI Clinical Coach*, testing whether a model can give trainees scalable, on-demand practice for the Simulated Consultation Assessment — the licensing exam's role-play component — with formative feedback between real teaching sessions. It's a modest, sensible use case: not diagnosing patients, but rehearsing the consultation itself.

**The view:** of all the places to point a language model, exam preparation is one of the safer bets — a low-stakes sandbox where a hallucination costs a practice point, not a patient. Having trained young GPs for a decade, I'd note the obvious risk too: an AI examiner rewards whatever pattern it was tuned to reward, and trainees are exquisitely good at learning to game the marker. Rehearsal is valuable; teaching to the algorithm is how you produce doctors who pass the SCA and freeze in the room. Worth watching whether the College keeps a human in the loop.

## 4. The EU AI Act's clock ticks — but the medical bits are delayed

The AI Act's enforcement phase opened on 10 July, and 2 August brings the next tranche: Article 50 transparency duties (chatbot disclosure, AI-content labelling, deepfake marking) and the Commission's enforcement powers over general-purpose models. But in an amendment passed in June, Brussels quietly pushed the high-risk obligations for AI *embedded in regulated medical devices* out from 2027 to August 2028 — a two-year reprieve for the SaMD manufacturers.

**Why it matters:** the pattern is becoming familiar — the flashy, consumer-facing transparency rules arrive on time and get the headlines, while the genuinely hard part, governing the clinical software that actually touches patients, slips gently rightward. There's a defensible case that the standards weren't ready. There's a less flattering one, that medtech lobbied for room and got it. Either way, the practical upshot for a European clinician is that the AI in your diagnostic device will run largely ungoverned by this Act until 2028, whatever the marketing on the transparency provisions suggests.

## 5. Deepfake fraud crosses a line — and your patients are the market

Deepfakes now account for roughly 11% of all global fraudulent activity, and one of the fastest-growing vectors is fabricated video of central bankers and heads of government "endorsing" crypto platforms, aimed squarely at elderly investors. Voice-cloning scams — the panicked grandchild, the bank's fraud team, the trusted official — have industrialised into a production line linking fake-document generators to the same underground markets that trade stolen data.

**The view from the surgery:** this is drifting into our territory whether we like it or not. The lonely, the recently bereaved, the mildly cognitively impaired — the people who fill our waiting rooms — are precisely the demographic these operations optimise for, and a life-savings loss lands on us as a mental-health presentation weeks later. It's not a medical story yet, but the fallout is thoroughly clinical. A word to older patients about "if it's a video asking for money, it's fake" may soon belong in the same bracket as the smoke-alarm question.

### From the Eye

**The accountability black hole:** the sharpest observation of the week from the health-AI trade press wasn't about capability but liability — the real bottleneck in agentic AI, the one that books the appointment and chases the referral, is no longer *can it act* but *who's accountable when it acts wrong, incompletely, or outside its bounds*. Nobody has built that architecture yet, and everybody is shipping the agents anyway. **The chatbot that never closes:** a vendor report notes that 29% of patients' AI health conversations now happen outside business hours, and 87% are "resolved" without escalation to a human — a statistic that will read as either a triumph of access or a vast unaudited out-of-hours service depending on how many of that 87% were quietly wrong. Those of us who built OOH co-ops for a living have seen this film. **A reprieve for the mouse:** buried in the UK's £75m animal-testing roadmap is a concrete near-term milestone easy to miss amid the AI noise — animal testing for skin and eye irritation is due to end by the close of *this* year, with DNA-based methods replacing mice for Botox potency testing by 2027. The organ-on-a-chip and in-silico crowd finally have deadlines, not just aspirations.
