## 1. Leeds says the scribe saves 13 minutes a patient

Leeds Teaching Hospitals is extending its use of the TORTUS ambient voice tool after a three-month pilot, February to April, in the ambulatory part of the emergency department at Leeds General Infirmary. The trust's evaluation reports that documentation took 61% less time, about 13 minutes saved per consultation. No serious safety incident was logged. Next come emergency nurses, majors, minors and paediatrics. This is the trust's own service evaluation of one area of one department, and I have trade-press reports only.

**The view from the surgery:** Thirteen minutes is far more than the 16 minutes per eight-hour day found in the big American study in April, so I would want to know how the time was measured before believing it. Ambulatory emergency care is also the tidy end of the department; majors at 3am is the real test.

## 2. The chatbot has a favourite brand

A study in *Nature Health*, led by Daiju Ueda of Osaka Metropolitan University with colleagues at MD Anderson, put 177,120 synthetic patient scenarios to five AI models and asked which medicine they would suggest. One report says the most-recommended product was chosen 97.5% of the time, and the models leaned towards branded drugs over generics. Between 12.4% and 14.7% of each model's recommendations failed a check against the regulator's records, mostly by naming a product that is no longer sold. The authors caution that invented scenarios do not measure real prescribing.

**Why it matters:** Yesterday's item had drug companies paying to learn how to get their evidence in front of the tool the prescriber asks. This suggests the tool already has preferences, and nobody chose them on purpose.

## 3. A risk score from the mammogram you already had

The FDA cleared Mammo Risk, from RadNet's subsidiary DeepHealth, on 23 November. It is reported as the first cleared AI tool to estimate a woman's three-, four- and five-year breast cancer risk from an ordinary 3D screening mammogram, with no extra scan. The company says it was tested on more than 65,000 screening examinations at ten American sites, but its press release gives no accuracy figures. A rival five-year risk tool from Clairity was authorised in May 2025.

**The view:** EDITH, which began on Monday, asks whether AI can find the cancer that is there. This asks who will get one, which is a harder claim to check and leads straight to the question of what you then offer the woman. Without accuracy figures I would not offer her anything yet.

## 4. Azhar says the AI boom is covering its costs

Azeem Azhar and William Gildea of *Exponential View* published "Borrowed Time" yesterday, arguing that the evidence points away from an AI overbuild. On the summary I have, they calculate that AI infrastructure revenue covers 143% of the $185 billion annual cost of the capital now in service, and is growing at 271% a year. They name three warning signs of a bust: coverage falling below 100%, growth slowing below 35% a year, and three consecutive months of falling GPU rental prices. I have a secondary digest, not the essay.

**The take:** It is to their credit that they say what would prove them wrong, which bubble-deniers seldom do. I note the title all the same; an analyst who thought the money was safe would have chosen another.

## 5. State hackers pick up an AI attack tool

Microsoft Threat Intelligence reported this week that six hacking groups it tracks have adopted ARTEX, an open-source framework in which AI agents carry out penetration testing. Among them are China's Salt Typhoon and North Korea's Onyx Sleet. In one attack Microsoft logged more than 151,000 failed sign-ins against Microsoft 365 accounts, peaking at 2,900 a minute. Microsoft says ARTEX adds no new technique and, by default, makes no effort to hide.

**Why it matters:** The methods are old and the labour is now free. NHS organisations run on Microsoft 365, and password spraying at that rate finds the one account without two-factor sign-in.
