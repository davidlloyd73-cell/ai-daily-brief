## 1. EDITH sends out her first invitations

The NHS began inviting women into the EDITH trial yesterday, starting at Cambridge University Hospitals, the first of about 30 breast screening sites. Every screening mammogram is currently read by two specialists; the trial tests whether AI can safely stand in for one of them. Press coverage says nearly 700,000 women, while Warwick's own trial page says 660,000. Women aged 50 to 71 will find the details in their routine invitation letter and can opt out; the appointment itself does not change. Warwick and Cambridge lead it, with tools from four companies and about £10 million from the NIHR.

**The view from the surgery:** This is how it should be done: a randomised trial at national scale, before the rollout and not after. Expect questions from women holding the letter, and the honest answer is that a human still reads every film.

## 2. A sandbox for the mental health chatbot

The MHRA and NICE launched an "AI in Mental Health Airlock" yesterday, an extension of the MHRA's existing regulatory sandbox, run with NICE's HTA Innovation Laboratory. Developers of AI mental health tools can apply until 5pm on 22 January; one report says up to five will be chosen to work alongside regulators, clinicians and people with lived experience. It sits within a Wellcome-funded programme that began in 2023 and runs to October 2028. The GOV.UK page calls the exercise "strictly exploratory". I have summaries, not the full notice.

**Why it matters:** On Sunday I reported that the FDA has authorised no mental health chatbot while a fifth of young Americans use one. Our regulators are at least working out how such a thing would be assessed, though five products in a sandbox until 2028 is slow.

## 3. Oregon wants $2 billion from OpenAI

Oregon's attorney general, Dan Rayfield, sued OpenAI in Multnomah County on 17 November, seeking at least $2.035 billion. The complaint alleges that ChatGPT was deliberately engineered to foster dependence and give "sycophantic" answers, and that the company pursued children directly with a product called ChatGPT for Teens while knowing the risks. Eight other states sued in October. I found no response from OpenAI, and these are allegations, not findings.

**The view:** Nine states is no longer a stunt. If discovery goes ahead, we may finally see what the company knew about dependence in young users, which matters more to a GP than the size of the cheque.

## 4. Huang is back, and the President takes a bow

Nvidia's board unanimously reinstated Jensen Huang as president and chief executive yesterday, less than two weeks after he resigned following press reports of extramarital affairs, and six days after the board said it had no intention of doing so. President Trump had urged his return and said afterwards: "The board decided to do what we wanted them to do." The shares rose 31%. The company's independent investigation continues, as do the government inquiries.

**The take:** I wrote on Sunday that Nvidia had no permanent chief executive; it has one again. But a board that reverses itself in six days under presidential pressure is not exercising independent oversight, and the questions it was asking have not been answered.

## 5. OpenAI's chief scientist asks for a speed limit

Jakub Pachocki published an essay on Sunday, "Perplexity and Intelligence", proposing that AI developers agree shared capability limits and not cross them until the monitoring of model behaviour reaches a concrete milestone. He suggests perplexity, a measure of how well a model predicts text, as the common yardstick, and warns that AI could begin speeding up its own training research as early as 2027. Secondary reports disagree on whether he names a threshold. Nothing binds anyone.

**Why it matters:** When the person building the thing says monitoring is not keeping pace, believe him. A voluntary limit proposed by one competitor, on a measure it chose itself, is still only a proposal.

### From the Eye

**Item one** South East Coast Ambulance Service's "PARA-AI" study, reported by HTN on 9 November, surveyed 171 of its paramedics and interviewed 14: 92% want paramedics involved in designing and testing any AI tool, a sensible request that procurement rarely grants. **Item two** At the Australian college's GP26 conference in Adelaide, Pulse+IT reported yesterday, the Australian Digital Health Agency said it is tightening clinical governance because more patients are using AI to interpret their own results before they see anyone; I have the opening paragraph only. **Item three** *Exponential View*'s chart column picked up a Pew analysis of more than 20,000 Facebook and Instagram political ads mentioning data centres: in October 57% were hostile and 3% supportive, where in January supporters had the edge at 42% to 31%.
