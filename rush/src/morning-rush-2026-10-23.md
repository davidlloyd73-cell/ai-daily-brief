## 1. AMIE meets the urgent-appointment queue

*The Lancet* published Google's feasibility study of AMIE, its conversational diagnostic AI, on 8 October. At a Boston primary care clinic, patients with urgent visits chatted with the system from home before seeing their doctor, with a physician watching each conversation live. Of 1,452 visits scheduled, 140 patients consented and 98 completed both the chat and the consultation. No conversation met a pre-set safety-stop criterion, there was one reported hallucination, and AMIE's differential included the final diagnosis in 90% of cases. One exchange was rated somewhat harmful because the patient may have been alarmed by lymphoma being raised. Fewer than half of patients felt the system would keep their information confidential.

**The view from the surgery:** Safe under constant supervision, in volunteers, at one clinic, funded by Google, with no control group. That is a feasibility result, not evidence of benefit. The pre-visit summary is the useful bit; the differential handed to an anxious patient at home is the risky bit.

## 2. Another trust, another scribe

Digital Health reports that Rotherham, Doncaster and South Humber NHS Foundation Trust is rolling out Accurx Scribe, built on Tandem Health's technology, in older people's mental health services. It drafts notes, summaries and referral letters that the clinician checks before anything enters the record. The local MP watched it write a note live. No figures on time saved or accuracy were given, so the benefits are early and self-reported.

**Why it matters:** Long, rambling, family-heavy consultations are where a scribe should earn its keep, and also where an invented detail does most damage. Ask any trust announcing one for the error audit, not the anecdote.

## 3. The doctor in the advert is not a doctor

Reports this month, including INTERPOL's warning that deepfakes defeat the usual signs of trust, describe AI-generated videos using real doctors' faces and voices to sell bogus treatments. The American Medical Association raised the same worry earlier in the year, and Canadian experts say it is getting harder to tell real from fake.

**The view:** Your patients will quote a "doctor" they saw on social media, and he or she may not exist. Worth a line in the practice newsletter, and a standing way to report impersonation of real clinicians.

## 4. One in eleven

Goldman Sachs economist Joseph Briggs put out a note yesterday saying up to 9% of workers, roughly one in eleven, could be displaced during the AI transition, with slower hiring costing another 3%. That is up from 6–7% in August 2025. He argues the losses are likely to cluster in a recession, not arrive in a healthy economy, and notes that only 23% of US establishments have built AI into regular workflows. Gross job losses are not unemployment, and the numbers rest on a 15% productivity assumption and on past technologies.

**Why it matters:** Estimates drift upward while the observed data stay modest, which tells you how much is assumption. Clinical work is not top of the exposure list, but the admin around it is.

## 5. Policy by cyber-incident

A Fox News round-up of Thursday's AI politics gives a flavour: an ABC/Washington Post/Ipsos poll finds 57% of US adults think AI is bad for society; Microsoft reports Chinese state-backed hackers using an open-source agentic AI framework in a supply-chain campaign; VirusTotal found 90 malicious AI agent "skills" hiding prompt-injection instructions from human reviewers; and Florida's governor floated criminal liability for AI executives. It is a live blog, so treat the detail as secondhand. I found nothing fresh and verifiable from *Exponential View* or *The AI Daily Brief* today.

**The view:** Agent skills are the new browser extensions. If your clinical system lets staff install them, someone should be reading what they ask for.

### From the Eye

**Item one** The AMIE paper's one "somewhat harmful" interaction was a chatbot mentioning lymphoma to a patient at home. Every GP knows that sentence cannot be unsaid, and no safety-stop criterion captures a bad night's sleep. **Item two** Rhode Island has sued Meta, alleging its AI ad tools helped scammers, while Reservoir Media has sued Anthropic and Suno over training on its music: the courts are slowly becoming the regulator by default. **Item three** Goldman's note records that AI-attributed layoffs in Challenger's tracking have fallen sharply from their spring peak, which sits oddly with the headline figure. Forecasters are always gloomier than the ledger.
