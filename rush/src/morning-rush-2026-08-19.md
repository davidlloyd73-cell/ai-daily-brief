## 1. Half of British general practice is now using AI

The third wave of an annual survey of UK GPs finds 52% now use AI in their work, up from 25% last year and 20% in 2024 — a threefold rise in three years. The composition matters more than the headline. Among the 522 GPs using AI, the most common tool by a distance was ChatGPT at 67%, followed by Heidi Health at 46% and Microsoft Copilot at 24%. The 2026 figures sit in a Research Square preprint that has not yet been peer-reviewed; the two earlier waves were published in BMJ Health & Care Informatics and Digital Health respectively.

**The view from the surgery:** two-thirds of AI-using GPs are reaching for a general-purpose consumer chatbot, not a procured clinical product. That is not an adoption curve, it is a workaround, and it is what happens when the tool people are permitted to use is slower than the tool they can open in a browser tab. Treat the caveat seriously — it is a preprint, and self-selected survey samples flatter enthusiasts — but the direction across three consistent waves is hard to argue with.

## 2. The scribe adopters have a profile, and it's the trainers

A cross-sectional survey of 598 UK GPs, published in npj Digital Medicine and supported by Barts Charity and Cancer Research UK, found 40% currently using AI scribes and a further 23% who had used them and stopped. Among current users, mean deployment was 60% of consultations, with a range from 5% to 100%. Adoption was significantly more likely among men, GPs in private practice (odds ratio 2.88), those with 5–6 clinical sessions a week (2.17), the more experienced (1.68) — and, most strikingly, GP trainers, at an odds ratio of 3.10. The authors call for national standards covering liability, data security, training and clinical governance.

**Why it matters:** the 23% who tried it and gave up is the number nobody quotes, and it deserves a study of its own. But the trainer finding is the one with consequences. The people most likely to be running an ambient scribe are the people teaching the next generation how to consult. Whatever habits are being formed at the desk are being transmitted, and there is still no national standard describing what good looks like.

## 3. An anticholinergic alert, switched on quietly

Calderdale and Huddersfield NHS Foundation Trust confirmed yesterday that it goes live on 1 September with a clinical decision support tool embedded in its electronic record, built around the Anticholinergic Medication Index and flagging cumulative anticholinergic burden in patients aged 65 and over. No press conference, no foundation model, no pilot cohort of four thousand clinicians. Just a burden score surfaced at the point of prescribing.

**The view:** this is the shape of the deployments that will actually stick. Narrow, bounded, safety-critical, auditable, and comprehensible to a trust board in one sentence. Anticholinergic burden in the over-65s is a problem every one of us has met — the falls, the confusion, the slow accretion of oxybutynin and amitriptyline and a sedating antihistamine nobody reviewed. If the tool halves that, it will have done more measurable good this year than most of what gets a launch event.

## 4. The Ohio guarantee shrinks by $145bn

A securities filing on Monday confirmed Nvidia will guarantee up to $105bn of financing for OpenAI's vast new data centre at the PORTS-Pike campus in Pike County, Ohio — supporting an initial 4.25 gigawatts of compute with an option on 3.75 more, eventually 8GW, requiring at least 10GW of new generation, phased from 2028. The interesting number is the trajectory. When the arrangement first leaked in July the figure reported was as high as $250bn; the Wall Street Journal had it below $120bn by 14 August; it landed at $105bn on signature.

**The view from the surgery:** a deal that halves twice on its way to the signature page is a deal whose demand assumptions are being revised in public. Nvidia guaranteeing the credit that buys Nvidia chips has always had a circularity problem, and the market noticed it in July when the shares dropped four and a half per cent on the news. Worth holding in mind next time an NHS supplier quotes you a price predicated on compute getting cheaper forever.

## 5. ChatGPT builds a separate door for teenagers

OpenAI began a global rollout yesterday of ChatGPT for Teens, aimed at 13 to 17 year olds, with content restrictions around suicide, self-harm and romantic or sexual conversation, study support designed to teach rather than answer, and explicit instruction not to use terms of endearment or imply that it has feelings — a deliberate attempt to reduce anthropomorphism and emotional dependence. Users who state their age go in automatically, but so do users whom OpenAI's age-prediction system estimates to be under 18, overriding a previously entered adult birth date. The company has not published how accurately that prediction performed in testing. Rollout completes within two weeks.

**Why it matters:** the anti-anthropomorphism instruction is the substantive change, and it is a tacit admission of what the previous product was doing. The age prediction is the part to watch: an unpublished classifier now decides which safety regime a child receives, and the failure mode nobody is discussing is the seventeen-year-old in genuine distress who is quietly routed somewhere more cautious than they needed, or the nineteen-year-old who isn't.

### From the Eye

**Item one** — the HSE published Ireland's first national Framework for Health Innovation on Monday, setting out governance, lifecycle and implementation pathways for adopting health innovation, which moves Ireland from ad hoc pilots towards something recognisably NHS-shaped; whether that is progress or a warning depends on which side of the Irish Sea you sit, but it is worth watching, not least because a small system with one framework may end up outrunning a large one with forty. **Item two** — University Hospitals of Leicester has quietly won a five-year national contract to deliver digital structured education for adults with diabetes across the whole of England, extending its MyDESMOND platform to cover Type 1 for the first time; single-platform national contracts of this kind are now the default route to scale in condition management, which is excellent news if your platform has an evidence base and terminal for everyone else, and it means the thing you refer your newly diagnosed patients to is about to become the same thing everywhere. **Item three** — Innovate UK's Women in Innovation cohort handed £75,000 grants to 61 founders earlier this month, among them AI cutting MRI scan times by up to 90%, home diagnostics for iron deficiency and cervical cancer, and a wearable that detects dehydration; set that against the ten-figure device deals signed the same fortnight and you have the state of British health innovation funding in one line — the grant end is healthy, the mega-deal end is healthy, and the Series B in between is where good companies still go to die.
