## 1. Switch off the AI, lose six points of detection

The most uncomfortable AI paper of the week isn't about what the machine gets wrong — it's about what happens to us. A multicentre colonoscopy trial found that when endoscopists who had grown used to AI polyp-detection went back to working unaided, their adenoma detection rate fell from 28.4% to 22.4%. With the AI running it held around 25%. In other words, regular use of the tool appears to have quietly eroded the operators' own unaided eye. It sits alongside a fresh *Annals of Internal Medicine* piece bluntly titled "The Deskilling Effect" and a scoping review in ESMO's real-world data journal reaching the same direction of travel across radiology and pathology.

**The view from the surgery:** six points of ADR is not a rounding error — it's the difference between a good list and a missable cancer. We've spent two years asking whether the AI is accurate. The better question is what it does to the clinician standing next to it when it's taken away.

## 2. Four thousand studies, nineteen trials

A sobering piece of stocktaking landed in *Nature Medicine*: an LLM-assisted systematic review of large language models in clinical medicine identified 4,609 peer-reviewed studies published between January 2022 and September 2025. Of those, only 1,048 used real-world patient data — and just 19 were prospective randomised trials. The rest lived on simulated vignettes, exam questions and retrospective notes. The literature, in short, is enormous and largely weightless.

**Why it matters:** the noise-to-signal ratio in medical AI is now formally documented. When a vendor waves a folder of "peer-reviewed evidence" at your ICB, the right response is to ask which of the nineteen it belongs to.

## 3. The doctors on your patients' feeds aren't the doctors

Deepfaked physicians are now a going concern. Fact-checkers have pulled hundreds of manipulated videos using the altered likenesses of real, respected clinicians to sell unproven supplements — menopause remedies a particular favourite — and a *Radiology* study found most clinicians couldn't reliably spot a faked X-ray. The American Medical Association has now demanded legislation to modernise identity protection and force platforms to pull impersonations faster.

**The view:** patients have always arrived clutching nonsense from the internet. The new twist is that the nonsense now wears a white coat and a familiar face, and your gentle correction is competing with something engineered to look more authoritative than you.

## 4. A two-track labour market — and radiology's in the fast lane

PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer argues the workforce has bifurcated. "Professionalised" roles — where AI handles the routine and pays out on human judgement — are seeing roughly twice the job growth and 42% faster wage growth than "democratised" roles where the tool flattens the skill premium. The barometer's own example of a winner is, of all things, the radiologist; the losers include medical secretaries. The Bloomberg jobs data underneath it is less cheerful: information and finance sectors are shedding around 28,000 posts a month.

**Why it matters:** the profession spent a decade being told radiology would be the first medical casualty of AI. The early data suggests the opposite — expert interpreters are becoming more valuable, while the administrative scaffolding around them thins out. Worth remembering when the scribe rollout reaches your reception desk.

---

## 5. The AI economy finally pays its own electricity bill

Azeem Azhar's *Exponential View* did the sums this month and found a threshold quietly crossed: generative AI turned over more than $110bn in the past year and now runs above a $175bn annualised pace, with quarterly sales outside China (~$25bn) finally exceeding quarterly infrastructure depreciation (~$21bn). For the first time the revenue is outrunning the rot on the hardware. He also flags that Fable 5 now completes 16% of real freelance projects at professional quality — roughly double the prior best.

**The view:** the bubble talk doesn't disappear on one quarter's figures — hyperscalers are still set to spend ~$850bn this year, much of it borrowed. But "it never makes money" is no longer quite true, and that changes the argument.

### From the Eye

**Item one** — the Character.AI case is worth a second look for the detail: the bot, "Emilie", didn't just imply competence, it allegedly claimed to be licensed in Pennsylvania and volunteered a fictional licence number. A machine that hallucinates a regulator's stamp is a new species of medico-legal problem, and no one owns it yet. **Item two** — buried in Azhar's roundup: a 35-billion-parameter model, trained differently, now matches trillion-parameter systems on some long-horizon benchmarks. If small-and-clever keeps beating big-and-brute, the economics of on-premise NHS models look a lot less fanciful than they did a year ago. **Item three** — the same roundup notes that more than 95% of Nvidia's Grace-Blackwell GPUs, shipping since December 2024, still haven't been switched on. A world both convinced it's short of compute and sitting on a warehouse of unplugged silicon is telling you something about how much of this is demand and how much is anticipation.
