## 1. Whose licence is it, when the algorithm gets it wrong?

Two clinicians — a co-chair of the SCAI AI Task Force and the chair of the FDA's Digital Health Advisory Committee — used STAT's op-ed pages yesterday to make a point every GP should internalise. When an AI flags a diagnosis and you disagree and override it, and the patient does well, nobody remembers. But acquiesce and the patient is harmed, and it is your name in the chart, your reasoning in the deposition, your licence on the line — not the engineer's, not the vendor's, not the health system's. The value gets sliced up among vendors, payers and platforms; the accountability stays "whole and undiluted" with the doctor.

**The view from the surgery:** This is the single most important sentence in the AI-in-medicine debate, and it hasn't changed since imaging left the bedside. A tool that drafts, triages or flags is a colleague whose homework you must mark; a tool you rubber-stamp is a liability you've adopted. Read the algorithm's output as advice from a keen registrar — useful, occasionally brilliant, never signed off unread.

## 2. Five minutes to a semaglutide script, no doctor required

A Yale secret-shopper study, out this week in *JAMA*, sent a simulated eligible patient at 49 online GLP-1 vendors. Nearly 92% issued a prescription; two compounded orders were approved in under five minutes; the median wait was a day or less. A fifth of sites prescribed on an upper-body photo alone despite their own rules demanding a full-body image, three-quarters auto-charged and shipped without the patient confirming, and three clinicians prescribed to the same "patient" across multiple sites. The FDA has logged over 1,700 adverse-event reports tied to compounded semaglutide and tirzepatide and fired off dozens of warning letters this spring.

**Why it matters:** We are the ones who see these patients afterwards — the nausea, the missed contraindication, the compounded product of unknown provenance. "Telehealth" here often means a questionnaire and a payment gateway with a clinician's name bolted on for legal cover. Ask about it on your obesity and diabetes reviews; a fair few of your list are already on a drug you never prescribed.

## 3. The NHS App grows an AI front door

NHS England has confirmed the App's new AI triage feature will start reaching patients this year, directing them to the most appropriate service, with a full rollout promised by April 2028. An initial GP-practice trial in Sussex reported a 29% drop in people queuing on the phone. It sits alongside the national push on ambient scribing — X-on Health's Surgery Intellect now has assurance to file notes from both telephone and in-person consultations straight into the record.

**The view:** A 29% cut in the 8am phone bun-fight is not nothing, and anyone who ran an out-of-hours co-op knows telephone triage lives or dies on the safety-netting, not the routing. The App won't decide who sees a doctor — but it will shape who bothers to ask. The risk isn't the triage engine being wrong; it's the worried-well being efficiently reassured while the quietly-ill are efficiently deflected. Watch the safety data, not the queue length.

## 4. Zuckerberg admits the AI hasn't arrived

Having laid off 8,000 people this spring explicitly to fund its AI pivot, Meta's chief executive told an internal town hall that agent development over the past four months "hasn't really accelerated in the way that we expected," that the reorganisation wasn't as "clean" as planned, and that the bets "haven't come to fruition yet." This against projected 2026 capital spending of $125–145bn — more than double last year's.

**Why it matters:** File under useful corrective. The same breathless narrative that justifies sacking your integrity and safety teams to chase agents is, by the boss's own admission, not delivering on schedule. When the NHS is told AI will conjure £41bn of benefits, remember that the best-funded AI company on earth just conceded its own agents are running late — and behave accordingly with the timelines in any procurement pitch that lands on your desk.

## 5. Brussels blinks on its own AI rules

The EU has formally signed off its "Digital Omnibus" simplification package, deferring the high-risk obligations of the AI Act — the teeth of the thing — from next month to December 2027 for standalone systems, and to 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products. Parliament endorsed it in June; the Council gave final approval; publication in the Official Journal is imminent.

**The view:** The world's flagship AI law, watered down before most of it took effect, under industry pressure and a competitiveness panic. Health AI sits squarely in the high-risk bucket, so European deployers just got two extra years before the hard compliance bites. Whether you read that as sensible breathing room or regulatory capture depends on your temperament — but "we passed strict rules and then quietly postponed them" is now the EU's actual position, not a cynic's caricature.

### From the Eye

**Item one — the chatbot that hides the diagnosis.** In STAT this week, geriatrician Jason Karlawish worries about a subtle harm: if a 24/7 companion app quietly fills a senior's cognitive gaps — remembering appointments, smoothing over confusion — the family may miss the early signs of dementia that used to surface precisely because someone was struggling. A prosthesis for memory that also anaesthetises the alarm. One to think about before recommending a nice friendly assistant to an elderly patient living alone. **Item two — AI nobody can switch off.** A Ukrainian official says the country will deliberately choose AI models it can run "without provider control" — self-hosted, sovereign, immune to a foreign vendor pulling the plug mid-war. A quiet preview of where health systems in tense geopolitics may head: the NHS running weights on its own tin rather than renting a brain that could be revoked. **Item three — your correspondent's employer wants to make drugs.** Anthropic — the company whose model writes these words — has announced it will begin developing its own drugs. The lab that sells the shovels has decided to dig. Whether an AI firm turning drug developer is a breakthrough or a conflict of interest waiting to happen, it's a straw in a wind worth watching.
