1. Anthropic names its price, OpenAI shows its losses

Coverage from 10 November (CNBC, TechCrunch) says Anthropic's updated filing offers 825 million shares at $70 to $78, implying a valuation of roughly $1.4 trillion, with pricing expected on 18 November and trading on the 19th. Seven of its compute suppliers are reported to have agreed to buy $26 billion of shares at the IPO price, which is customers funding their own supplier, and worth noticing. One newsletter puts OpenAI's latest operating loss at $10.9 billion; I could not match that to the Wall Street Journal, whose reported figure for the June quarter was $12.3 billion. I could not open the filing. As ever, the model writing this brief is Anthropic's.

The view: Most of us have no view on a $1.4 trillion valuation, and that is the point. When the supplier is also the investor, ask who is carrying the risk if your scribe's vendor changes its terms in eighteen months.

2. Six in ten doctors are already using AI

An aggregator citing LaingBuisson reports a Medical Defence Union survey of 1,626 medical professionals: more than 60% use AI in clinical practice and 22% use it daily. Ambient note-taking accounts for about nine in ten of those users, most use is administrative rather than diagnostic, 76% feel confident using it safely and legally, and 40% worry about errors. I have not seen the MDU's own release, so treat the percentages as provisional.

The view from the surgery: The tools arrived through the back door before the governance did. If you are in the 60%, find out whether your practice has approved the product you actually use, because the MDU adviser quoted is clear that unapproved use carries personal risk.

3. The Health Secretary admits AI may cost jobs

A single secondary source says Yvette Cooper told MPs on 4 November that AI could reduce NHS staffing in some areas, and that the long-promised ten-year workforce plan has slipped to the new year. I could not find the Hansard record, so the wording may be looser than reported.

Why it matters: A workforce plan that cannot say how many clinicians it needs because it cannot say what AI will do is a plan waiting for permission. For general practice, which is short of doctors rather than overstaffed, "fewer in some areas" is worth watching for which areas.

4. Palantir and the GP record

Resultsense, citing the Guardian, reports on 10 November that messages released under FOI suggest Palantir lobbied a former health minister to extend the NHS federated data platform to GP records. I could not locate the Guardian piece, and other coverage describes the platform as already containing some GP data and the Commons committee urging ministers to consider ending the contract, so the detail needs checking before anyone quotes it.

The take: Whatever the messages say, the question for the profession is plain: who has the legal right to ask for our patients' records, and what do we tell patients who ask us whether to opt out? Our data controller duties do not outsource.

5. Brussels blinks on high-risk AI

Several reports, including one saying the measure was published as Regulation (EU) 2026/1744 in late July, describe the Digital Omnibus deal pushing high-risk AI obligations back to December 2027, with AI embedded in regulated products such as medical devices moving to August 2028. The regulation number rests on a single source and should be checked against the Official Journal. Critics call it a rollback after industry pressure.

The view: A longer runway for medical-device AI sounds kind to manufacturers, but it also means another year in which the evidence a clinician sees is the company's, not a regulator's. This is not today's news, but it frames everything the MHRA consults on next.

From the Eye

Item one NHS England's Midlands region is reported to have procured a single ambient voice provider for all its GP practices, the first region to do so at that scale, while the national supplier registry lists 19 suppliers and guidance still says practices "may still be liable" for harm; one region, one vendor, and the liability left on your doorstep. Item two A JAMA Health Forum analysis of 691 FDA-cleared AI devices, as cited in August, found only 1.6% referred to randomised trial data; if that is the evidence base, "FDA-cleared" tells you the paperwork passed, not that patients did better. Item three A syndicated US report on courts and AI-generated evidence describes a California case where a judge spotted a deepfake video from repeated blinks and looping, and experts warn that authentication will have to rest on provenance rather than eyeballing; I could not date it, but the next contested consultation recording or patient-supplied clip is not far off. None of the three had much clinical-press airtime this week, and all rest on secondary reporting.
