THE MORNING RUSH

A short brief on AI in medicine — and the world it's moving through. Read it before the 8am phone lines open.

Monday 10 August 2026 · ~5 min read · Issue 101

On the line today: a survey published Friday finds that seven in ten health organisations getting real value from AI still cannot scale it, and the reason has quietly stopped being trust; four in five report staff running their own unsanctioned AI tools; American medical schools start restricting scribe access for students on the grounds that writing the note is the learning; PwC's billion-advert study finds the entry-level rung being sawn off across the labour market; and cloning a convincing voice now costs thirty seconds of audio and five pounds. From the Eye: a certificate for responsible AI that only one hospital holds, drones carrying prescriptions to front doors, and a consultation nobody in general practice has opened.

1. The pilot worked. That was never the problem.

Carta Healthcare released survey findings on Friday with an uncomfortable headline number: 71% of healthcare organisations that report measurable value from an AI project are still not expanding it at pace. More telling is what has displaced what. The leading barrier to adoption is now integration with the electronic record, cited by 44% of respondents — well ahead of clinician trust and regulatory concern, each on 26%. And 26% of organisations have nobody formally accountable for AI strategy at all. Where an owner does exist, it is now most often a clinical leader rather than IT or the executive floor. Chief executive Brent Dover's summary: "Success in a controlled setting tells you the model is capable. It does not tell you the organisation is ready to operationalise it."

The view from the surgery: every one of us who lived through fundholding, PCGs, PCTs, CCGs and ICBs recognises this shape. The pilot is always brilliant because the pilot has a champion, one workflow and everybody's attention. Spread it and all three vanish. The genuinely new fact here is that trust has dropped to joint second. We have stopped arguing about whether the thing works and started arguing about whether it will open inside EMIS without four extra clicks. That is progress, of a dispiriting sort.

2. Four in five have staff running their own AI

A separate survey from Nutanix, out at the end of July, found that nearly four out of five healthcare organisations have employees outside IT implementing their own AI tools or agents. Eighty-eight per cent of healthcare IT professionals said their current infrastructure is not fully ready to support AI workloads on their own premises; 83% said internal silos make technology initiatives harder to run. The company's chief AI officer called shadow AI "perhaps the most jarring finding in our research" — high appetite among clinicians and staff, creating, in his words, massive risk around patient data.

Why it matters: shadow AI is not a hospital phenomenon. It is a receptionist pasting a complaint letter into a free chatbot to make it sound nicer, and a locum drafting a referral the same way. The honest response is not another policy nobody reads. It is giving people a sanctioned tool that is good enough that the unsanctioned one loses its appeal. Prohibition without provision is how you get shadow anything.

3. What the scribe quietly takes from the trainee

STAT reported last week that American medical schools and health systems are restricting trainee access to ambient scribes while they wait for better evidence. The worry is not accuracy. It is that the act of writing the note is where the thinking happens. "The process of deliberately crafting the note forces us to use our brains to really wrestle with what's happening," said Jaideep Talwalkar, associate dean for educational technology at Yale. "There's an importance in doing that with great repetition." Educators want graduates fluent in the technology and are simultaneously nervous about what off-loading the differential does during the formative years.

The view from the surgery: I trained young GPs for a decade and this is the first AI question that has genuinely given me pause. The scribe has been the best thing to happen to my working week in years — but I have thirty-five years of pattern recognition sitting underneath it. An ST1 does not. There is a real distinction between a tool that removes typing from an experienced clinician and one that removes reasoning from an inexperienced one, and nobody in the UK has written the training rule that reflects it.

4. The bottom rung is being sawn off

PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer, published on 31 July from more than a billion job adverts across 27 countries, describes a labour market splitting in two. Entry-level roles exposed to AI are now seven times more likely to demand traditionally senior skills — judgement, leadership. Those roles have grown 35% since 2019 while other entry-level roles fell 10%. Jobs requiring AI skills are growing 69% against 9% for the market overall, and the wage premium for those skills has climbed to 62%, up from 57%.

Why it matters: this is the mechanism by which "AI won't take your job" becomes "AI took the job you would have had at twenty-three." Medicine is unusually protected — you cannot skip F1 — but our administrative and coding roles are not, and those are exactly the jobs local school leavers take. If the first rung requires judgement, where does judgement come from?

5. Thirty seconds and five pounds

UK deepfake attempts rose 94% over the past year while overall fraud volume stayed roughly flat, according to banking and identity-verification data reported this month, against total losses of about £1.28bn. Cloning a serviceable version of a voice now takes around thirty seconds of audio and a few pounds of AI credit. The government's Fraud Strategy 2026–2029 names generative AI explicitly as lowering the barrier to credible fraud at scale.

The view: thirty seconds of audio is a voicemail. Half my list has left one. The scam that will land in Harrow is not a Nigerian prince — it is a grandchild's voice in distress at eleven at night, and the safeguarding conversation we ought to be having with older patients is a family password, not a leaflet.

From the Eye

Item one: the US Joint Commission quietly launched a Responsible Use of AI in Healthcare certification in June, and on 29 July Hackensack Meridian Health became the first health system in the country to hold it — governance, bias assessment, monitoring, patient transparency, staff training, all audited. One certificate, one holder, six weeks in. Britain has no equivalent, and the MHRA framework everyone is waiting on will regulate the software, not the organisation using it. Item two: Cleveland Clinic has signed with Zipline to fly prescriptions by drone directly to patients' homes — announced 6 August and barely covered outside the trade press, which is odd given every commissioner in England has spent a decade arguing about delivery charges for housebound patients. Item three: NHS England has a consultation open until 10 September on two new contracts for commissioning neighbourhood health services. It is the dullest document of the summer and it will decide who holds the money for the thing that replaces the walk-in centre. Nobody in general practice has opened it. Somebody should.
