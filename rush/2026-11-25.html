1. Great Ormond Street hires some agents

Great Ormond Street Hospital announced yesterday a 15-month "Agentic Hospital" programme, which the joint press release calls the first of its kind in an NHS hospital. NHS England is funding it. The software firm Hyland builds the platform with the hospital's DRIVE innovation team, and Amazon Web Services supplies cloud credits and advice. The agents will run alongside the existing manual processes, under human oversight, and will make no clinical decisions; testing starts with finance work and searching hospital policies. If the hospital wants to carry on afterwards, it says there will be a competitive procurement. All of this comes from the press release; nobody independent has looked yet.

The view from the surgery: Starting in the back office and running the agents in parallel with the humans is the cautious way to do it, and I approve. The thing to watch is whether a supplier that built the pilot on free cloud credits can really lose the tender that follows.

2. Europe's doctors and the chatbot nobody approved

A report published yesterday, covered by EUobserver, finds that doctors facing burnout in EU hospitals often upload sensitive patient data to commercial chatbots without approval; other coverage names ChatGPT and Gemini. Hospitals told the researchers they ban the practice but cannot control what staff do on their own phones. The lead researcher, Júlia Keserű, says of the EU's overlapping privacy and AI law: "There are too many rules and they are too complicated." The report also says EU-funded health startups rarely reach Europe's public hospitals. I have not seen the report itself or its methods.

Why it matters: Nobody should imagine this stops at Calais. When the approved tool is slow, absent or stuck in governance, a tired clinician will use the one in their pocket, and a ban will not change that.

3. Ant gives its medical model away

Ant Group, the Chinese payments company, on Monday released LingSante, the family of medical models behind its Afu health app, which it says has about 150 million users and handles nearly 20 million consultations a day. Two models can be downloaded freely, one of them reported at 560 billion parameters. Ant claims first place among open medical models on a benchmark it helped to build, which is no independent test. The model cards bar independent diagnosis or treatment decisions, and an international version promised for 170 countries has no date.

The view: A hospital could run this on its own servers with no patient data leaving the building, which answers the worry in item 2 rather neatly. It also has no regulator's mark, and nobody outside Ant has tested it.

4. Two states sue SpaceX over Grok

Oregon and Virginia filed coordinated lawsuits on Monday against SpaceX, its platform X and its AI arm, formerly xAI. The attorneys general, Dan Rayfield and Jay Jones, allege the company failed to stop Grok and X users making and sharing sexual deepfakes, including suspected child abuse material, while marketing the products as safe. They want restitution for victims, civil penalties and a bar on profiting from the feature. SpaceX says such material has no place on its services and that it keeps strengthening its safeguards. These are allegations.

The take: Rayfield sued OpenAI last week and SpaceX this week. American AI regulation is now being written by state attorneys general using consumer protection law, because Congress has left the field to them.

5. Guterres asks for a framework within a year

At a special summit on AI safety in Bridgetown, Barbados, on Monday, the UN Secretary-General, António Guterres, urged member states to agree a multilateral framework for managing AI risk within a year, with independent and credible oversight. He suggested starting with shared safety measures, a testing regime and basic oversight requirements. About 250 people attended. The summit creates no binding rules and names no body to do the overseeing, and Guterres leaves office at the end of December.

Why it matters: Yesterday OpenAI's chief scientist asked for a speed limit; today the UN asks for a referee. Neither request binds anybody, and the one-year deadline falls to a successor.

From the Eye

Item one At the Australian college's GP26 conference in Adelaide, Pulse+IT reported on Monday, the futurist Dr Ben Hamer told GPs that AI agents will soon handle referrals, prepare documentation and contact the practice on a patient's behalf; I have a summary only, and note that the receptionist may shortly be negotiating with software. Item two An arXiv preprint from the University of Arizona, submitted in September, surveyed clinicians from students to faculty about AI tools their institution never approved: chatbots and diagnostic aids were the most used, across every task asked about. I could not find the sample size, but it suggests item 2 is not only a European habit. Item three The AI Daily Brief's newsletter headline on the Nvidia reversal was "Too Big To Fire".
