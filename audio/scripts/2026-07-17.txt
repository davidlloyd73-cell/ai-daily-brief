David's AI brief for Friday, the seventeenth of July.

The one idea today is a Chinese model called Kimi K3, and the bold claim that comes with it — that a model as capable as the very best Western systems is now sitting, open, on your desk. The host's verdict is careful. K3 is real. It may be the first Chinese model to narrow the gap with the American closed-source frontier to under three months, and on the benchmarks it is genuinely near the top — third overall on one respected index, the strongest open model ever released, and clearly ahead of Opus on coding. But, he warns, the story that made past Chinese releases feel revolutionary — cheap, tiny, runs on your laptop — simply does not apply here. K3 is a two-point-eight-trillion-parameter behemoth. It is slow. It burns tokens — thirteen thousand of them just to reason its way to a three-thousand-token answer in one test. It costs nearly as much as the premium models. And to host it yourself you would need something like forty-four Mac Studios, or a full rack of hardware. So the picture is not a cheap giant-killer, but a genuine frontier-class open model that is big, lumbering and expensive — and one that ships, worryingly, with almost no safety guardrails at all.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing ten days earlier, thinks the whole "is China catching up" panic is, in his words, a child's fantasy. His point is that the thing everyone fears has already happened, and nothing changed — Chinese labs are just building the same kind of models the Americans are. The only development that genuinely matters, he argues, is cheaper open models offering similar performance to the premium products — and that is a threat to the industry's economics, not proof of a thrilling race. And even if open models outlive the big labs, he asks the awkward question: who actually pays to run them, when no one has yet shown that serving this kind of model can be done at a profit? Where the two men quietly agree is the real signal: Chinese open models now match the frontier, and the unsolved problem is the cost of running them.

For the surgery, two thoughts. First, the detail that should catch a clinician's eye is not the scores but the safety. Testers found K3 has almost no guardrails — so if a supplier ever pitches a scribe or triage tool, ask what model sits underneath, and what safety testing it has passed, not just how well it transcribes. And second, remember that "open" and "free" do not mean the NHS can run its own. Holding a model this size costs a fortune in hardware, so the realistic route to keeping consultation data in-house is not self-hosting — it is a clear agreement that nothing leaves the practice, and that no patient data trains anyone's model.
