David's AI brief for Monday, the seventh of September.

A Labor Day special from The AI Daily Brief, and the one idea is that the next frontier of agents is multiplayer. Twenty twenty-six has been the year of agents, the host says, but so far they have changed only the work we do alone. He quotes a survey of some sixteen and a half thousand office workers finding more of the day spent with others than alone, and another putting sixty per cent of time on what it calls work about work. That is the half agents have not touched.

The evidence he assembles all points one way. The team at Every began with each person owning a mirror agent, then moved their agents into shared spaces because that was how work actually got done. Anthropic's Claude Tag puts one shared agent in each team channel, with its own context and permissions, so nobody explains things from scratch twice; Anthropic claims nearly two thirds of its product team's code now comes from that internal shared agent. The OpenClaw maintainers, after seven quiet weeks rebuilding, built a multiplayer interface where two developers can sit in the same session. And Y Combinator's autumn request for start-ups names exactly this theme. To help teams get ahead of it, the show launches a free four-week sprint: inventory what your team actually uses, build a shared context repository, score candidate tasks on shared need, staleness cost, permission sensitivity and checkability, then ship one shared agent and repeat.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron's most recent long piece, from the first of September, borrows Adam Curtis's idea of hypernormalisation: an industry telling itself a simpler, reassuring story. The shared repository at the heart of the sprint is, in his telling, the trap itself. Models that tell every user their idea is genius let people feel productive while they prompt an ever-growing wiki of their own work that produces very little value. He also notes that after three years and hundreds of billions of dollars, there is no productivity signal visible in national statistics. The variable both men circle is whether shared context makes agents more useful or merely more visible, and the thing to watch is whether any company outside the labs ever reproduces that sixty-five per cent figure.

For general practice, three thoughts. First, a surgery is a multiplayer workplace: handovers, tasks to reception, results workflows, the multidisciplinary meeting. A shared agent that knows this week's rota, the local pathways and what was agreed last Tuesday is a plausible thing to want. If Zitron is right, it becomes a hobby for the two keen doctors and nobody else touches it, which is what happened to most intranets. Second, in the NHS shared context means shared patient data, so the permission axis dominates the scoring. Start with candidates that touch no identifiable data at all: rota, protocols, referral criteria, the practice handbook, and judge success by whether staff stop asking each other the same questions. Third, the only hard number in the edition is Anthropic's own. Shared agents in clinical teams are an experiment, so treat any pilot as one and measure a baseline before it starts.
