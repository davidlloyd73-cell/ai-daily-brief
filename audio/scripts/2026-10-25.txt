David's AI brief for Sunday, the twenty-fifth of October.

Today's One Idea from The AI Daily Brief is that personal agents are still an experiment. They have early traction, especially Meta's Muse, and the form has been around since the OpenClaw craze earlier this year. The open question is what they are actually for. Are they a chief of staff that coordinates everything you do with AI, or a proactive proxy of you that works out how to help without being asked? After a few days with Anthropic's new Claude Duet, the host's answer is that it is much less a change in how he uses AI and much more an easy-to-set-up little baby version of himself that does the jobs he does not want to do. Valuable, but future-of-the-industry valuable remains to be seen.

Today's edition is the host's own tour of how he uses AI. About sixty per cent of his time is what he calls strategic externalisation, talking ideas out while walking and having a model respond. The hardest test of a model is pushback that is based on something, and he says Claude's Opus 5.5 is the best strategy model in a long time. For high-stakes research, since his sourcing is spoken aloud in public, he has two different models grade each other. The show itself uses shockingly little AI to make. Building is done in Claude Code, and Fable's real breakthrough, he says, was lowering the energy needed to finish a project, not start one. He launches a new weekly show on Monday, called How We Got Here.

On personal agents specifically, he found the canonical pitches, such as booking flights or shopping, take as long to explain as to do. Duet was so biased toward proactivity that it overvalued work it could be proactive about, but it earned its keep on the starred-email problem, drafting replies to the kind of email he would flag and return to days later. OpenAI's Dot, by contrast, was so flat that it stonewalled six hours of strategy. His advice is not to sweat which ecosystem you pick, because any feature that works in one appears in the others within weeks.

There is no counter-source today. Ed Zitron's recent writing is about finance and market structure, and has nothing on personal workflows, so I have left his side out rather than invent a view.

For general practice, three thoughts. First, ask any tool to critique your reasoning instead of confirming it, since agreement without reasons is a liability in medicine. Second, where an error would matter, a second independent check, by another model or another clinician, catches mistakes a single pass misses, though it never replaces the clinician. Third, tools that draft email replies could help with administrative correspondence, but they read the whole mailbox, so patient information must stay out until a data protection assessment is done.
