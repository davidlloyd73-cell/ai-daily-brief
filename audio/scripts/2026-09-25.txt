David's AI brief for Friday, the twenty-fifth of September.

Today's One Idea is that cheap, instant judgment at scale is a genuinely new tool, not just another chatbot.

Ten days ago a small model called Jev launched from a company called TypeSafe, and it's already reportedly raising money at a ten billion dollar valuation, up from a two hundred million dollar seed. Jev doesn't write code or draft anything. It answers exactly three kinds of question: pick the best option from a list, score something on a scale you describe in words, or give a yes-or-no probability. That's the whole interface. What makes it different is speed and cost: forty to four hundred times cheaper than asking a full AI model, answers in under half a second, and once you're asking, extra questions in the same call are nearly free.

People have spent the ten days finding what that's actually good for. One marketer ran twelve questions across seven hundred and twenty-four live adverts in forty seconds for nine cents. Someone else had it read a website's five hundred and eighty-six pages and rebuild the internal link structure in forty-five seconds; a full AI model had barely started the same job after spending over a dollar. Another user built a live-prioritised inbox, rating a hundred emails by importance in under half a second, matching his own judgement on every one, and said, reasonably, that he wanted that yesterday. It's also being used to route work: deciding which AI skill or which level of reasoning a task actually needs before spending money on the expensive model.

The company that makes it is admirably honest about where it falls down: multi-step reasoning, counting, dates and arithmetic, and it can be inconsistent. Its own advice is to keep it away from anything high-stakes, hiring, money, security, unless there's a human checking the borderline cases, because the score comes with no reasoning attached.

Now, for general practice, that caution is the whole lesson. A tool like this is well suited to sorting a large pile quickly, flagging incoming correspondence, results, or referral letters for urgency, but the design has to stay one-way: it ranks and flags, a person decides, never the reverse. It's also worth asking your scribe or admin software supplier whether something like this is quietly running underneath already, classifying or routing before the model you actually see does its work, because that's one more component with its own error rate that nobody's told you about. And step back from the technology for a moment: the real story is economics, not intelligence. If judgment this cheap reaches practice administration, the fair question for any supplier at renewal is why you're still paying per-document rates for something that now costs almost nothing to run.
