David's AI brief for Wednesday, the twelfth of August.

Today's one idea is that mass adoption of AI agents was never a capability problem. It was a packaging problem.

The product is GrokBot, one of the first things out of the combined Cursor and SpaceX AI. You talk to it through a chat window that looks like a messaging app. Behind that window, each agent runs on its own virtual computer, signs into your web applications and drives them through the ordinary human interface. You can teach it a task by asking it to watch you do it once, and it saves the steps as a routine you can correct later. One user set up a researcher and a writer, then asked a third agent to coordinate them, fully expecting the whole thing to collapse. It worked.

Nathaniel Whittemore's point is that none of that is new. Virtual computers, multiple agents, learned workflows: all of it has existed since the start of the year. What is new is that the complexity has been hidden. Nothing to configure, nothing to toggle. You install it, connect your accounts, and start asking. Inside the two companies that built it, the bots became the interface for much of the work almost immediately. One venture investor calls it the first product that really gets the idea of a virtual colleague right. Whittemore's own caution is about credentials. He signed it into his email happily, then hesitated over an account where a mistake would matter, because computer use means genuine access, not a narrow permitted channel. For now it sits behind a tier costing three hundred dollars a month.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron's argument is that packaging was never the constraint either. Willingness to pay is. His exhibit is Microsoft's own artificial intelligence assistant for Office: an estimated three point eight billion dollars of revenue for the year against some thirty million claimed paid seats, which works out at roughly one hundred and thirty dollars per person per year. He reads that as deep discounting rather than a real business, and argues much of the remaining demand is peer pressure and attachment to productivity gains nobody has measured. A product gated behind three hundred dollars a month, on his logic, tests the enthusiasm of the already converted. Both men are polemicists, and both agree the price gates ordinary people out.

Two thoughts for our end of the world. The first is that an agent of this kind holds your actual login, not a limited key. Against a clinical system with a smartcard identity, that is a shared login by another name, and no audit trail would distinguish the machine from the clinician. The rule worth holding now, before anyone gets enthusiastic: agents may touch systems where a mistake is recoverable and attributable, and nothing else.

The second is watermarking. Anthropic has begun embedding invisible markers in the text its models generate, in every region, designed to survive copying and some editing. That makes provenance provable, which may help if a record is ever challenged. It also means pasted text can carry an origin marker the author never knew about. Another good reason to read what comes out rather than paste it.
