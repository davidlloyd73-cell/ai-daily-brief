David's AI brief for Friday, the thirtieth of October.

The One Idea today: being truly AI-native means tearing out the systems built for humans, not simply speeding them up. Nathaniel Whittemore profiles Spawn, a ten-person gaming start-up that spends about thirty times more on tokens than it pays in salaries, around three point one million dollars last month. Its fleet of named, persistent agents shipped some seventy-seven thousand code changes in a month, roughly one hundred and eight an hour. Spawn abolished pull requests, because agents spent most of their time telling each other what they were changing. It lets players' own in-game agents report bugs straight to the fleet. And it turned two years of the founder's decisions into written precedent that AI judges apply. In eight days those judges made over eleven thousand decisions, and the founder personally decided twenty-seven. Daily shipped changes rose from about one thousand to over seven thousand. What is left for humans is taste, deleting work that new models make redundant, and asking for ambitions the agents will not propose themselves. Whittemore's caution is that this is hard to copy: you need a feedback loop most products do not have, and a willingness to let agents break production.

In the headlines, an AI legal start-up has bid for the brand and data of the bankrupt law firm Weil, Gotshal and Manges, and tech earnings showed Amazon web services growing thirty-nine per cent while several share prices fell anyway.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, in a piece from the twentieth of October, argues that the AI boom is a chain of conditions that must all hold: rising hyperscaler spending, growing revenue, cheap debt and continued venture funding. He says much of the labs' enterprise revenue comes from loss-making start-ups burning tokens, which is exactly what a company spending thirty times its payroll on them might look like. He did not write about Spawn.

The variable both men orbit is whether the value agents create exceeds what they cost to run. Both accept the spending figures. The question that will resolve is whether that spend is paid for by customer revenue rather than investor money, and the thing to watch is whether Spawn or its peers publish their unit economics. Both are polemicists with incentives, one bullish, one certain of a bubble, so I would trust where they agree: compute cost is the constraint that matters.

Now, some thoughts for general practice. First, precedent rather than heroics. Spawn's written precedent is a reminder that AI scribes and triage tools behave best when our own protocols and pathways are clear and current, so tidying those is worthwhile before asking software to follow them. Second, closing the loop. Spawn's product reports its own faults. In a surgery, that means an easy way for staff to flag scribe errors, omissions and wrong codes, with someone reviewing them. The sceptical point is that a game can tolerate a bug and a consultation note cannot, so clinician sign-off on every note stays. Third, ask what the bills say about vendors. If prices today are subsidised, a supplier could change terms or fail, so ask about contract length, data export, and what happens to patient records if the company is sold. Otherwise, the healthcare relevance today is modest.
