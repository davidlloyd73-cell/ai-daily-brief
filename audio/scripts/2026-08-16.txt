David's AI brief for Sunday, the sixteenth of August.

No headlines this weekend. Instead, the one idea is a change in the questions people are asking. A year ago the argument was still about whether artificial intelligence was going to amount to anything. That argument is over, and what has replaced it is a set of far more useful questions about how to do it well. How do you stop the flood of lazy machine-written prose without banning the tool outright? How do you ration something that costs money every single time somebody uses it? And how do you keep producing genuine experts when the machine eats the very work that used to make them?

Take the money question first, because it is the one most people get wrong. Unlike ordinary software, every prompt carries a real cost. Some firms have burned through a year's budget in a matter of months. The response — spending caps, tiered access, different models for different problems — has been reported as panic, and Nathaniel Whittemore thinks that reading is unfair. Serious organisations are not slapping on limits and hoping; they are treating the technology as capital to be allocated, with routes to apply for more if you can show the value. Which leads to his sharpest point: if you demand proof of return on every single use, you will only ever get the smallest wins, because those are the easiest to prove.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron's most recent piece argues that all this sophistication is beside the point if the money never arrives. He reckons Microsoft's artificial intelligence revenue for the year is about thirty-four billion dollars, of which roughly twenty-four billion is OpenAI buying its own computing power — leaving about ten billion from everything else Microsoft sells, against more than two hundred and sixty billion in capital spending since twenty twenty-two. His conclusion is blunt: if Microsoft does not have the demand, nobody does. Note, though, where the two men agree despite opposite instincts. Both accept that tokens are a running cost, and that value has to be demonstrated per unit of work rather than per seat.

Two thoughts for general practice. The first is the training problem. There is a paper doing the rounds about what its authors call the cognitive commons: checking the machine's work requires expertise, expertise is built from years of unglamorous labour, and the unglamorous labour is exactly what gets automated first. Registrars have always learned by summarising the notes and drafting the referral and then having it picked apart in tutorial. Remove that and you keep the output but lose the apprenticeship. The remedy is dull — keep some work deliberately unassisted, and treat reviewing machine-drafted material as teaching rather than rubber-stamping.

The second is about writing. The company Clay published four rules this week: stand behind every sentence, remember that writing is thinking, spend more time writing than your reader spends reading, and longer is not better. Read those as guidance for consultation notes and they hold up perfectly. A record padded by a model is worse than a short honest one. The policy bans laziness, not the tool. I sign the note, so I have to have thought it.
