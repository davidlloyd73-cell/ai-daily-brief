David's AI brief for Friday, the eleventh of September.

The one idea today is that some launches matter for what you can do with them, and others for what they tell you everyone will be doing soon. This was the second wave of releases in a single week, and Nathaniel's reading is that two trends run through all of them. The first is voice. OpenAI has shipped its live voice model to developers: it can talk and listen at the same time, cope with a noisy room and interruptions, and hand harder tasks to a reasoning model in the background, all for five cents a minute. Cognition built Devin Voice on top of it, and one of their engineers says ninety per cent of his day's work is now spoken rather than typed. Nathaniel's advice is blunt: start shifting your own work to voice now, because the industry is pushing you there regardless.

The second trend is cheap and good enough. Cognition's new coding model reaches near-frontier scores at sixty-four per cent lower cost. DeepSeek's Flash model beats its own larger sibling at a quarter of the price. The point is not any single model but that teams are now building stacks that match capability to the job rather than paying frontier prices for everything.

Underneath both trends sits a compute wall. Demand after GPT-6 was so heavy that OpenAI paused new two-hundred-dollar subscriptions, the first open rationing by a lab. Microsoft plans to triple its data-centre capacity to thirty-eight gigawatts by twenty thirty-two, and Jensen Huang says NVIDIA can confidently deliver seventy per cent growth. One developer's summary went round the industry: the era of subsidised tokens is ending.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron's essay Concentration Risk, published three days earlier, says the money beneath all this is circular. Most of the labs' enterprise revenue, he argues, comes from a tiny group of venture-funded startups that buy tokens at list price and then give them away to their own users at a loss. Those labs justify the hyperscalers' spending, and the hyperscalers justify NVIDIA, so a compute shortage is evidence of dependency rather than demand. What both men agree on is that the free-token years are ending within months. The thing to watch is whether real businesses, rather than startups, keep paying when the price is real.

For general practice, three thoughts. First, voice arrived in the consulting room before it arrived in offices, and the new cheap two-way voice models mean the next scribes will be conversational, able to be interrupted and questioned mid-consultation. Ask any scribe supplier what their price becomes if their model costs double. Second, the cheap good-enough models are the right fit for NHS drudgery: coding letters, summarising discharges, sorting the inbox. Commission the job, not the supplier, so the model can be swapped. Third, rationing reaches the front line quietly, as slower responses on a busy Monday. Anything clinical needs a written fallback for when the model is unavailable.
