David's AI brief for Thursday, the second of July.

The one idea today is a surprise in the jobs data: the companies leaning hardest into AI are hiring more people, not fewer. Ramp and Revelio Labs matched AI spending against payroll for twenty-one thousand American businesses. Firms with high AI adoption grew headcount by ten per cent over two years, while low-adoption firms stayed flat. More striking still, entry-level hiring at those AI-heavy firms grew by twelve per cent — the very jobs everyone assumed would vanish first. The spending involved was modest, around thirty dollars per employee per month, and the hiring picked up in step with adoption, which hints at cause rather than coincidence. A Box survey of over sixteen hundred companies echoes it: seventy-nine per cent of the most mature AI adopters expect headcount to rise. Meanwhile a hard new benchmark, the Remote Labor Index, finds the best model completes just sixteen per cent of real freelance tasks to professional standard — up fourfold in eight months, but still leaving eighty-four per cent needing a human. Nathaniel Whittemore's reading: his long-held jobs optimism finally has numbers behind it, though he is careful to add that real, painful displacement in particular roles is still coming, and tech and finance are already shedding twenty-eight thousand jobs a month.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing on the thirtieth of June, sees the whole labour story as a con running in both directions. The agents sold as autonomous, he argues, actually chain people to longer hours — he points to engineers working until four in the morning alongside the bots they deploy — while the replacement threat is fear-marketing from labs whose models cost about as much as a person yet can neither replace one nor turn a profit. What is worth noticing is where the two men agree despite opposite incentives: both accept that today's AI completes only a small fraction of real work. The genuine test is whether the hiring advantage persists once the cheap money stops — watch the next Ramp follow-up.

For the surgery, three thoughts. First, that entry-level finding cuts both ways. If Whittemore is right, practices that adopt scribes and triage tools early may end up hiring more AI-literate support staff, not fewer. If Zitron is right, the same tools mostly add supervision burden — and every unverified AI note is checking work for a clinician. Second, sixteen per cent is really a medico-legal number. The scribe drafts, but the GP remains the quality gate for the other eighty-four per cent — so treat AI output as a junior's draft, never a finished record, and count the checking time into the consultation. Third, Ford rehired three hundred and fifty veteran engineers to fix AI trained on documents alone, and promptly topped the quality surveys. The NHS parable writes itself: experienced clinicians' judgement is the training signal these systems need, and letting senior GPs drift away while automating on guidelines alone is a false economy.
