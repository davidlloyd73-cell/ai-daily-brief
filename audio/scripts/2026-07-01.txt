David's AI brief for Wednesday, the first of July.

The one idea today is that Fable 5 is back, but the real story sits underneath it. After nineteen days offline, Anthropic's most capable model has returned for paying users worldwide, with up to half of your weekly usage subsidised until the seventh of July. Nathaniel Whittemore's advice is not to hoard it for your hardest technical puzzles alone. From his own use, he finds it markedly better at strategic thinking, because it holds its ground under pushback instead of caving the moment you disagree, and better at disciplined, rubric-driven writing, with fewer of the tell-tale artificial turns of phrase.

But the connective tissue across the whole edition is a frantic race to make these models cheaper to run. The Information reports that OpenAI found a technique that halved the computing needed to serve its models, letting it serve every signed-out ChatGPT user on just one hundred graphics chips. Five founders told the investor Harry Stebbings they had cut their inference spending by more than seventy-five per cent in a single day, with no loss of performance. And a coding platform called Base44 has built its own model to control costs. Whittemore's own caution is sensible: there is still no free lunch, and some of this is being tested on the least demanding users, where a drop in quality matters least.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing in his piece "AI Is Too Expensive", would read all of this the opposite way. He argues that no one has ever shown these labs are actually profitable on the inference itself, and that cheaper tokens do not fix that. He points to Anthropic's own sworn figures, which suggest it spent roughly three pounds of computing for every pound of revenue, and still loses money even though most of its income is straight usage. His line is blunt: if the margins were good, they would be sharing the margins. To him, falling prices are a subsidy war, not a path to sustainability.

The honest position is that both men are watching the same number from opposite ends, and the thing that would settle it is a single audited figure, cost per token served against the price charged, that no lab has yet published.

For our world in general practice, a couple of thoughts. If the optimists are right, the ambient scribes and letter-drafting tools we already lean on should get cheaper or better at the same price. If Zitron is right, today's very cheap tools are loss-leaders that could be withdrawn, so treat a bargain vendor as a flight risk and keep an exit plan. And watch the quieter trend of models being downgraded for routine use. For a scribe summarising a consultation, it is worth asking which model actually produced a given note, and how it flags anything it is unsure about, because a summary that drops a red-flag symptom is a safety matter, whoever is paying for the compute.
