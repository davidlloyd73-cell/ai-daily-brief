David's AI brief for Wednesday, the twenty-sixth of August.

Today's one idea is that the purity test for AI writing is going to die, but the quality test will not.

Here is what happened. Stanley Druckenmiller, one of the most respected investors alive, whose fund had not a single losing year between nineteen eighty-one and two thousand and ten, published an opinion piece in the Wall Street Journal criticising the Treasury Secretary. Within hours nobody was discussing monetary policy. They were discussing the fact that the piece had very obviously been written by a machine. It was full of the tells, and one detector put the odds of artificial authorship at one hundred per cent. Druckenmiller did not apologise. His answer was, of course I used it, for the same reason I use a calculator when I do maths. The Journal's opinion editor backed him, saying what matters is whether the argument is the author's own.

Nathaniel Whittemore's argument is that this settles the legitimacy question and opens a better one, namely how to do it well. His rules are worth having. Different kinds of writing need different rules: an email is not a strategy memo, and a memo is not an opinion piece. Quality reads as effort, and the absence of effort is glaringly obvious, which was the real offence here. Longer is not better. And writing is thinking, so the standing risk is not that you use the tool but that you quietly hand over the reasoning as well. His practical division is the one worth remembering: the beginning is human, deciding what you actually want to say; the middle, the researching, drafting and tightening, is where the machine earns its keep; and the end, is this any good, is it true, do I believe it, is human again.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing the day before, argues that the embarrassment people feel about admitting they used a model is not a dying taboo but accurate self-knowledge. He draws a hard line between outsourcing work and outsourcing thought. A script, a search through a long document, pulling some data, all fine. Composing an argument, not fine, because the words are where the thinking is supposed to show, and editing the syntax afterwards only hides the absence more skilfully. His line is that if your work is not better than the machine's, you are bad at your job. Both men are polemicists with something to sell, and the interesting thing is that they agree on the part that matters: visible laziness is contempt for the reader, and the thinking cannot be delegated.

For our own work, three thoughts. The scribe is doing summarisation, not composition, and that boundary is worth keeping sharp. It compresses what was said; you decide what it meant. Second, referral letters and reports sit closer to the opinion piece than to the email. A consultant who hears a chatbot's cadence in your letter will discount the clinical judgement inside it, fairly or not, so draft the reasoning yourself and check every drug name and every figure before it leaves the surgery. And third, for a bereaved family, there is no version of that letter that is improved by generated prose. Write it yourself.
