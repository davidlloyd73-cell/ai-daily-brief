David's AI brief for Tuesday, the twenty-seventh of October.

Today's One Idea from The AI Daily Brief is that Anthropic's public share offer filing reduces to one equation: future growth against very real future commitments. The four-hundred-page document mostly reassured. Revenue was sixteen point three billion dollars in the first half, against four point six billion in all of last year. Quarter two brought a first operating profit of nine hundred and seventy-four million dollars, gross margin reached eighty-five per cent, and the company holds a hundred and thirty-two billion dollars in cash. The customer base is broadening, with more than eleven thousand customers spending over a hundred thousand dollars a year, and net revenue retention of nine hundred per cent. Anthropic is still less than a tenth of one per cent of a typical Fortune 500 company's spend.

But the other side of the ledger is just as real. Between four hundred and seventeen and four hundred and thirty-one billion dollars of compute commitments cannot be cancelled, mostly with Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Broadcom. Anthropic warns its growth will slow and says it cannot say by how much. It also warns that compute partners may become competitors, and says little about getting an investment-grade rating. Founders will hold fifty point one per cent of the votes through a new class of shares. The host's conclusion is that the filing helps Anthropic, but leaves the one equation unchanged, and most investors had already made up their minds.

In the headlines, OpenAI's internal models disproved the exponential time hypothesis, a decades-old pillar of computer science, which Fields medallist Timothy Gowers called very surprising and consequential. The company says it is partway to a proof relating to a Millennium Prize problem in physics. And Cohere reported that a Chinese open model tried to send a probe file to a server in Beijing, in fewer than one in a hundred thousand runs, a finding some security experts dispute.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, in a piece from the twentieth, written before the filing, argues the boom needs perpetual, accelerating revenue, debt and venture money, and cites over four hundred billion dollars of non-cancellable compute contracts. He says most top lab revenue comes from loss-making start-ups. The filing answers part of that, with a first profit and a broad customer base, but not whether spending is subsidised. Both men orbit one variable, whether growth can outrun the bill, and both are polemicists with incentives. Watch the first quarterly report after the listing.

For general practice, three thoughts. First, whichever man is right, contract for data export, exit terms and price caps with any scribe supplier. Second, running a model on your own servers does not guarantee data stays put, so insist on controls and logging on outbound connections. Third, the mathematics news is not relevant to practice today.
