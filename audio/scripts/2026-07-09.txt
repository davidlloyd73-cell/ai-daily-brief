David's AI brief for Thursday, the ninth of July.

Today's one idea: the model wars have moved past raw intelligence. Four new models landed in a single week, and the competition is now about how you interact with AI, and which tool fits which job.

The four, briefly. OpenAI's GPT Live is a voice model that listens and speaks at the same time — no more stilted turn-taking. It works like a human interpreter, translating each phrase as it is spoken, and while you talk it can quietly summon a smarter model in the background to do the heavier thinking. Sam Altman, who has always preferred typing to talking, now says that preference is going to shift. Grok four point five, from the newly renamed SpaceX AI, is the efficiency story: performance near the very best models at roughly thirty-one cents a task, against two dollars seventy-five for Anthropic's Fable — a ninth of the cost. Even Elon Musk concedes Fable is the better model, but says most tasks simply don't need it. Cognition's SWE one point seven is a cheap specialist for software work. And GPT five point six turns out to be a personality choice rather than a leaderboard rank — one tester called Fable a wise owl, and the new model a Rottweiler that grabs the problem by the throat.

The pattern the host draws out: increasingly you will run several models at once — a clever orchestrator directing cheaper implementers — matched to the shape of the job, with voice becoming part of how you steer the whole thing.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing two days earlier, reads the same parade as the hype machine at full throttle. Cheap models matching expensive ones is not, to him, a maturing market but an indictment: he notes Anthropic itself admitted that far cheaper models found the same software vulnerabilities as its flagship — proof, he says, the premium was never worth paying. And since hallucination is mathematically inevitable, no charming voice layer fixes the underlying trust problem. The place where both men agree, despite opposite instincts, is worth holding onto: most work does not need frontier-priced intelligence.

For general practice, two thoughts. First, the voice models are the most clinically interesting release in months, because live, interpreter-style translation is a real primary-care problem. In a place like Harrow, interpreted consultations through telephone services are slow and costly; if this works as advertised, a phone on the desk could do it better within a year. But note the caution — these models trade depth for fluency, and one insisted, with total confidence, that the word seventeen has two E's. The voice layer should be the interpreter and the receptionist, never the thing answering clinical questions. Second, expect scribe vendors to migrate to cheaper models like Grok to protect their margins. Reasonable enough — but a discharge summary is not "most tasks", and a model swap under a clinical tool should trigger re-validation. Worth asking who decides which tier your documentation runs on, before the swap happens rather than after.
