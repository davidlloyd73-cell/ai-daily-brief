David's AI brief for Tuesday, the twenty-eighth of July.

Two open-source stories collided this week, and between them they tell you where the argument in artificial intelligence has moved. First, NVIDIA has made a substantial investment in Safe Superintelligence, the secretive company Ilya Sutskever founded after leaving OpenAI, and has given it access to its next generation of chips. Safe Superintelligence has no product. It says only that it has reached the point where its research is worth scaling, and expects to multiply its computing power tenfold over the next year.

The second story is the one Nathaniel Whittemore builds his edition around. Washington has been edging towards banning Chinese open models, after a Chinese release called Kimi K3, at two point eight trillion parameters the largest open model ever published, beat the American front runner on a coding benchmark. In response, an open letter defending open weights was signed by companies worth, together, about eight trillion dollars. Jensen Huang of NVIDIA led it with his first ever post on X, which drew sixty-two million views. Sundar Pichai, Satya Nadella, Mark Zuckerberg, Elon Musk and Palantir all put their names to it. Sam Altman initially declined, then OpenAI signed after pushback, which left Anthropic as the only frontier laboratory standing outside. Open weights, if the phrase is new to you, simply means a model anyone can download, inspect and run on their own computers.

Whittemore's point is that Anthropic's refusal has become a mirror. To the former artificial intelligence adviser David Sacks it is proof that the company means to kneecap open source. To others it is principle: Dario Amodei has said repeatedly that frontier-grade cyber capability available for anyone to download is a serious concern. And the cynical reading, from an investor quoted on the show, is that eight trillion dollars of market value came out in support of open weights because it is good for their business.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron published the same day and never mentions the letter. What he disputes is the assumption beneath it, that these are healthy companies whose interests happen to point at a public good. He describes Safe Superintelligence as a company with no products or plans other than to rent NVIDIA's chips, and reads the whole pattern of investments and guarantees as an industry manufacturing the demand it cannot find. Both men, from opposite directions, end up saying the same thing: this is about commercial self-interest.

Three thoughts for the surgery. First, open weights are the only arrangement under which a scribe could ever run entirely inside the health service. Every ambient scribe in English general practice today sends the consultation to a supplier's cloud. Ask where the model runs, not just where the data sits. Second, a ban drafted in Washington is a supplier risk in Harrow, because several cheaper transcription tools are built on open weights of uncertain parentage. Ask your supplier, in writing, which base model they use and whether they would tell you if it changed. And third, on the safety argument itself, general practice does not need to pick a side. Whoever is right, our exposure comes from the estate we actually run. Patch it, segment it, and rehearse the paper fallback.
