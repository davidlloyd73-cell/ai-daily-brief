David's AI brief for Tuesday, the eleventh of August.

Today's one idea is that artificial intelligence has finally found a loud optimist, and that optimism cannot outrun a trust deficit.

Mark Zuckerberg published a six-and-a-half-thousand-word essay called The Future is for Everyone, arguing that the worst possible outcome for superintelligence is that its power ends up concentrated in a very few hands. He plants his flag opposite the doomers, and opposite Dario Amodei at Anthropic. He says he does not understand why anyone who believes this technology will eliminate most jobs and much of humanity's relevance would rush to build it. On work, he argues there is no rule that automation must outpace people's ability to grow; that compute is finite, so it makes more sense to point it at inventing valuable new things than at replacing existing jobs; and that companies will get smaller, which means more of them rather than fewer jobs. Alongside it came a fund of one billion dollars for the communities that host data centres, and an open-weights model small enough to run on your own hardware.

The reaction is where Nathaniel Whittemore finds the story. Bloomberg noted the irony of jobs optimism from a company that has cut eight thousand roles this year. Others seized on the modesty of the examples: superintelligence helping his daughter with baking recipes. The Verge's response was the sharpest. An assistant can pick a better recipe, but it cannot give your father your time, and it cannot knit your scarf for you, because the knitting is what soothes. Underneath all of it, one word kept surfacing. Trust. Sixty-four per cent of Americans say social media has harmed democracy, and Whittemore's read is that this industry is paying for social media's sins. Not the messenger he would have chosen, he says, but he will take anyone making the case for the good.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron published the same day, and his argument is that the distrust is not about manners at all. It is about arithmetic nobody will look at. Bank analysts put seventy per cent or more of Microsoft's, Google's and Amazon's artificial intelligence revenue at OpenAI and Anthropic's own compute spending. Set roughly one hundred and ten billion dollars of genuine annual demand against the trillions the planned capacity would require, and the manifesto starts to look less like a message and more like a prospectus. Both men are polemicists. What is striking is that both say the industry has not earned the trust it asks for.

Two thoughts for our end of the world. The first is that the trust question is the one we answer at the start of every consultation. A patient who is easy about a scribe is not judging the technology. They are judging the doctor who asked. Told plainly, in the room, with a real option to decline.

The second is the knitting. The tasks worth handing over are the ones where the doing carries no value: the typing, the coding, the chasing of letters. The ones worth guarding are those where the doing is the point. The pause after a difficult answer. The call to a bereaved relative. Medical examiner work sits almost entirely in the second category.
