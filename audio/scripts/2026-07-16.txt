David's AI brief for Thursday, the sixteenth of July.

The one idea today is about a quiet shift in the enterprise. For the last few years the only real question for a company buying artificial intelligence was simple: which frontier model do you rent — OpenAI's, or Anthropic's? This week the host argues the question has changed. The new battle is over who owns the model you customise. Two things happened together. Mira Murati's lab, Thinking Machines, released its first model, called Inkling. On the benchmarks it is unremarkable — ranked around nineteenth, and one respected tester found it distinctly rough. But that, the host says, is to miss the point. Inkling is an open-weights model, built deliberately to be the foundation you fine-tune on your own data, through the lab's Tinker platform. And in the same week, Bloomberg reported that Microsoft is now training its salesforce to tell customers not to trust the frontier labs with their data — a foot in the door for Microsoft's own models and its own paid fine-tuning service. So the choice facing a serious buyer has exploded. Not just which model to rent, but how much independence and control you want over the model itself — for cost, and for data sovereignty.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing the day before, cuts underneath all of this. To him the ownership debate is a rounding error, because the real problem is that enterprise artificial intelligence has shown no measurable return at all. He argues the whole boom rests not on profit but on vibes and, in his words, a cult-like psychosis — and that no clever architecture, open weights or otherwise, changes the economics. His blunt line: without OpenAI, he says, the industry doesn't exist. He notes that running these models has already become, in one report's words, a huge issue for enterprise customers on cost. The honest common ground is narrow but real: both men agree that the cost of running these models is a genuine, unsolved problem. The thing to watch is whether any company ever publishes hard numbers showing fine-tuning actually pays for itself — or whether the bills simply keep climbing.

For the surgery, three thoughts. First, this whole fight is really about data sovereignty — where the consultation data goes and who may train on it. That is exactly the question to put to any ambient-scribe supplier: does anything leave the practice, and is our data used to train the vendor's models? Second, be wary of the bespoke, trained-on-our-data pitch. Even the host's own guests warn that fine-tuning is far more ongoing effort than it looks; general practice rarely has the capacity to keep a custom model current, so a well-governed general model is usually the honest default. And third, the Microsoft story — salespeople coached to rubbish a rival — is a clean reminder to judge these tools on your own audit and independent testing, never on the supplier's comparison slide.
