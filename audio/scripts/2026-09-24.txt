David's AI brief for Thursday, the twenty-fourth of September.

Today's One Idea is that personal AI agents are finally leaving the screen, and the "nobody wants this" argument is meeting real evidence for the first time.

Until now it was theoretical. Silicon Valley says people want their errands automated. Sceptics like Ben Thompson say people want to shop, complain and be entertained, not be productive; he points out that his own human assistant, ten years in the job, still doesn't book his flights. But the evidence is arriving. Meta's assistant, Muse, sits at number one on the App Store. A New York Times reviewer had it fill in forms, pay a photographed parking ticket, argue with his dental insurer's phone line in different personalities, and haggle on Facebook Marketplace, turning forty-five dollars of savings into a set of ceramics bowls. His verdict was that it's the most useful AI app he's used. Meta is pushing hard on the back of that: a refreshed line of Ray-Ban glasses, including a camera-free audio pair, with hands-free control of Muse listed first among the new features, plus a new always-on device called Charm, about the size of an AirPods case, meant to ship by the holidays. Separately, Tesla has put its own assistant, GrokBot, straight into the car's existing voice system, and owners are already using it to order coffee and manage email while the car drives itself.

Interestingly, the scepticism hasn't disappeared, it's moved. The Times reviewer went in doubting whether these agents were useful enough and came out doubting whether people will be comfortable handing over that much personal data. That's the real bottleneck now, not capability.

Elsewhere: Anthropic says one thousand Claude agents spent twenty-one hours and about ten thousand dollars of compute finding a novel gene cluster that resembles CRISPR machinery. Outside scientists call it genuinely interesting and very, very preliminary; nobody yet knows what it does. And at the UN, Trump renamed AI "superintelligence" from the podium, while Sam Altman, Dario Amodei and the White House gave the Security Council three quite different pitches on how much any of this should be governed at all.

Now, for general practice. The privacy question that just ambushed Muse is the same one hanging over ambient scribes: usefulness alone doesn't settle consent, or where the recording goes, or who else's systems touch it. Worth another look at your own patient information leaflet with that in mind. The CRISPR story is a good template for how to treat any AI-flavoured medical claim that reaches a patient before proper peer review: striking is not the same as validated. And as agents move from transcribing to acting on their own initiative, the same discipline that should already sit around your scribe applies, everyone knows how to switch it off, and what happens to the consultation if it fails.
