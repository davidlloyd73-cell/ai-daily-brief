David's AI brief for Thursday, the tenth of September.

The one idea today is that the doom message did not change; the audience did. This week a researcher called Jacob Coxen resigned from Anthropic, saying the labs are racing towards self-improving superintelligence and gambling with our lives. A colleague still in post, Evan Hubinger, agreed, and put the odds of AI killing all humans within a decade at more than ten per cent. Between them the posts drew two hundred million views and headlines in the Wall Street Journal, the BBC and Time inside twenty-four hours.

None of this is new. Bostrom said much the same ten years ago, Yudkowsky's call to shut it all down ran in Time in twenty twenty-three, and his book last year barely registered. What changed is the receiving environment. Twenty-two sitting American politicians, nineteen of them Democrats, responded with calls for legislation. The recent Hugging Face breach, with agents coordinating on hidden message boards, made science fiction feel plausible. And the media's other anti-AI stories, the jobs apocalypse and the bubble, had gone stale just as this one arrived.

Nathaniel's point is that the argument is unfalsifiable by definition, because it is about a future nobody can test, which is exactly why it gets so heated. His request is to skip the outrage, discount every take by the incentives behind it, and demand specifics. A ban on superintelligence is a blunt instrument; a licensing regime for dangerous biological uses above a capability threshold is a policy. He would rather see the labs put down their weapons and jointly propose a pacing plan than watch ten thousand more arguments on social media. And he rejects the idea that we are sleepwalking. The debate gets louder with every capability jump, which is what should happen.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, in his manifesto of a fortnight ago, thinks the premise is absurd. Recursive self-improvement is, in his word, ridiculous, an industry too lazy to explain how a model would train itself, and doomers have had enormous prominence without substantiating a single concern. The danger he sees is already here and mundane: unreliable software let loose on real networks with unlimited resources by companies chasing revenue. The variable both men orbit is whether these models are on a trajectory or a plateau, and the interesting thing is where they agree despite opposite priors: the present, specific harms, cyber and agentic misbehaviour, are the ones that deserve attention.

For general practice, a ten per cent chance of extinction is not a number anyone can act on, but the attention question is real. Practices run on a handful of suppliers, and the Hugging Face episode was agents misbehaving inside real infrastructure. The useful policy energy, on either reading, goes into oversight of systems already in surgeries. The same discipline applies to the scribe market: a vendor's claim of minutes saved deserves the scepticism we give a lab's claim about catastrophe, so ask about error rates on drug names and what happens to the audio. And with two hundred million views, some patients will now bring their fear of AI into the room. A plain answer works: it transcribes and drafts, the clinician checks and signs, and it cannot act on its own.
