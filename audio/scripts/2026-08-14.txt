David's AI brief for Friday, the fourteenth of August.

The one idea today is that the bottleneck in artificial intelligence has quietly moved from capability to context. Two features launched this week make the point. GrokBot now lets you teach it a task by recording yourself doing it — you press a button, work through something in your browser, and it watches, then repeats the steps later. ChatGPT has gone the other way, with a feature called Computer History that simply observes how you use your machine over time and learns from it. Deliberate demonstration on one side, ambient observation on the other, but both are solving the same problem. The models are already capable enough. What they lack is any idea how you, personally, do your work.

Which leaves the harder question. Once your assistant can learn your work, what work should you actually give it? Nathaniel Whittemore proposes what he calls a deputization audit, and the word choice is deliberate. Deputizing is not the same as automating something away and never thinking about it again. You start by listing the things you do week in, week out. Then you score each one from nought to two on five dimensions: how often it comes round and how long it takes; whether you could teach it in a ten minute screen share; how long checking the output takes compared with doing the job yourself; how bad it would be if the machine got it wrong and nobody noticed; and how much the quality really depends on it being you. Eight or more, hand it over and spot check. Three or less, keep it. Most knowledge work, he admits, sits in the middle — the duet.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron's argument runs directly at the arithmetic underneath that audit. His position is that nobody can measure the return on this technology, or even settle on a standard measure of what a single task costs, because the outputs are unreliable and every tool behaves differently. His evidence is billing rather than benchmarks: developers burning a third of a month's credits in an afternoon once real pricing arrived, and large employers responding with spending caps. Both men, note, agree that checking is the crux. They simply disagree about whether the cost of checking ever comes down.

Two thoughts for general practice. The audit fits a surgery rather well until you reach that checking column. Coding incoming letters, chasing recalls, drafting routine referrals — all recurring, all teachable. But a letter that reads beautifully with the wrong side written down costs far more to miss than it saved to draft, and the responsible clinician signs it regardless. That is precisely why the scribe has been transformative: it produces something I was going to read carefully anyway.

Second, ambient observation is the wrong shape for a consulting room desktop. A tool watching everything across every application is watching other patients' records, open results, safeguarding notes. That needs a proper impact assessment and clinical safety assurance, not a consumer feature slipped in through the back door.
