David's AI brief for Monday, the twentieth of July.

There is no new-model drama today. Instead, the host offers a practical field guide to getting the most out of the latest models, Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol, and the one idea running through it is this: every big leap in intelligence forces you to unlearn your old habits, and then to raise your ambition.

The unlearning comes first. The way you prompted last year now often hurts you. The long rule lists people wrote for older models make the new ones worse and cost more; the guidance now is to state each instruction exactly once. Cutting repeated instructions, OpenAI reports, lifted scores by ten to fifteen per cent while cutting the token bill by up to two-thirds. Blanket rules like "keep it brief" now strip out too much, because the newer model is already terse. And vague adjectives like "friendly" no longer land. Better to spell out the behaviour you want: name the patient's problem in the first line, give the fix as numbered steps, skip the apology.

But the host's real point is bigger than prompting tricks. The genuine unlock, he argues, is higher ambition: stop using these tools to do the same work a little faster, and start handing them the hard, high-leverage work you never trusted them with before. Treat the model as a co-creator. Adopt new patterns, like loops, where an agent keeps working until it meets a target you have set, rather than stopping when it decides it's done. Assume, for a moment, that there are no limits, and push until you find where the real ones actually are.

Now, the other side. The writer Ed Zitron, a month ago, took direct aim at exactly this. He argues that loops are not a breakthrough but a way to make you burn more tokens, and so more money, dressed up as progress. Of the sudden industry chorus that the age of prompting is over, he says simply: it's unclear what benefit a loop actually has. To him, "raise your ambition, adopt new rituals" is a kind of cargo cult - an industry out of ideas, mistaking activity that looks like success for the real thing, with no evidence of return. The honest common ground is this: the new patterns do cost more, and nobody yet has clean data showing they produce more.

For the surgery, three thoughts. First, the safest lesson needs no view on the hype: set clear boundaries, like "prepare this as a draft, don't send it," because the more capable the tool, the worse the consequence if it acts unbidden. Second, if you do try these agentic patterns on practice admin, measure a baseline first - time, errors, safety - and only scale what genuinely helps, not what everyone else is adopting. And third, keep the ambition aimed at the paperwork, not the diagnosis. Finding a model's limits by pushing is fine for a referral letter; it is the wrong method for a patient.
