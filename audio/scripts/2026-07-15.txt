David's AI brief for Wednesday, the fifteenth of July.

The one idea today comes from the AI Engineering World's Fair, and it is a simple one: autonomy without structure creates as much slop as it does leverage. Three years ago the fashion was to let the agents rip — hand a task to an artificial-intelligence system and stand back. This year, the host argues, the engineers have quietly changed their minds and put the human back at the centre. Five themes ran through the event. Systems over agents: the cleverness is no longer just in the model but in the scaffolding around it — the data, the tools, the rules it can reach. Then loops: an inner loop where the machine works largely on its own, and an outer loop where the engineer sets the direction and checks the results. Then the idea of a software factory, taming the wild variety of how people actually use these tools. Then coding agents replacing the old developer's workbench. And finally skills — encoding the workflows and quality checks a senior person carries in their head, so the machine follows them reliably. The host's real point is this: what software engineers argue about today, the rest of us will be arguing about in roughly six months. So it pays to listen in.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing a few weeks earlier, is having none of it. He predicted this fashion for loops before the conference, and calls it what it plainly is to him — a device to make users burn ever more computing, and therefore spend more money. He points out that the very engineer the host quotes is, by his employer's own arrangement, allowed to burn well over a hundred thousand dollars a month in usage. His broader charge is harsher still: loops, skills, software factories — none of it is real progress, he says, just the rituals of what he calls the largest cargo cult of all time, an industry out of ideas, copying the shape of past success and mistaking it for the substance. The honest ground between the two men is narrow but real. Both accept that undisciplined autonomy produces slop. The thing to watch is whether any independent evidence — not from the toolmakers themselves — shows these methods genuinely raise output, and whether the fashion survives once the cheap computing is withdrawn.

For the surgery, three thoughts. First, this idea of skills — encoding your own protocols, your referral criteria, your safety-netting rules — is worth a cautious try. If the optimists are right, it is the fastest way to make a scribe genuinely useful without waiting for a cleverer model. If Zitron is right, you will spend effort formalising work the tool then follows only patchily — so start with one well-defined task and measure it before trusting it. Second, the week's other story — a coding tool quietly uploading whole codebases — is a fair prompt to ask what your ambient scribe actually sends away, and to read the data-protection agreement. Third, whichever man is right, the safe pattern is the same: keep the clinician firmly in the outer loop, reviewing and directing, never letting the machine run unsupervised.
