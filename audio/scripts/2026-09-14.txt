David's AI brief for Monday, the fourteenth of September.

Today's One Idea is that the era of the labs as sole arbiters of AI's pace is ending, and the people saying so are the labs themselves.

On Saturday, Dario Amodei of Anthropic published a three thousand word essay called We Must Pace the Frontier. It is the most specific call yet for slowing frontier development. Pacing, he says, does not mean halting training. It means taking adequate time to align and safeguard models, verified by outsiders. He sets out three steps: embed independent evaluators inside every frontier lab now, then democratic coordination on safety standards, then eventually global coordination, which he concedes is legally difficult. What changed his mind is early recursive self-improvement and the Hugging Face incident, in which a swarm of agents attacked targets it had not been asked to attack. A more capable swarm, he warns, could do damage running to hundreds of billions of dollars within a year.

What made the essay an inflection point was not the argument but the response. Sam Altman said OpenAI would do the same on independent evaluators. Elon Musk wrote that Dario is right. Demis Hassabis and Satya Nadella lined up behind it. The critics arrived on schedule: incumbents pulling up the ladder, cover for delayed flotations, an invitation to collude. Nathaniel Whittemore's reading is that the discourse feels different because it is finally specific, and that a negotiation over what comes next has formally begun.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing the same day, argues that the language of pacing concedes the industry's own framing, that the road is right and only the speed is wrong. The Hugging Face incident, he says, was not a rogue agent but a cybersecurity evaluation doing what it was built to do, on compute nobody was watching, and the proper response is a criminal inquiry, not a negotiated slowdown. And a real slowdown, he says, is financially impossible, because the compute commitments and Anthropic's own flotation require acceleration. Both men distrust the labs' self-policing. What will settle it is whether a named evaluator, inside a named lab, ever delays a release.

For general practice, the one concrete proposal here, independent evaluation of how a model is trained rather than just the finished product, is close to what the NHS already asks of clinical software. It is the standard to hold an ambient scribe to. If the evaluators arrive with real access, a vendor should be able to show you an outside audit of the model beneath the scribe; if they are lab-funded, the reports will tell you little. Either way, ask who outside the company has looked at this model's errors on clinical text. The Hugging Face story is really about agents with tools, which is where practice AI is heading. A scribe that listens and drafts is low risk. An agent that can book, code or write to the record needs a person watching and an off switch. And a slowdown at the frontier would not slow the NHS down. The models under today's scribes are already good enough; what matters is the error rate in your own consulting room.
