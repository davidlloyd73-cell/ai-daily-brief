David's AI brief for Sunday, the twenty-third of August.

This was a weekend long-reads episode, and the one idea is that the tired question — will AI take all our jobs — is finally being replaced by better ones. The publisher Every has launched a project called Thesis Statements: one hundred builders and thinkers writing short essays on the future they actually see coming. Nathaniel Whittemore reads through the first twenty-five, and a pattern emerges.

The first strand is that automation may create more expert human work, not less. Dan Shipper, who runs Every with a team of around thirty people, says they have not let a single person go in favour of agents. His argument is mechanical rather than sentimental. Agents take over the stable, repeatable, well-framed layer of a job. Somebody still has to point them at the right problem, judge what comes back, catch what is wrong, and turn the result into a decision that someone will act on. Models know work that has already been done. Humans are alive to this particular patient, this particular afternoon.

The second strand is about how organisations hold together. Noah Brier argues the real failure of agentic work is not buggy output but agents building the wrong thing very efficiently, so the right metaphor is a software company rather than a software factory. Tom Critchlow puts it more sharply still: agents operate in seconds, teams meet weekly, finance plans quarterly, and each is living in a different present. The company with the best clock, he says, will beat the company with the best model.

And the third strand is where human advantage moves. Toward wisdom rather than knowledge. Toward judgement, taste, and being a bit odd. One contributor puts it plainly — the last stage of work belonged to the brain, the next belongs to the heart.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron's objection is not to any single essay but to the entire genre. He argues that the whole conversation about AI and jobs is theoretical, that the labs' economic research units are marketing operations, and that Anthropic's own head of economics has conceded there is no material increase in unemployment to date. His charge is that all this talk of the future of work is a deliberate distraction from a harder question: an industry with roughly one hundred and ten billion dollars of annual revenue carrying hundreds of billions in commitments. Where Whittemore hears practitioners getting specific, Zitron hears people describing a future paid for with money that has not arrived.

For general practice, three thoughts. First, the more-expert-work argument is a fair description of what a scribe has already done to a consultation — but the time it frees is not neutral. Spend it on the uncertain patients, or you have bought efficiency and nothing else. Second, the best clock problem is just the discharge summary problem wearing new clothes; before buying a second tool, ask whether it writes into the record everyone already reads. And third, whichever man is right, bank the change in your working practice, not in the supplier. Own the notes, keep one clinician competent without the tool, and know what happens the Monday the vendor goes dark.
