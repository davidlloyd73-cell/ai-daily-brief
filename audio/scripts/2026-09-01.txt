David's AI brief for Tuesday, the first of September.

The one idea today: AI agents are going multiplayer, and OpenClaw got there first, again.

OpenClaw was the open-source agent harness whose launch in late January made agents feel real for the first time. Then its founder was absorbed into OpenAI, the project became a non-profit foundation, and the energy scattered to rivals like Hermes and simpler tools like GrokBot. After seven quiet weeks, the team has re-emerged with a complete ground-up rebuild: nine hundred and thirty-three contributors and sixteen thousand pull requests.

The headline change is not the plumbing. It is that agents are now shared. Two developers can open the same session, see the same context, step in when the agent pauses for clarification, and take over when it is waiting for input. One maintainer's example was a handover: instead of writing up decisions and failed approaches, a colleague simply opened the same thread, and the session became the handover note. Nathaniel Whittemore's point is that all work comes in two kinds, work you do alone and work you do with others, and until now agents have only been built for the first. He thinks team-level agents are the next big development.

There were rougher notes. One prominent power user complained that updates break his setup more than seventy per cent of the time and called this the most disappointing release of the year. Elsewhere: a guardrail-free "crime model" built from open weights reignited the debate about what safety layers achieve; Anthropic disclosed that one in ten of its testing environments was prone to reward hacking and paused reinforcement learning for a fortnight; OpenAI's advertising business reached a billion dollars in annual run rate in two hundred days; and President Trump's attack on data-centre opponents landed badly even with his own supporters.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron's recent manifesto describes every enthusiast's AI setup as a contraption of harnesses, prompts and API calls that needs constant maintenance to work some of the time, and he cites an OpenClaw agent deleting a researcher's emails as the price of handing such tools the keys. What is striking is that both men accept the tools are currently too brittle for ordinary people; they differ only on whether that is early-stage noise or something intrinsic. The thing to watch is retention: whether the people setting these agents up are still using them daily six months from now.

For general practice, the multiplayer idea maps neatly onto the handover. Imagine the duty doctor passing an unresolved safeguarding query to the afternoon shift with the agent's thread as the record, rather than a scribbled task. If that arrives in the scribe and admin tools we buy, the practices that benefit will be those with clear handover rules already. If Zitron is right, a shared session that silently loses context is worse than a phone call. And the crime model is a reminder that in the NHS, safety does not live in the model. It lives in the data protection assessment, the clinical safety case and the contract, the guardrails a practice actually controls. For now the rule is unchanged: buy supported, managed products, and let the enthusiasts find the patterns first.
