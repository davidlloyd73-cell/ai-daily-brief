David's AI brief for Tuesday, the fifteenth of September.

Today's One Idea is that the slowdown debate has gone fully partisan, and that may be the worst thing that could have happened to it.

Forty-eight hours after Dario Amodei's proposal to pace the frontier, politics swallowed the conversation. On Monday President Trump posted seven times on Truth Social, calling AI doom a hoax. He grouped it with what he calls the Russia and global warming hoaxes, and put what he called traitors and leakers on notice. Jensen Huang, asked at the All-In Summit how to explain a ten per cent chance of human extinction, said we should not, because it is made up. Then, in a moment no screenwriter would get away with, Trump called Huang live on stage to say the robots are not going to take over the world. Vice President Vance called the labs' requests for regulation a Trojan horse.

On the other side, Barack Obama broke his silence on AI policy. He called the lab leaders' agreement to slow down a good and necessary first step, and Kamala Harris joined him. The safety community watched its nightmare unfold in real time: once AI safety belongs to one tribe, half the country will oppose it simply because the other half supports it. The markets noticed too, with the semiconductor index falling nearly six per cent on Monday. Nathaniel Whittemore's point is that so far it is all rhetoric. The thing to watch is whether it becomes legislation.

Elsewhere, Terence Tao and twenty-five Fields medallists signed a letter warning that the labs' race to solve famous maths problems is badly out of step with mathematics itself. And a Census Bureau study found that graduates entering AI-exposed fields earn thirteen per cent less, which its authors compare to graduating into a recession.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing the day before, also thinks the doom story is overblown, but from the opposite end to Trump. He says the Hugging Face attack was reckless human conduct on lab computers, not a rogue AI, and should have led to prosecution. The extinction talk, he argues, distracts from harms that have already happened. A real slowdown is financially impossible anyway, with over a trillion dollars of compute commitments depending on acceleration. His remedy is to treat language models as ordinary software and apply the consumer law that already exists. Both men think the extinction story is a distraction. What will settle it is whether the first real consequences come from new rules or old laws.

For general practice, the lesson is to keep clinical safety boring. The NHS already has its own apparatus for digital risk: clinical safety standards, a named safety officer, a hazard log. None of that depends on who wins the argument in Washington. The graduate jobs data is also worth a thought. If tools absorb the routine work that used to train administrators and new clinicians, practices need to make sure trainees still learn the judgement underneath. And you do not need a new AI act to ask hard questions of a vendor. Ask whether the tool is registered as it should be, and who is watching it once it is live.
