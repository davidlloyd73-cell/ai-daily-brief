David's AI brief for Tuesday, the eighth of September.

The one idea today is that GPT-6 Astra, which OpenAI released over the weekend, is an opportunity model rather than an efficiency model, and that is why nobody can agree about it. It is not built to do what you already do a little better. It is built to let you do things you could not do before, and the weekend's reviewers judged a new thing by old criteria that did not fit.

The confusion is real. On the Artificial Analysis intelligence index Astra initially scored sixty-one, level with its predecessor and five points behind Fable 5.1. That index leans on fact recall, and within days its maintainers rebuilt it to weight agentic tasks. On the tests OpenAI cares about it leads clearly: forty-one per cent on a benchmark for driving a computer, against thirty-one for Fable and eighteen for the previous GPT; near-perfect scores in frontier mathematics; and a perfect score on building software exploits, which is why cybersecurity partners had it first.

The social media weekend was all three-dimensional graphics. People turned a nineteen seventy-seven text adventure into a playable 3D game, built an interactive atlas of the ankle to understand their own pain, and modelled how a weight-loss drug acts in the body. The host calls this the third capability handed to people who never had it, after image generation and coding. And the launch video, watched a hundred and thirty-two million times, showed nobody touching a keyboard: people talking to a laptop while it listed items for sale and booked tennis courts. The interaction pattern, he says, is changing from clicking and typing to telling a computer what you want.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron published the same day, and he opens with this very launch, but his target is the chorus declaring that artificial general intelligence has arrived. He argues the hype exists to stop you looking at the money: by one payments firm's data, eighty per cent of OpenAI's and Anthropic's enterprise revenue comes from one per cent of customers, mostly AI start-ups spending venture capital on tokens, and every launch produces a revenue surge because those start-ups must offer the newest model whatever it costs. The variable both men circle is whether new capability becomes paying, durable demand before the funding runs out. They agree the capability is real, the tests were wrong, and nobody yet knows what Astra is for. Watch that customer concentration figure a year from now, not the benchmarks.

For general practice, the capability that matters is the screen driving, not the games. A model that can operate clinical software for hours could chase results, file letters and reconcile medication lists after discharge. If the host is right, that arrives within a couple of years. If Zitron is right, it arrives from a supplier whose subscription does not cover its costs and disappears with the funding. Second, we judge AI scribes the way the benchmarks judged Astra, on transcription accuracy, when the real change is in the consultation. And one dull but important point for NHS information governance: a model this good at exploiting software weaknesses makes slow patching of old systems far less tolerable than it was last year.
