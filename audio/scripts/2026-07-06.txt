David's AI brief for Monday, the sixth of July.

Today's one idea is that the AI and jobs debate finally has hard data, and it points somewhere unexpected: not to mass unemployment, but to workers leaving firms to go it alone. Nathaniel Whittemore's argument is that AI is quietly making the one-person, million-dollar company a real category.

The evidence comes from two directions. The economist Lia Palagashvili, writing in the Wall Street Journal, found that solo business applications have risen nearly twenty-seven per cent since early twenty twenty-four — but only in the sectors with the highest AI adoption: professional services, information, education, finance. In construction and wholesale, where AI has barely landed, the numbers are flat. Solo self-employment in AI-exposed occupations is up about twenty per cent over three years. Her conclusion is that AI's first labour shock is not replacing workers but making traditional firms less necessary — one person can now do what once took a small team.

Then Stripe, the payments firm, confirmed it from its own books. Businesses founded in twenty twenty-five reached a million dollars in first-year revenue three times as often as the twenty nineteen cohort. The number of million-dollar solopreneurs has more than doubled since twenty twenty-three. And sixty-three per cent of new C corporations formed last quarter had a single founder — an all-time high. Stripe's explanation is that AI now fills the gaps founders once hired for: the technical co-founder, the first marketing hire, even sales, with AI-driven recommendations now delivering four times the share of new customer journeys. As the writer Derek Thompson put it, there has never been a better time to get rich working alone.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing the day after this edition, argues the ground this golden age stands on is subsidised. Subscribers, he says, are getting thirty or forty dollars of compute for every dollar they pay — and selling forty dollars for one is not a real business. On his reading, the cheap tools minting solo founders are loss-leaders from two deeply unprofitable labs, and when the subsidies stop, so does the boom. The honest signal is where these opposites agree: the era of almost-free AI is ending. Watch whether the solopreneur cohorts keep their revenue once tokens are priced at cost.

For general practice, the same forces reach medicine's small-business layer. If Whittemore is right, an AI stack for letters, coding and bookkeeping makes small independent practice viable again after years of consolidation — good news for locums and single-handers. If Zitron is right, that stack is priced below cost, so build nothing you cannot run without it. And note Tesla capping engineers at two hundred dollars a week of AI spend: usage budgets will reach NHS scribes eventually, so it is worth measuring now what the scribe actually saves per clinic, before an accountant asks.
