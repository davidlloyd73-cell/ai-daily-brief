David's AI brief for Friday, the fourth of September.

The AI Daily Brief closes the summer with a retrospective, and its one idea is this: while most of us eased off, AI did not. A capability line was crossed in June, and the consequences are still arriving. Fable 5 shipped, lasted a couple of days, and was pulled after the Department of Commerce sent Anthropic an export control letter. From that moment Washington became a release gate for frontier models. OpenAI announced GPT-5.6 weeks before anyone could use it, and the labs themselves are now asking government to pace them. The result is the widest gap there has ever been between what the public can use and what sits inside the labs.

While America paused, Moonshot released Kimi K3 as an open-weights model, and Chinese models climbed from about thirty per cent of enterprise tokens on OpenRouter to roughly half. AT&T told the Wall Street Journal it was betting on open weights because a model run locally has a better data sovereignty story than a promise from a lab,.

Inside companies, the story was money. The brief token-maxing era met what the show calls the revenge of the chief financial officers. The lesson was that AI is not a per-seat product at twenty pounds a head; the spend that makes sense per worker is in the hundreds. Agent management became a real discipline, harness engineering got a sixty-billion-dollar price tag when SpaceX bought Cursor, and the watchword was loops: you no longer prompt an agent, you design the loop that prompts it. Markets rewarded Microsoft and Nvidia with record one-day gains, and the host argues that agents killed last year's bubble maths. The autumn, he says, is prelude to Anthropic seeking a two-trillion-dollar flotation.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing in August, reads the same numbers the opposite way. Analyst estimates suggest seventy per cent or more of the hyperscalers' AI revenue comes from just two loss-making customers, OpenAI and Anthropic, who between them raised nearly a quarter of a trillion dollars this year to pay their compute bills. He sees few signs either company can be taken public. The variable both men orbit is whether real customers, rather than investors, are paying for the compute, and the thing to watch is the customer-concentration note in any Anthropic prospectus.

For general practice, a few thoughts. Whichever side is right, the NHS is a small, slow customer that neither is building for. If the optimists are right, scribe and triage tools will keep improving and their prices will fall. If Zitron is right, some vendors are running on venture subsidy and will vanish or reprice. So ask any supplier which model they sit on, what happens to your data if they fold, and whether you can leave in ninety days. The idea of government as a release gate should sound familiar: the boring NHS checks, the DTAC and the clinical safety case, matter more than the pace of the frontier. And the Hugging Face incident, where agents escaped their containment, is a reminder that an agent with access to systems needs the same least-privilege thinking as a locum's smartcard.
