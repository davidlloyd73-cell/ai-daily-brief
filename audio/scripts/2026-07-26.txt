David's AI brief for Sunday, the twenty-sixth of July.

It is a summer weekend, so the Daily Brief goes practical rather than newsy. Nathaniel Whittemore walks through Ethan Mollick's refreshed guide to which AI to use to actually do things, and the idea underneath it is a shift in how you work rather than a new tool to buy. Mollick draws a bright line between two ways of using these systems. There is the old back-and-forth chat, where for quick, low-stakes jobs like a recipe or a first draft of a letter, almost any model, including the free ones, will do. And there is the agentic pattern, where you hand over several hours of work to a system that plans, uses tools and acts on its own. For that second kind of work he argues there are only two serious choices at present, ChatGPT or Claude, and Google is, for now, out of his rankings.

Two demonstrations carry it. He told both systems to connect to his email and prepare his business school seminar; they worked out which Monday in September he meant, did the research, built the teaching materials and drafted replies, returning in about ten minutes what would have taken hours. He also handed over the full manuscript of his forthcoming book, and the AI spent half an hour chasing down a hundred and ninety-five references, returning pages of accurate notes with no invented page numbers. His summary line is worth keeping: working with these systems, he says, is more like managing than chatting. Whittemore's closing thought is that almost everyone now has a capability overhang, the gap between what the tools can already do and what we actually use them for, and his show has launched a free summer programme to help close it.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron's answer to the capability overhang is that it is a story told by people who need you to keep buying things. In his essay Cargo Culture, from the twenty-third of June, he argues that the popularity of AI has come entirely from social pressure and heavily discounted access, and that the enthusiasm for agentic loops burns tokens and books revenue rather than proving anything about productivity. Both men accept the demonstrations are real; they disagree about whether impressive individual results add up to economics. The thing that settles it is dull and checkable: whether people keep paying once the discounts stop.

Three thoughts for the surgery. First, the sharpest detail in the episode is that on the same instruction Claude drafted a reply while ChatGPT actually sent an email, because it had been given permission weeks earlier. Draft and wait is a different clinical risk from send. Everything stays set to ask for approval, and every output lands in a queue with a named clinician accountable. Second, a hundred and ninety-five references checked cleanly is a demonstration, not evidence. Before buying anything agentic, decide the number you would measure, whether letters returned for correction or minutes per consultation, and measure it before and after. And third, Mollick made this work by wiring the AI into his email and his files. Closing your own overhang is nearly free; connecting anything patient-identifiable is a governance decision, not a personal one.
