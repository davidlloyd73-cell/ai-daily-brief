David's AI brief for Sunday, the sixth of September.

The AI Daily Brief takes a Sunday to work through a list that has been doing the rounds: Alex Lieberman's thirty features of an AI-native company. The one idea is that the phrase has finally acquired substance. A year ago firms boasted about how many AI use cases they had. Now, the show says, everything is a use case, the agentic transition has actually begun, and the real question is what companies are turning into. The through-line across all thirty features is not to bolt agents onto old processes. Map how work is done, certainly, because that frees the knowledge trapped in people's heads, but then give agents the goal and the guardrails and let them find a better route than the human one.

A few features stand out. Context is treated as code, kept up to date and planned deliberately. Skills are distributed across the organisation rather than prompts hoarded by individuals. Work is done in loops, where an agent iterates against a verifiable success measure, which means everyone is now in the evaluation business. Autonomy is earned, on a ladder from observation to suggestion to acting with approval to acting alone. Governance is treated as a partner rather than a blocker, with agents inheriting the permissions of whoever asks. And there is a thirty-first feature, surfaced from the comments, which the host thinks is the most important: every AI workflow needs a named owner, a measurable goal, and someone responsible when it goes wrong.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron's Hater's Manifesto, from late August, sees a list like this as the problem rather than the answer. Endless tuning of harnesses, context files and skills is what he calls Jarvising: tinkering with your assistant until the tinkering becomes the job, producing a strong feeling of productivity and very little evidence of it. He says enterprises moved onto metered token billing this year and promptly started cutting budgets, because costs rose while nobody could point to an improvement. The variable both men circle is whether the spend survives an audit, and they agree, from opposite ends, that today's measurement is poor. The thing to watch is token budgets at renewal time.

For general practice, three thoughts. First, the thirty-first feature is already the NHS rule. A clinical safety officer under the DCB standards exists precisely to own the result, so a practice using a scribe or a triage tool should treat it as a governed service with a named owner, not a gadget. Second, throwing everything away every three months is not an option in a surgery with a data security return and a clinical system supplier. Boring stability from a vendor, with model changes notified in advance, is worth more than cleverness. Third, the earned-autonomy ladder is simply how we supervise trainees, and it maps well onto the medical examiner's office: gathering the record is an observation-rung task, while the scrutiny itself should stay several rungs higher. Where the two commentators part is whether anything ever earns the top rung.
