David's AI brief for Friday, the twenty-eighth of August.

No new frontier model this week. Instead, a dozen small shipped features that together move the line between an AI assistant and an AI operator. Claude now opens its own browser alongside your session, so if a task touches a website it can fill the form itself. ChatGPT at work has been given a computer in the cloud. Google's new transcription model does not just write down what you said, it tidies the rambling into what you meant, across eighty-five languages. And video generation crossed a strange threshold: one new model averages three and a half seconds to produce a clip, against sixty-eight seconds for its nearest rival, which means it now makes video faster than you can watch it. Whittemore's argument is that this quiet quality-of-life wave, rather than any single model release, is where artificial intelligence stops giving answers and starts opening the tab and getting the job done.

Behind that, the money. Nvidia confirmed it is buying Hugging Face, the main distribution point for open models, for twelve point nine billion dollars — around eighty times revenue. Its own quarter came in at ninety-six billion dollars, growth of a hundred and six per cent.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron published the same day and reads that quarter very differently. His case is that Nvidia's entire strategy is to create demand for compute among its own customers — which is why, he says, it builds agentic platforms, spends six billion licensing a coding company, and is discussing backing Perplexity at thirty billion. His flat claim is that Nvidia's biggest customers are, for the most part, funded by Nvidia. The tell he points to is buried in the same results Whittemore celebrates: free cash flow halved, receivables up fifty per cent, and the time customers take to pay stretching from forty-five days to sixty. The variable both men orbit is whether agentic tools generate genuine demand or manufactured demand — and the thing that settles it is what happens to usage when somebody finally passes the full compute cost through to the customer.

Three thoughts for the surgery. First, this is the direction our scribes will be sold in next: not writing the note, but coding the encounter, ordering the bloods, generating the referral. That is a different animal, and the practice carries the risk, so the rule has to be the one we use for students and locums — the machine prepares, a named clinician authorises, and the authorisation is recorded.

Second, Zitron's sharpest practical point is about price rather than intelligence. Agentic tools burn far more compute than dictation, and the current per-clinician price is almost certainly below cost while the market is being bought. Before signing anything, ask what happens at renewal, whether the notes come out in a usable form if we leave, and whether the contract survives an acquisition.

Third, more than a hundred companies, including the major cloud providers and Visa, have signed an open letter warning that artificial intelligence enabled attacks on hospitals will spread in the coming months. The defences it implies are the dull ones we already know, and they were worth doing before anyone mentioned artificial intelligence.
