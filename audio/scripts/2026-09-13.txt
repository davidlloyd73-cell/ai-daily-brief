David's AI brief for Sunday, the thirteenth of September.

Today's One Idea is that the value of the newest models lies in things you have never considered, which means what you need are thought starters, not benchmarks.

GPT-6 Astra has now been out for a week, and the verdict from people who use it hardest is odd. It is, they say, the smartest and the dumbest model they have worked with. For familiar work it often feels no better than what came before, and sometimes worse. Advanced coders are quietly moving back to the older model, Sol, because Astra's effective cost roughly doubled for results that were hard to justify. Yet the genuine excitement around Astra is about things that were never part of most people's jobs: building games, editing video, modelling in three dimensions.

Nathaniel Whittemore's reading is that this marks a turning point. He draws a line between efficiency AI, which does your existing work faster and cheaper, and opportunity AI, which opens up work you never did at all. Until now that was a distinction of mindset. Astra, he argues, is the first model where the difference is real. Efficiency gains will become table stakes; the winners will chase new opportunities. The problem is the blank page. Nobody carries a complete inventory of things they might make, so he offers a dozen starters: marketing people can play, video pipelines you build yourself, product demos organised by the buyer's curiosity, proposals a client can reshape, simulators for business decisions, expertise packaged as a small product. The thread running through them is the what-if machine, reasoning you can pick up and manipulate.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, in his AI Hater's Manifesto from late August, reads the same instruction as the tell. Models have got better, he says, in ways that are increasingly hard to define and that make no economic sense for the person using them, and constant model churn makes it impossible to know whether the model improved or you simply got better at building scaffolding around it. The call to experiment he calls tinkering that feels like productivity and produces none. Both men accept that Astra makes ordinary work no better and that coders have retreated on cost. What will settle it is retention: how many of this month's weekend experiments are still running, and being paid for, a year from now.

For general practice, ambient scribes are efficiency AI, and the frontier no longer moves them much. When a vendor announces an upgrade to a newer model, judge it on the error rate in your own surgery, not on which model sits underneath. The one thought starter that fits a practice is the decision simulator. Whether we need another salaried session hides several disagreements about demand, triage capacity and how work is shared; a small model that lets partners change the assumptions and see the consequences is cheap to build now and touches no patient data. The test is whether a partners' meeting ever changes a decision because of it. And simulated consultations for training are an old idea in new clothes; ask who validated the scenarios.
