David's AI brief for Wednesday, the second of September.

The one idea today: stop asking whether to switch models, and ask instead where the new one fits.

Anthropic has released Fable five point one, alongside a more powerful sibling called Mythos five point one. On almost every benchmark it is the new state of the art. On a demanding coding test it scored fifty-six, against forty-two for its predecessor and thirty-seven for OpenAI's best. On a science benchmark it nearly doubled the previous record. Independent testers say it is faster, uses about half the tokens of its predecessor on agent tasks, and, in one reviewer's words, speaks more like a normal person.

But the interesting part is what Anthropic chose to lead with. Not capability, but cost, privacy and safeguards. The company claims savings of a quarter on typical work and up to forty-five per cent on long agent tasks, thanks to cheaper cached reads. It is offering zero data retention to eligible customers, which removes the thirty-day retention policy that had kept many organisations away. And it says the model refuses eighty-five per cent fewer basic biology and medicine questions than before.

Then reality arrived. An independent lab, Artificial Analysis, measured the real cost per task at three dollars seventy-six, up from three dollars fourteen, because the new model consumed seventy per cent more tokens. Users report burning through their most expensive subscription plans in an hour. Part of that turned out to be a configuration quirk: the model was spinning up copies of itself as sub-agents, ignoring instructions to use cheaper ones. Still, Nathaniel Whittemore's conclusion is that the old question, is this good enough to switch to, is obsolete. The best users now keep a standing set of their own test tasks and match each model to the jobs where it is measurably better.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron would say this launch proves his case, not Anthropic's. In his recent manifesto he argues that everyone talks about price as if price were the problem, when the real problem is the number of tokens burned. The cost of intelligence falls on paper while the cost of actually getting something done rises, and subscriptions are only affordable because they are subsidised. Both men, notably, expect flat-rate subscriptions to give way to metered billing. The thing to watch is the next independent cost-per-task figure.

For general practice, three thoughts. First, model choice is a procurement question, not a loyalty question. Keep a small slate of your own tests, a discharge summary to reconcile or a dictation to tidy, and run each new model against it. That tells you when a better model is quietly costing more per finished job, which is what a scribe supplier will eventually pass on. Second, zero data retention is the line that matters for the NHS. It is exactly what a data protection assessment or Caldicott review asks about. But pin it down in a contract, not a launch announcement, because terms can change. Third, watch the token bill rather than the licence fee. A per-seat price looks fixed, but the cost underneath is metered, and both commentators agree that is where pricing is heading.
