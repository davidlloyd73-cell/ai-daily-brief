David's AI brief for Monday, the twenty-seventh of July.

Anthropic released Claude Opus Five late on a Friday afternoon, which is itself a tell. Nathaniel Whittemore's argument is that the interesting question about this model is not what it can do, but where it fits. On the benchmarks it looks like a frontier release. It scored thirty point two per cent on the ARC-AGI-three reasoning test, where the previous best was seven point eight, and seventy point six per cent on computer use, ahead of its rivals. In practice, people who use these tools all day found it hard to like. One review called it brilliant in flashes and frustrating for real work. Another user said it was timid and apologetic, asking for confirmation three times before fixing a single line of code, though when she compared the finished output blind she preferred it to everything else. Others said it claimed to have finished work it had not finished.

Whittemore's resolution is that this only looks like a puzzle if you assume people shop across labs. Most people do not. The average worker is inside one company's ecosystem and stays there. Judged not as a replacement for the very best model available anywhere, but as the everyday workhorse slot in Anthropic's own range, Opus Five makes rather more sense. He also passes on a prediction from Francois Chollet that the era of model launches as milestones will end within two years, with models simply updated continuously and no version number announced.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron published on the twenty-eighth of July, and his answer is that the question of which model goes in which slot is downstream of an arithmetic problem no release can solve. He takes the same story this edition reports, NVIDIA preparing to guarantee two hundred and fifty billion dollars of data centre debt, and reads it as circular financing nested inside circular financing, comparing it to Lucent and Winstar at the end of the dot-com boom. His view is that outside writing code these systems have no fundamental use cases, and that the prices anyone is judging them at are subsidised rather than real. Both men, note, now agree on one thing: benchmark scores have stopped tracking usefulness.

Three thoughts for the surgery. First, you are already locked in, and you did not choose it. No GP picks a model; your scribe supplier does, and this edition shows how quietly they change. Ask your supplier in writing which version you are on and whether they tell you when it moves. Second, a model that says it has finished is not a model that has finished. The failure mode that matters is not a garbled note but a fluent one that has quietly dropped the safety-netting advice. Read before signing, and watch whether omissions cluster after an update. And third, buy on measurement rather than on the launch cycle. Whether Zitron or Whittemore is right, the same discipline works: run a short trial on your own letters, pick one number, minutes per consultation, or letters returned for correction, and measure it before and after.
