David's AI brief for Friday, the twenty-fourth of July.

Today's one idea comes from Anthropic's head of economics, Peter McCrory, who set out to answer a question a lot of people have been asking: if artificial intelligence is as capable as we keep being told, why hasn't it pushed up unemployment? His answer is that, so far, it simply hasn't — the American labour market is stable and close to full employment, with joblessness at about four point two per cent in June, and even in the roles most exposed to AI there is no unexpected rise in unemployment.

His explanation is that, at least for now, AI is behaving as what economists call a skill-biased, labour-augmenting technology. In plain terms, it complements expertise rather than replacing it. It still relies on a human to steer a frontier that remains jagged and unreliable, and it tends to expand what a skilled person can do rather than shrink the number of people needed. He points out that jobs are not fixed bundles of tasks: even when individual tasks get automated, roles get re-bundled around them, and the people who bring the most domain knowledge get the most out of the tools. After seven months watching how people use agentic coding, he found the return to human expertise went up, not down.

He is careful to add caveats. Hiring for younger workers in exposed roles does look weaker — though he blames the wider low-hire, low-fire market as much as AI — and he concedes this could all change as agents get more capable, or if AI starts to automate innovation itself. But for now he does not expect unemployment to be noticeably higher a year from now because of AI.

Now, the other side. The writer Ed Zitron would dispute the very foundation of this. In a recent piece he argues the AI build-out is producing no genuine return on investment — no revenue, no profitability, no measurable productivity gains — and calls it the greatest capital misallocation in history, held up only because OpenAI has not yet collapsed. On his reading, the productivity figures McCrory leans on are noise dressed up as signal. Where they quietly agree is telling: both accept that mass unemployment has not arrived. The honest thing to watch is the next few quarters of productivity data, and whether OpenAI's enormous committed spending stays funded.

A thought for general practice. The idea that these tools augment rather than replace maps rather well onto what an AI scribe actually does in the consulting room: it drafts the letter, and the clinician still steers, corrects and signs. That is a good posture to keep, whichever way the economics fall. The one thing worth guarding is the training ladder — if AI absorbs the routine work that juniors once learned on, someone has to make sure trainees and new doctors still get the reps that build judgement. And whatever you believe about the productivity numbers, avoid long or costly lock-ins built on today's subsidised pricing lasting forever.
