David's AI brief for Tuesday, the twenty-first of July.

Today's one idea sounds like abstract geopolitics, but it isn't: the fight now under way in Washington over open-weight models will decide which artificial intelligence you are actually allowed to use, at what price, and how you build with it.

It began with a single tweet. Dean Ball, who heads strategic futures at OpenAI, wrote that the administration wouldn't need to ban cheap Chinese open-weight models outright - it could simply direct every agency to issue soft rules that manufacture enough fear, uncertainty and doubt to make regulated businesses back away. Eleven million people saw it, and a firestorm followed. Ball went further, calling open-weight models inherently decelerationist and warning that a world dominated by them leads to what he called full A.I. communism. Critics heard an incumbent asking the government to strangle its cheaper competition, and drew a straight line back to Steve Ballmer calling Linux a cancer twenty-five years ago.

The host's real point is that this is no longer theoretical. Reports suggest the White House is weighing a clearinghouse that would decide which companies get frontier models, a self-governing body modelled on the financial regulator, even adding Chinese firms to a trade blacklist. Even David Sacks, no dove, called weaponising regulatory uncertainty as a competitive tool completely unacceptable. Meanwhile China is positioning itself as the champion of open A.I., and Moonshot's new Kimi K3 was in such demand that it ran their servers out of computing power within forty-eight hours - a reminder that free model weights still don't escape the physics of power, chips and data centres.

Now, the other side. The writer Ed Zitron would say the reason OpenAI wants regulators to kneecap cheap open-weight rivals is simpler, and less noble, than national security: its premium business is a bubble that commoditisation would expose. He calls OpenAI one of the largest liabilities in recent economic history, notes it plans to burn over eight hundred billion dollars by the end of the decade with no real return to show, and argues that without OpenAI the justification for the industry's trillions in spending simply evaporates. On his reading, the talk of decelerationism and communism is an emperor with no clothes reaching for the regulator.

For the surgery, three thoughts. First, which model ends up inside your scribe or your letter-drafting tool won't be your choice - it will be shaped by these procurement and policy fights, so it is worth asking any supplier which model sits underneath and where the patient data goes. Second, open-weight models can be run on the health service's own infrastructure, keeping data in the country - a genuine advantage for information governance, whoever wins the argument about hype. And third, none of this changes today's consultation: keep checking every A.I.-drafted letter before it leaves, and treat vendor and model churn as a procurement risk to plan for, not a reason to change how you practise.
