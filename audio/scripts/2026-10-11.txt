David's AI brief for Sunday, the eleventh of October.

Today's One Idea is that the big arguments about artificial intelligence have grown up. Last year the debates were small, such as whether coding with AI was overhyped. This year they are structural, and Nathaniel Whittemore names five: whether the money works, whether this is a mass market or a power user market, whether businesses want sovereign AI or simply cheap AI, how it should be regulated, and whether data centres can win over their neighbours.

On the money, the bubble question has become a question about demand. Combined spending plans for the big cloud and AI firms this year are about eight hundred and twenty-five billion dollars, heading towards a trillion next year. Bain estimates a revenue shortfall of around eight hundred billion dollars by twenty thirty-one. Ramp found that eighty per cent of enterprise revenue at OpenAI and Anthropic comes from one per cent of customers. That can be read as dangerous concentration, or as a very large untapped market among everyone else. ChatGPT has about one point two billion weekly users, yet only two per cent of American households were paying in April. Cost is also overtaking raw capability: spending limits are replacing leaderboards, and cheap American models have narrowed the price gap with Chinese open models.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing on the sixth of October in a piece called Credit Crunch, argues the money does not close. He says the big cloud firms are well short of the revenue needed to justify their spending, and that OpenAI and Anthropic carry roughly one point three trillion dollars of compute commitments against heavy losses. In his words, you cannot pay those commitments with hope.

Both men are watching the same thing: whether revenue grows fast enough to service the capital. They agree on the facts, that spending is huge and revenue is concentrated. They differ on whether the ninety-nine per cent will arrive. Both are polemicists with incentives, one bullish, one certain of a bubble, and the signal is that each treats concentration as the live risk. Watch how Anthropic's flotation is received.

For the surgery, three thoughts. First, when choosing tools such as scribes, ask what they cost per clinician per month, and what happens if the price changes. Zitron's view suggests suppliers under financial strain may raise prices, so insist on sensible exit terms. Second, the sovereignty debate is really about data protection. Know where patient data goes, who processes it, and whether the supplier can swap the underlying model without telling you. Keep the data protection impact assessment current. Third, adoption is uneven. Expect a few enthusiasts to carry most scribe use, and measure whether notes are properly reviewed and signed rather than how many people have logged in. Little else in this edition touches the NHS directly.
