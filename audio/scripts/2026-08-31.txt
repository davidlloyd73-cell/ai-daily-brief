David's AI brief for Monday, the thirty-first of August.

The one idea today: the lab wars have come for your harness. On Friday night, OpenAI announced it is pulling its models from Cursor, the popular coding tool, now that Cursor is part of Elon Musk's SpaceX. The cutoff lands on the twelfth of November — the maximum notice the contract allows. On its own it looks like corporate pettiness, and plenty of developers said so; Epic's Tim Sweeney complained that nobody wants companies waging corporate warfare inside our computers. But line it up with the pattern — Anthropic cutting off Windsurf with under five days' notice last year, the blocks on xAI, subscription rules rewritten to exclude third-party tools — and it stops being an incident and becomes the operating norm. The enterprise lesson, in Nathaniel Whittemore's telling, is that the sovereignty conversation companies have been having about models now extends to harnesses too — the software wrapped around the models. If you don't want to be subject to the whims of the companies that control either layer, you cannot rely on any one company for either. Moody's calls building your own harness the new resilience discipline, and the prediction is that the open-models debate becomes an open-harnesses debate.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing a fortnight ago, would say the harness wars are a sideshow. His arithmetic has OpenAI losing nearly twenty-one billion dollars last year on thirteen billion of revenue, with more than eight hundred billion in compute obligations due by twenty-thirty — sums he argues nobody can keep supplying. On his view, the real dependency problem is not enterprises relying on OpenAI, but the entire AI economy relying on OpenAI's spending. What's striking, though, is where the two men converge despite opposite priors: neither thinks you should depend on any one lab. That agreement is the signal.

For general practice, three thoughts. First, the scribe you dictate into is a harness, and the model underneath is a vendor relationship you don't control. The question to ask any ambient scribe supplier at renewal is whether they can swap the underlying model without disrupting service — and what the fallback is if their model provider changes terms. Second, the NHS has seen this film before: dependence on a single supplier looks efficient until the supplier's incentives change. Model-layer and harness-layer dependency deserve separate lines on the risk register. Third, cheaper tokens cut both ways. An eighty per cent price cut drove a nearly fourteen-fold jump in usage, and a third of users stayed on at full price — good news for practice-level automation if prices hold, but if Zitron is right that today's prices are subsidised by unsustainable losses, any business case built on current costs should be stress-tested against a doubling.
