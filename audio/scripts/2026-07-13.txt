David's AI brief for Monday, the thirteenth of July.

The one idea today: the AI wars have turned personal — and, for now at least, the chaos is working in your favour. The race is no longer just about who has the cleverest frontier model. It has spread to hardware, chips, learning loops and geopolitics, and nobody — not the labs, not the investors — has a confident handle on where the value will finally settle. That anxiety is spilling into public: Apple has filed what the Wall Street Journal calls a thermonuclear lawsuit against OpenAI, alleging stolen hardware secrets and pointing to more than four hundred former Apple staff who have crossed over. Elon Musk and Sam Altman are trading barbs again. But the same anxiety is also driving a subsidy price war. When the new GPT model launched, Anthropic didn't poach customers — it extended free trials and raised usage limits instead. Analysts at Semi Analysis reckon a twenty-pound-a-month-style subscription still buys several hundred pounds' worth of computing, and the top tier can burn as much as fourteen thousand dollars' worth. The host's advice: take advantage while it lasts, because the frontier will not be subsidised forever.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing a few days earlier, says that subsidy is the whole con. Demand that only exists because someone sells you forty dollars of computing for one dollar is not demand at all, he argues — it is venture capital passing through the labs on its way to the chipmakers. And when the discount ends and the bubble pops, his prescription is blunt: no bailouts, no handouts, let it burn. The honest ground between them is narrow but real: both men accept the same numbers, and neither claims today's prices are sustainable as charged. The thing to watch is the first big lab to raise prices or tighten limits — and whether its users stay.

For the surgery, three thoughts. First, the AI scribes and admin tools now arriving in general practice sit on top of that subsidised computing. If costs genuinely keep falling, today's licence fees will hold. If Zitron is right, expect price rises or quietly weaker models — so avoid long lock-ins, and insist on price-review and exit clauses. Second, Satya Nadella's line this week — that you pay for AI twice, once with money and again with your knowledge — applies to every correction you make to a scribe's draft note. It is worth asking whether your supplier uses those corrections to train its models, and on what terms. Third, whichever man is right, the supplier landscape will stay turbulent. A practice that depends on an AI tool for clinical documentation needs a tested answer to the old question: what do we do the week the service goes off?
