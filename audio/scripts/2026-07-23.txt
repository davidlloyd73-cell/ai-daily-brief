David's AI brief for Thursday, the twenty-third of July.

Today there is really one idea, worked through at length: the latest market panic about artificial intelligence, and what all these panics have in common. Since ChatGPT arrived, investors have cycled through the same handful of worries - that Chinese models are suddenly too cheap, that the financing is dangerously circular, that revenue does not justify the spending, that the spending itself is running too hot, and, back in twenty twenty-four, that the models had hit a wall and would stop improving. This week's version is the cheap-Chinese-model fright, set off by Kimi K three. Nathaniel Whittemore walks through each freakout in turn and lands on a deliberately counter-intuitive conclusion: the market's relentless determination to call everything a bubble is itself the pressure-release valve that stops a real, nineteen-ninety-nine-style bubble from ever taking hold.

His supporting points are worth hearing. The scale is genuinely enormous - by one Bloomberg figure, AI investment now accounts for a quarter of all United States economic growth, and something like half of the S and P five hundred is now AI or AI-exposed, so even a quiet pension saver is carrying a bet on this. On the fears themselves, he is reassuring: the circular financing, he argues, is not the dot-com trap people remember, because OpenAI pays cash for its chips rather than borrowing against them. Kimi K three is cheaper, yes, but served at about a third of the price of the leading model, not pennies. And half of this year's announced data centres were cancelled or delayed - which he reads not as collapsing demand, but simply as building being hard.

Now, the other side. The writer Ed Zitron, in a piece published just the day before, argues almost exactly the opposite. He says the data-centre build-out is a genuine debt-funded bubble, whose special-purpose financing vehicles are the modern equivalent of the toxic mortgage bundles of two thousand and eight - off-balance-sheet structures, faintly reminiscent of Enron, hiding well over a trillion dollars of obligations. And he claims that seventy to ninety per cent of all the demand comes from just two loss-making companies, OpenAI and Anthropic, with something like fifteen times more capacity being built than real demand can fill. On his reading, the freakouts are not preventing a bubble - they are belatedly noticing one.

For the surgery, three practical thoughts. First, whoever is right, do not bet the practice on the cheapest tool: choose an AI scribe from a supplier likely to still be solvent in three years, and make sure your notes and letters can be exported if it vanishes. Second, most clinical AI tools are thin wrappers over one of these same few models, so it is fair to ask any vendor which model sits underneath, and what happens to your service if that provider stumbles. And third, be honest that this is a money story, not a safety story - keep a clinician in the loop, and a commercial wobble upstream stays an inconvenience rather than a clinical risk.
