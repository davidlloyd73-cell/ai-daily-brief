David's AI brief for Wednesday, the ninth of September.

The one idea today is that model month is not about crowning a new king. It is raw material for your own stack. In nine days we have had Fable 5.1, GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 3.8 Flash, Meta's MuSpark 1.3, a new ChatGPT image model and Meta's personal agent, each with a different mix of capability, speed and cost. The question is no longer which model is best overall but which combination suits your actual work.

The details support that. Gemini 3.8 Flash is the cheapest model measured at its level of intelligence and the fastest, but its real cost per task rose forty per cent because it now spends more tokens and takes more steps. Meta's MuSpark is pitched as frontier performance almost too cheap to meter, at about a quarter of the cost of Opus 5, and Meta's own Alexandr Wang concedes it is not as strong as Astra or Fable. Analysts say both are the most obviously benchmark-gamed models yet. The most useful comparison in the edition is a head-to-head where the top model won on quality but took twenty-four minutes, and Flash was nearly as good in thirty-seven seconds.

The headline, though, was a trust fight. OpenAI announced a solution to the Navier-Stokes equations, one of the seven Millennium Prize problems, found by an internal model more capable than GPT-6. A New York University mathematician says he had spent a year attacking the same problem inside OpenAI's coding tool and was told of the solution days after asking about rumours. OpenAI says no human or agent looked at his data, but cannot rule out that de-identified usage helped improve its models. The real unanswered question, one former DeepMind researcher says, is whether the labs can see your work and beat you to it.

Now, the other side. Ed Zitron, writing the day before, sees the same cavalcade of launches as the machinery of a bubble. In his account the biggest buyers of tokens are AI start-ups that resell them at a loss on borrowed money, so every launch spikes lab revenue without any customer paying from cash flow. He points to the very events in this edition, OpenAI cutting off Cursor and Cognition doubling its valuation in three months, as evidence that a few hundred subsidised customers hold up the whole structure ahead of two hundred billion dollars of compute bills due next year. The variable both men circle is whether model abundance is a healthy market or a subsidised one. They agree cost per task now matters as much as capability, and they agree on the trust problem: who trains on your work.

For general practice, the Navier-Stokes row is the confidentiality question we should already have asked of every AI scribe. Swap trade secret for consultation transcript. Whichever man is right, a surgery should only use tools with a written data-processing agreement, no training on its data, and a definition of what de-identified means. Second, most surgery tasks, a discharge summary, a referral letter, a coded consultation, are thirty-seven-second jobs, so price tools by cost per task rather than by leaderboard. And third, Meta's personal agent is aimed at ordinary people, which means within a year some patients will have software reading the surgery's letters and booking its appointments for them. Nobody has yet checked what such an agent does with a medical letter.
